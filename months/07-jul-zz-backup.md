# 🌧️ July — Backup Days & Extra Ideas

These five pages are for the mornings when the day on the printed page has
already fallen over. Two of them are full days for weather that keeps everybody
in, which in July means thunder all morning or an afternoon too hot to stand
in. Two are for the days somebody is sick, and those ask less of you than an
ordinary page does. The last one is a list to open at eight in the
morning when you have ten minutes and no plan.

---

<a id="jul-weather-1"></a>

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 1 — Lightning Keeps Everybody In

A July storm parks over the house for hours, takes the light out of the rooms
and makes going out a bad idea. Nobody is leaving today, and the plan runs on
that. Nothing on this page sends anybody to a store.

### 🌙 Prep Tonight

> Find the whistle and the flashlight and stand them on the kitchen counter, and leave the biggest empty box you own flat behind the sofa.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule

- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: What the Whistle Will Mean
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: The Hour With No Words (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Watching It Come Down (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: The Boat That Fits the Whole Toy Box (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🤸 Indoor Energy Burn: Cushions From One End to the Other (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: The Forks
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: Inside the Boat
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: Pajamas Early, Three Books
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: What the Whistle Will Mean

**🧰 You need:** 1 whistle, or a saucepan lid and a wooden spoon · 1 sheet of paper · 1 marker · 1 roll masking tape.

1. Blow it once at the table, then twice, and ask her which one sounds like *come here*.
2. Settle two meanings and no more. She picks which is which, and you draw both on the paper: one blast, two blasts.
3. Tape the paper to the fridge at her height and run three practice rounds before the bowls are cleared. She signals, you obey.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Hour With No Words

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 whistle (a saucepan lid and a wooden spoon does the same job)
- The signal sheet from this morning, taped to the fridge
- 1 kitchen timer, or the timer on your phone
- 1 marker

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Say the rule out loud one last time, because it is the last thing anybody says: from the timer starting, nobody in the house speaks.
2. Set it for ten minutes to begin with. Ten is a long stretch with no talking and she will break it inside four, so restart the timer instead of calling it a failure.
3. Run a real job inside the silence. Get the snack out, lay four places, carry the recycling to the door. She reads where you are looking and what your hand is doing, and the whistle covers the rest.
4. Swap over. She takes the whistle and hands out the jobs, and you do what her signals mean rather than what you can see she wants.
5. Halfway through, let her invent a third signal and give it a meaning. Add it to the sheet on the fridge in marker while she watches, then use it wrong on purpose twice and let her correct you both times.
6. Finish with one round where the winner is whoever hears something nobody else did: the gutter, the fridge, a car going through the water outside, thunder a long way off.

> 💡 **Tip:** Blow it soft. A whistle at full strength in a closed house ends the game inside a minute and wakes whoever is asleep upstairs.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Watching It Come Down

Pull two chairs up to the window that faces the weather and stand them a
stride back from the glass. Say the one rule before you sit down: nobody
touches the window or the frame while the thunder is overhead. Put her hand
flat on the wall beside the window instead, and let her feel the big ones come
through the house. Watch where the water goes: down the gutter, along the
curb, into the drain, standing in the low corner of the street. Pick one
puddle and one leaf, then come back at lunch to see what both of them did.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 13 months and turns his whole body toward a sound he cannot place, which is most of what a whistle is for at his age. He cannot blow one, and it is the exact width of his throat, so it hangs around your neck or it goes on a high shelf. Give him the wooden spoon and an upturned plastic bowl for his own signal. He walks and carries at the same time now, so the recycling job in the silent hour is his: one item, from your hand to the box by the door.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Boat That Fits the Whole Toy Box

The street outside is under an inch of water. Everything in this house that
matters to her is getting on board, and she decides who that is.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 large cardboard box, big enough for her to sit in
- 1 craft knife or heavy scissors (**adult only**)
- 1 roll masking tape and 1 roll packing tape
- 1 permanent marker
- 1 flattened box offcut for the ramp
- 1 blue sheet, blue trash bags, or any blanket that will do as water
- Paper and 1 pen for the passenger list
- Every soft toy and plastic animal in the house

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Tape every seam of the box shut and stand it up, then let her mark the doorway with tape where she wants it. Cut on her marks with her standing back, and say why she is standing back.
2. Tape the offcut on as a ramp from the floor to the doorway. It has to hold a teddy and it will not hold her, so say that once and mean it.
3. Write the boat's name on the side in marker. She chooses it and she gets the whole side to spell it across.
4. She dictates the passenger list and you write it down, one name per line, including the ones that are not animals. Kreston goes on it.
5. Spread the sheet around the boat as water. Nothing walks across it, so everything that boards comes up the ramp, and Kreston is the one who ferries them over.
6. Roll call. You read the list, she answers for each passenger and marks a check beside the name once it is aboard. She will find two missing and send you for them.
7. Sit in it with her while the rain keeps going, and let the water rise every time the thunder rolls.

> 💡 **Tip:** Cut the doorway smaller than looks right. A box with three walls still standing is a boat until Thursday, and a box cut into flaps is a pile of cardboard by four o'clock.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- One box, one blanket, no list. Get in and stay there.
- Draw the boat on a flattened box instead and leave the toys out of it.
- Line every animal up along the hall in size order and count them.
- Build a raft for two teddies out of the recycling and float it in the sink.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** Carrying a thing to a place is the job of his month, and the ramp gives him a destination he can see. Hand him one animal at a time and let him work out the walk. He will drop half of them on the way and go back for them without being asked. Keep the tape and the marker up on the table between steps, and let him have a small box of his own so the boat is not the only thing on the floor.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **A game with a whistle and rules teaches listening better than being told to listen.**
> Telling her to listen is a sentence she has heard four hundred times, and it
> names no action she can take. A whistle with two agreed meanings names one.
> She has to hold both rules in her head, wait for a sound that arrives with no
> warning, and act on it in the middle of doing something else, which is the
> whole of what adults mean by the word. Rules she helped set are the ones she
> defends. Expect her to police your obedience harder than her own, and expect
> the signals to outlast the day. One blast at the back door still works in
> September.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Nobody goes out while the thunder is going, and that includes
> the yard, the porch and the doorstep. Wait thirty minutes after the last
> rumble before anyone opens the back door. Both chairs at the window stay a
> stride back from the glass, and nobody puts a hand on the window or the frame
> while the storm is overhead. Baths and showers wait too, because
> plumbing runs the length of the house, so tonight's bath comes after the storm
> has moved off or gets skipped for a washcloth. Keep both children off the
> corded phone and away from the open back door. The whistle is the size Kreston
> chokes on and it never goes on the floor. The craft knife is adult-only, cuts
> the boat once and goes straight back out of reach. If the power drops, use
> flashlights and no candles, because a walking 13-month-old and an open flame
> at his eye level do not share a room.

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 2 — The Afternoon That Belongs to the Shade

The other kind of July weather. The sun is on the front of the house by nine,
the sidewalk is too hot for bare feet, and taking two children out into it
between ten and four buys you nothing. Drop the blinds on the sunny side and
run the whole day out of the coolest room you have. The kitchen already holds
everything this one needs.

### 🌙 Prep Tonight

> Open every window once the sun is off the house and leave them open overnight, drop the blinds on the sunny side before you go up, and stand the masking tape and a marker by the sink.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule

- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: Lining Up Everything That Holds Cold Water
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: The Pitcher That Has to Be Empty by Dinner (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Wrists Under the Cold Tap (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: The Coolest Clothes in the House (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🧊 Cool-Down Hour: The Frozen Bottles in a Dish Towel (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: Refilling the Pitcher for Dinner
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: The Clothes Pile and the Mirror
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: Wet Hair, Cool Sheets, Two Books
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Lining Up Everything That Holds Cold Water

**🧰 You need:** every pitcher, bottle, jar and lidded cup in the kitchen · her two hands.

1. Open the cabinet doors and let her pull out everything that holds water. She lines them up along the counter, biggest to smallest, and she gets to argue the order with you.
2. She picks the one that holds the most, and that one is the pitcher for the day. Two of the small bottles go in the freezer for two thirty, each one wrapped in a dish towel when it comes out, and the rest go back in the cabinet.
3. Give her the last job: work out which room is going to be coolest at two o'clock. That room is where the afternoon happens.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Pitcher That Has to Be Empty by Dinner

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 large pitcher
- 1 roll masking tape and 1 permanent marker
- 3 clear glasses
- 1 lemon, or any fruit in the bowl, or a spoonful of jam out of the pantry
- 1 cup per person, and a lidded cup for Kreston
- 1 tray
- The kitchen clock

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Three glasses on the table: water from the tap, water from the fridge, water with the fruit squashed into it or the jam stirred through. She tastes all three and picks what goes in the pitcher today.
2. Run a strip of masking tape from the top of the pitcher to the bottom. She draws a line across it for every hour between now and dinner, and the lines come out crooked and uneven, which changes nothing.
3. Fill it to the top line, the fruit or the jam in, and stand it on the tray in the room she picked at eight, with everybody's cup around it.
4. First round is hers. She pours for every person in the house, carries the cups, and then reads which line the water has dropped to.
5. From there, every time the clock hand comes around she checks the level against the lines and says out loud whether the house is behind. Say nothing about drinking yourself. The pitcher is doing the asking.
6. Empty by six is the target. Refill and start again if she gets there early, and put a mark on the tape for how far past the last line the house got.

> 💡 **Tip:** Hand her the pouring and let the tray take the spills. A pitcher she is allowed to lift is one she comes back to twelve times.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Wrists Under the Cold Tap

Stand her on a chair at the kitchen sink and run the cold tap over the inside
of both wrists for a slow count of twenty. She will tell you where she feels it
first. Do the backs of her knees and her neck with a cold washcloth, then do
her feet in a bowl on a towel while she stays on the chair. Let her do yours
after, and let her get you wetter than is necessary. Fill the cups from the
pitcher before anybody dries their hands.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 13 months and heats up faster than either of you, with less to say about it. He gets his lidded cup topped up every half hour whether he asks or not, and the back of your hand on his neck is how you check him: hot and dry means he comes to the coolest room now. Sit him on the towel with his own washcloth and a shallow bowl with an inch in it, within arm's reach of you the whole time. He will chew the cloth, which is fine, and carry it around the kitchen, which is what he does with everything this month.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Coolest Clothes in the House

An hour of rummaging on the coolest floor in the house, and it ends with her
wearing the answer.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The clothes drawers and the closet, hers and yours
- 1 old sheet, a scarf and a dish towel
- 1 mirror, or the biggest one you can carry into the room
- 1 laundry basket
- Sun hats, both of them
- Your phone, for the photographs

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Tip a basket of clothes onto the floor of the coolest room, hers on top and a few of yours underneath. Sit down in the middle of it with her.
2. Two rules and no more: nothing that needs ironing, and everything goes back in the basket at the end.
3. She feels her way through the pile and makes two heaps, hot clothes and cool clothes. Jeans, fleece and anything tight land in one, cotton and loose things in the other. Take her sorting as it comes, and put your hand on the fabrics she asks about.
4. She builds an outfit for every person in the house out of the cool heap, yours included, and everybody puts theirs on. The sheet is a robe, the scarf is a headwrap, the dish towel goes over the back of a neck.
5. Stand the mirror against the wall and let everybody look before the camera comes out. One photograph each in the doorway, and let her direct where people stand.
6. Last job is tomorrow's. She picks what she will wear in the heat in the morning and lays it flat on the end of her bed with the hat on top of it.
7. Everything else back in the basket, carried between the two of you, and the basket stays in that room with the mirror for four o'clock.

> 💡 **Tip:** Wear what she picks for you. An outfit she chose that gets refused stops the game, and the whole hour runs on her being the one who decides.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- One outfit each, five minutes, then books on the floor.
- Try on every hat in the house and rank them for shade.
- Sort a drawer of her clothes into fits and does-not-fit.
- Lay out four days of hot-weather outfits and stack them in order.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** Strip him to a diaper and a t-shirt for the rest of the day and leave him that way. At 13 months he climbs anything with a lip on it, so the basket of clothes is a thing he will try to stand in, and a full one will go over with him. Keep it half empty and beside you. Give him one sun hat to carry from the pile to the door and back, which is a trip he will make six times. He wants a cup every half hour, sitting down.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **A measurement she takes every day teaches more than a fact does.**
> *Drink your water* is a fact she hears from you and drops on the floor. A line
> on the side of a pitcher is a thing she reads herself, and reading it belongs
> to her. Repeated measuring gives a three-year-old what one explanation never
> does: a number that moves, a reason to go and look, and a stake in what it
> says. She finds out where the water went by watching the level fall past marks
> she drew. Use the same pitcher and the same tape tomorrow and the day after,
> because a measurement earns its keep next to the one she took yesterday.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Heat is the whole hazard today. Water into both of them every
> half hour whether either asks or not, and watch for the quiet flushed child
> with a hot dry neck, which is the one to carry to the coolest room and cool
> down before anything else happens. Neither child goes into a parked car today,
> for any length of time, for any reason, including a two-minute errand: the
> inside of a car in July reaches lethal heat within minutes and the windows
> make no difference. Upstairs rooms hold the day's heat until late, so keep
> both of them downstairs until the sun is off the house. A bottle straight out
> of the freezer sticks to skin, so it stays in the dish towel for both of them.
> A floor fan tips over and a 13-month-old puts fingers through a grille, so
> stand it high and out of his reach or leave it off while he is loose in the
> room.

## 🤒 Sick Day 1 — Fever in a Hot House

This is the flat day. She is hot, heavy-limbed and on the sofa, the plan for
today is gone, and the only thing on the page is a shape for the hours. Nothing
here has to happen, and everything here can be abandoned halfway through
without spoiling anything.

### 🕰️ The Shape of the Day

- **Whenever she wakes.** Straight to the sofa in what she slept in. No table, no getting dressed, no schedule.
- **Before the first long sleep.** One quiet thing on your lap, twenty minutes at most.
- **The rest block.** The middle of the day, and the part everything else bends around.
- **When she asks for food.** Anything she names, in a small amount, on a small plate.
- **The afternoon dip.** The worst hour. Expect tears over nothing and hold the bar on the floor.
- **The long end of the day.** Lights low from five, and bedtime an hour early with nobody in a hurry.

### 🧺 What to Set Up Before She Comes Down

Five minutes of work buys the whole day. Sofa cushions flat, one folded blanket
even in July because a fever runs cold and hot in the same hour, a bowl of cool
water and three washcloths on the floor beside her, her cup with a straw in it
on a low chair she can reach without sitting up, a small trash can within arm's
reach, and the curtains half drawn on the sunny side.

### 🛋️ The Rest Block

The center of the day is a long stretch of nothing, and the job is protecting
it. Lie down with her. Phone face down and out of your hand for the first
twenty minutes of it, because she reads the top of your head as an instruction
to hold on and ask for things. Let her sleep if sleep arrives, and let her lie
awake and quiet if it does not. Two hours of this is a good day's work for both
of you, and the hours after it are easier for having had it.

### 📖 Three Things She Can Do Lying Down

**The Animals That Sleep All Winter.** Her eyes stay shut and you talk, with no
book open and nothing for her to do. A bear in a hole, a bat under a roof, a
hedgehog in a pile of leaves. Ask her what each one needs before it can sleep
that long, and take the answers slow. She is under a blanket in July with the
curtains drawn, which is the same arrangement, and she will get there herself.

**One Thing Out of the Window.** From where she is lying she can see one strip
of window. Name a thing in it, then let her name one, and go slow enough that
the gaps get long. A bird, a car, the light moving off the wall.

**A Sip a Page.** Her cup, a straw and a stack of books. One sip at the end of
every page, counted by her. Small sips going in all day beat a full cup she
refuses at four o'clock, and this turns the drinking into something she runs.

> 👶 **Where Kreston Goes:** A sick sister and a walking 13-month-old cannot share a sofa. He will climb on her, pull her blanket and take the cup, and today she has nothing to meet that with. Give him the floor at the far end of the room behind a gate or a pushed-back armchair, with a box of things he has not seen for a couple of weeks, and put yourself between them. His cup, his spoon and his washcloth stay his, and hers stay hers. If there is a second adult in the house or a grandparent who can take him out for two hours, today is the day to ask.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Thinking about what an animal needs in winter is easier than thinking about herself.**
> Ask a three-year-old with a fever how she feels and you get nothing, or you
> get an answer she has borrowed off your face. Her own state sits too close to
> look at, and the words for it arrive years later. A bear in a hole is far
> enough away to think about. She can tell you the bear needs the dark, a full
> stomach and nobody bothering it, and every one of those is a thing she wants
> and cannot ask for. Take her answers as a list of requests. Sick days go
> better when she works the whole question out on something that is not her.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Fluids matter more than food today, so small sips often, and
> count wet diapers or trips to the toilet rather than guessing. Call the doctor
> the same day for any of these: no pee for eight hours, a fever with a stiff
> neck or a headache she keeps holding, breathing that is fast or looks like
> hard work, a rash that stays visible when you press a glass against it, a
> child too sleepy to wake up to you, or a fever in a child who is getting worse
> after three days. Trust yourself over the list; being worried is a reason to
> call. Keep her cool with a washcloth and light bedding, never a cold bath,
> because shivering drives her temperature back up. Write down the time of every
> dose of whatever medicine your doctor has told you to give, because by the
> afternoon you will not remember.

## 🤒 Sick Day 2 — The Day She Sends You for Things

The fever has broken and she is still not right. She is grouchy, quick to take
offense, tired by anything that lasts ten minutes, and bored out of her mind.
Today has more in it than yesterday and less than a normal day, and the shape
that works is her running things for other people from where she is sitting.

### 🕰️ The Shape of the Day

- **Whenever she wakes.** Dressed if she wants to be, pajamas if she does not. Both are fine.
- **The first stretch.** One thing with an end to it, done on a lap or a sofa.
- **When she asks for food.** She is hungry again today, and small and often still beats a plate.
- **The rest block.** Shorter than yesterday and it still happens, whatever she says about it.
- **After the rest.** The one hour in the day with any energy in it. Spend it on the round.
- **Before the afternoon dip.** Everything winds down from three, because she has half a tank.
- **The end of the day.** Early bath, early bed, and no negotiating over either.

### 🛋️ The Rest Block

She will fight it today, which is the difference between this day and
yesterday. Lay it out as a fact rather than an offer: after lunch everybody lies
down, and she can look at books instead of sleeping. Half of the sleep on a mend
day happens in the twenty minutes after she has announced she is not tired. Stay
in the room. Getting up too early into a full afternoon costs you the evening.

### 🔔 The Round She Runs

Give her the household to run for an hour from the sofa. Set it up as three
jobs, in this order, and keep a warm soapy cloth on the arm of the sofa for the
two that touch food.

**The Drinks Order.** She asks every person in the house what they want, holds
the answers, and sends you for them. She hands each cup over herself and takes
the empties back. Two rounds of this is not too many.

**Her Own Plate of Fruit.** Wash her hands with the warm cloth first, both
sides. You load a plate with four or five pieces out of the fruit bowl and hand
it to her on a towel, and that plate belongs to her. She wipes each piece, sets
them out in an order she likes and eats her way through them. Nothing on the
plate goes back in the bowl for anybody else.

**Kreston's Snack, Handed Over By Her.** Wash both sets of hands again, hers
and yours, before the plate comes anywhere near him. She holds it, he comes to
the sofa for one piece at a time, and she is the one who gives it to him. Three
days of him being kept away from her ends with her deciding when he comes
close, and that order matters to her more than it does to him.

### 📖 Two Things For the Flat Stretch

**The Catalog She Marks Up.** Any junk mail catalog, a pen, and she circles what
she would buy for each person in the house. She will spend twenty minutes on it
and she will explain every choice.

**The Photograph Round.** Your phone, the pictures from last month, and she
names who is in each one and what they were doing. Let her scroll and let her go
back to the same one four times.

> 👶 **Where Kreston Goes:** He has had a strange three days and he wants his sister back, so let them be close in short bursts with you sitting between them. He can carry things to her, which is the job of his month and the perfect job for today: her cup, a book, one shoe, whatever she sends him for. She will manage ten minutes of it and then be finished, so take him off to the other end of the room before she has to say so herself. Cups and washcloths still stay separate for a few more days.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Serving other people is a role she'll take more seriously than being served.**
> Three days of being carried, wiped and handed things puts her at the bottom of
> the house, and a child who has been done to for that long comes back grumpy
> and clinging. Handing her the drinks order turns it over. She has to hold what
> each person said, remember who is still waiting and get it to them, which is
> more thinking than any toy asks of her and it comes with a person saying thank
> you at the end. Being useful is the fastest route back to feeling herself,
> and it costs you nothing beyond fetching what she asks for.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** She is still passing this on. Her cup, her towel and her
> washcloth stay hers for a few more days. Wash her hands and your own before
> she touches any food anybody else will eat, before she hands Kreston his
> snack, and after every diaper and every nose. Swimming, playgroup and
> anybody's house wait until she has been fever-free for a full twenty-four
> hours with no medicine in her. Give a mend day the coolest part of the house
> and keep her out of the July afternoon altogether, because a child who has
> been running a temperature dehydrates faster in the heat than she did last
> week. Keep the fluids going at the same rate as yesterday. You are the one
> who loads Kreston's plate before she hands it over, because at 13 months his
> pieces have to be soft and no wider than his finger, and she has no way of
> judging that. A fever that comes back after a day clear is worth a phone
> call rather than a wait.

## 🎲 July — Extra Ideas

For the morning you open the book with ten minutes, a hot house and no plan.
Nothing here needs a shopping trip and nothing takes longer to set up than it
takes to run.


> ⚠️ These run under the same rules as the rest of the month: an adult within
> arm's reach of any water, small parts up off the floor while Kreston is on
> it, and anything taped across a doorway down and in the trash before he
> crawls through.
### 🎲 Alternative Activities
- **One Cube in Each Hand.** Two ice cubes, one per fist, and the first hand to come up empty wins.
- **The Coldest Thing in the Fridge.** The back of her hand on every shelf and every jar, ranked coldest to warmest.
- **The Full Cup Down the Hall.** A cup filled to the brim carried the length of the hall and back without a drop.
- **The Smell She Has to Find.** One open jar hidden in the kitchen, and she walks toward it with her eyes shut.
- **The Shadow Marked Every Hour.** A strip of tape laid where the sunlight edge falls, once an hour, and the strips counted at dinner.

- **The Photograph She Explains.** One old picture and ten questions about who is in it and where they were.

- **The Paper That Fades.** Keys and a spoon laid on colored paper in the sun for a day, then lifted off.
- **Foil From One Square.** One square of foil, one animal, no tape and no scissors.
- **The Cardboard Fan.** A cereal box cut into a paddle, drawn on by her, waved at whoever is hottest.
- **Newspaper Hats.** One each, then a row of small ones for the animals.
- **Junk Mail Confetti.** Torn small, shaken in a jar, poured out on a dark plate and sorted by color.
- **The Envelope Zoo.** Old envelope corners cut into mouths, drawn on, fed paper scraps.
- **The Long Strip Family.** Everyone in the house drawn end to end on a strip as long as the table.
- **Bottle Cap Printing.** Caps dipped in paint, printed in rows, counted when they dry.
- **Paper Plate on Her Head.** She draws a face on a plate held on top of her head, then looks at what she made.
- **Chalk on Dark Paper.** A flattened cereal box colored dark, chalk over the top, smudged with a thumb.
- **The Flip Book.** Ten pages of a stick figure on the corner of an old notepad, thumbed through fast.

### 👶 Kreston's Turn
- **The Basket That Goes to the Other Room.** Three soft things in, carried through, tipped out, carried back.
- **One Step, Then Down Again.** The bottom stair or a firm cushion, up and down with one hand held, twenty times.

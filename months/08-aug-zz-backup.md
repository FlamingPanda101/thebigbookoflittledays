# 🌧️ August — Backup Days & Extra Ideas

These five pages are for the days August takes off you. Two of them are
weather days, which in this month means a storm that sets in before breakfast
or heat that makes the front step a bad idea by ten. Two are sick days, one
for the worst of it and one for the long bored middle, and neither has a clock
in it. The last page is a list of one-line ideas for the morning you open the
book with ten minutes and no plan.

---

<a id="aug-weather-1"></a>

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 1 — The Storm That Sat Still

August storms tend to blow through in twenty minutes. This one has not. The
sky went dark before breakfast, the rain is coming straight down, the yard is
under an inch of water and the gutter has given up. Nobody is going out today,
so the whole day is built around the one thing a dark house is good for. Week
35 gave her that flashlight and a wall of shadows. Today the same flashlight
hands her color, and she will recognize the setup the moment the curtains go
across.

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Stand every clear glass, jar and plastic lid you own on the kitchen table with a flashlight, a hand mirror and a stack of white paper, set the cornstarch and the food coloring beside them for the afternoon, and check the flashlight batteries before you go up.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: Every Clear Thing in the House
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: The Rainbow She Can Turn Off (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Crossing the Room on Cushions (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: The Puddle That Goes Hard When She Hits It (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🛋️ The Storm Hour: Blankets, Books and the Big Light Off (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: Putting the Cups Out for Everyone
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: The Jars on the Windowsill
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: Warm Washcloths and Pajamas Early
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Every Clear Thing in the House

**🧰 You need:** 1 laundry basket · every clear glass, jar, plastic lid and bottle you own · 1 old CD or DVD · 1 hand mirror.

1. Send her around the house with the basket for anything she can see through. Take the ones you would rather she did not carry out of her hands at the door of each room and carry those yourself.
2. Line them up along the table in front of the window, tallest to shortest, and let her look through each one at the rain.
3. Put the CD and the mirror at the end of the row and tell her both of those have a color hidden in them. Leave the claim sitting there until quarter past nine.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Rainbow She Can Turn Off

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 flashlight with fresh batteries
- 1 tall straight glass, filled to the brim with water
- White paper, 4 sheets
- 1 hand mirror and 1 shallow bowl of water
- 1 old CD or DVD
- 1 roll masking tape
- A room you can get dark

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Curtains across, big light off. Stand the full glass at the edge of the table with a sheet of white paper on the floor underneath it.
2. Shine the flashlight through the glass from low down and off to one side, and walk the beam around until a band of color lands on the paper. She holds the flashlight for this and finds it herself, which takes longer than you want it to.
3. Once she has it, tape the glass down and tape the paper where it lies. Now she switches the flashlight off. The colors go. She switches it back on and they come back in the same place. Let her run that switch as long as she wants.
4. Move the flashlight nearer and further while she watches the band stretch and shrink, then hand the job over and sit where you can see her face.
5. Tilt the CD under the beam. Same light, a different rainbow, and this one slides about the ceiling when her wrist moves.
6. Last one: mirror leaning against the inside of the bowl of water, beam down onto the wet half of it, and the band comes out on the ceiling above her.

> 💡 **Tip:** The angle is the whole trick. A flashlight held straight on gives her a white blob, so start it low and to the side and let her hunt from there.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Crossing the Room on Cushions

Legs still need spending. Pull every cushion in the house onto the living room
floor and lay them in a path from the door to the window, close enough that
she can step between them. The floor is the water. She crosses, and each time
she gets over you take one cushion away and she works out a new route. Give
her the last two and let her set the crossing for you, then follow her route
to the letter, including the bit where you have to jump.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 14 months and walks straight into the
> middle of a dark room. Sit him in the color while it is on the paper, so the
> band lands across his hands, and give him a clear plastic tub to look through
> and carry off. He is at the stage of moving a thing rather than keeping it
> still, and he will bring it back. On the cushions he gets his own two, flat
> on the carpet at the edge, to climb on and off.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Puddle That Goes Hard When She Hits It

Morning was dark and still and about looking. This afternoon her hands go into
something that behaves like nothing else in the house.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- Cornstarch, 1 box, with a cup of it held back in the box
- Water, about half as much as the cornstarch
- 1 large shallow tray or a roasting pan
- 1 old towel under the tray
- Food coloring, 1 color, or a spoon of any drink mix in the cupboard
- 1 spoon and 1 small pitcher
- 3 or 4 small plastic toys
- 1 bowl of warm water and a washcloth, standing by

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Towel down, tray on it, most of the box tipped in and a cup of it left in reserve. Let her run her hands through the dry powder first, because the change is the point and she needs the before.
2. Pour the water in a little at a time from the pitcher while she stirs with both hands. Stop when a spoon dragged through it leaves a line that takes a moment to close behind it. Add powder if it turns to soup.
3. Show her the two things it does. Slap it flat with your palm and it goes hard as a plate. Rest your fingers on it and they sink to the bottom.
4. Roll a lump between her hands. It holds while she works it and runs off her wrist the second she stops.
5. Drip food coloring on the surface and watch the color sit there in a line rather than spreading. She stirs it in, and the whole tray turns.
6. Sink the small toys and let her fish them out. Slow hands get them, fast hands get nothing.
7. Finish with hands in the warm bowl and the washcloth, and leave the tray to dry out rather than washing it into the sink.

> 💡 **Tip:** Half the water you think. Too wet is a tray of milk and the way back is more powder, so the cup you held back stays in the box until the mix reads right.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- One cup of cornstarch in a cereal bowl, on her lap, and stop there.
- Flour and water instead, thick, and let her make a paste to stick paper with.
- Skip the tray and build the biggest cushion pile the room allows.
- Sort the clear jars from this morning by how much water each one holds.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** Cornstarch powder is not for him at 14 months, so
> he is not at this tray. Give him a baking tray with an inch of warm water in
> it on a towel at the other end of the room, two jar lids too wide for his
> mouth, and stay within arm's reach of it. Tip it out the moment he is done.
> Once the tray of goo is drying, let him press one flat hand into what is left
> in the bowl and wash it straight off.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **A rainbow with a switch on it puts the cause in her hand.**
> A rainbow in the sky arrives whole, belongs to nobody, and gives her no way
> in. The one on your floor answers to her thumb. She switches the flashlight
> off and the colors go, switches it on and they come back in the same place,
> and the cause sits in her own hand. Three-year-olds sort the world into
> things that happen and things they do, and moving an event from the first
> pile to the second is worth more to her than any account of white light. She
> will not carry away the word refraction. She carries away that the colors
> were in the flashlight the whole time and the water let them out.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** A hand mirror on the floor with a walking 14-month-old in the
> room is a sheet of glass waiting to be stood on. It stays on the table, it
> goes back in the drawer the moment step six is done, and Kreston is on your
> hip or out of the room while it is out. Check the battery door on the
> flashlight is screwed or clipped shut before it goes near him, because button
> batteries are the worst thing he can swallow. Never pour cornstarch down the
> sink; it sets in the trap. Let the tray dry and throw the powder away. Keep
> the cornstarch box up high and out of his hands, since a lungful of loose
> powder makes a 14-month-old cough hard. An August storm this size carries
> lightning, so nobody stands at a running faucet while the thunder is close.
> Fill the bowls and the washcloth water between rounds of it, and leave any
> bath until the storm has been gone twenty minutes.

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 2 — Too Hot to Open the Door

The other August bad day. No rain, no wind, no excuse she can see out of the
window, and a heat index that makes an hour on the path a bad idea for a
three-year-old and a worse one for a 14-month-old. The blinds stay down,
the house stays shut on the sun side, and the whole day runs indoors on cold
water and moving air.

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Fill both ice trays and a lidded tub, close the blinds on the west side of the house before bed, and stand four clear jars, the shaving foam and the food coloring on the kitchen table.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: Cold Water in Four Jars
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: The Foam Cloud That Rains (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Sky From the Coolest Window (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: Everything the Fan Can Lift (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🧊 Cool-Down Hour: Wet Washcloths on the Coolest Floor (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: The Bowls Down the Table
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: The Jar of Ice She Watches Go
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: A Cool Bath and the Curtains Still Shut
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Cold Water in Four Jars

**🧰 You need:** 4 clear jars or tall glasses · cold water · 2 small cups · food coloring · 1 teaspoon.

1. She fills all four jars two-thirds full of cold water at the sink and stands them on a tray in the middle of the table.
2. Two small cups of water beside them, four drops of coloring stirred into each. That is the rain, and it waits on the table until quarter past nine.
3. Walk the house together while it sits. Flat hand on every window, blinds down on the hot ones, and the two shaded windows opened wide for a draft through the hall. She calls hot or cold at each one and you follow her call.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Foam Cloud That Rains

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The 4 filled jars, on a tray
- 1 can of shaving foam, or a wad of cotton balls teased flat
- The 2 cups of colored water
- 1 teaspoon
- 1 dish towel under the tray
- 1 permanent marker and a strip of masking tape for each jar

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Dish towel under the tray. Heap shaving foam onto the water in the first jar to about a finger's depth and flatten the top with the back of the teaspoon. That is the cloud, and she says out loud what is underneath it.
2. Spoon the colored water on top a teaspoon at a time. She counts each spoon as it goes on. For a good while nothing happens at all.
3. Keep going past the point where she says it is broken. The cloud holds the color until it cannot, then a thread of it drops through and spreads down the jar in slow ropes.
4. Second jar gets a thin cloud, third gets a cloud twice as thick. Tape a strip on each and write her guess at how many spoons it will take before the rain comes. Test them one at a time and write the real number underneath.
5. Read the three numbers back with her hand on the jars. Thicker held more, and she got there before you said it.
6. The fourth jar is hers to wreck. Let her stir it, pile it, tip the whole thing into the sink and start again with whatever cloud is left.

> 💡 **Tip:** Keep her spoon out of the first jar. Stirring gets her the color in four seconds and costs her the only interesting part, which is the wait.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Sky From the Coolest Window

Take the coolest window in the house, the one with the blind still up on the
shaded side, and pull two chairs to it with a pitcher of water. Give her three
names for what is up there: the thin high streaks, the flat cotton ones with
gray undersides, and the tall piles that build all afternoon and turn into the
evening storm. She draws one of each on a card while she looks. Come back to
the same window after lunch and again at four, cards in hand, and see which
kind has taken over. On a humid August day the tall ones win by five o'clock.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 14 months and hot children get quiet
> before they get loud. Wring a washcloth out in cold water and hand it to him
> to chew and wave about, and give him a second one for the back of his neck.
> He has three to five words, so name the fan and the sky every time you pass
> either. At the window he will stop dead when the room darkens under a cloud,
> which is worth watching for.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: Everything the Fan Can Lift

The morning made weather in a jar. The afternoon makes wind in the hallway,
and she gets to decide what it carries.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 fan, standing on a table or a chest of drawers, never on the floor
- Paper napkins or tissue paper, 6
- Printer paper, 6 sheets
- 1 roll masking tape
- 1 cotton ball, 1 coin, 1 metal spoon, 1 plastic lid, 1 sock
- 1 pencil
- String, 4 short lengths, and 1 clothespin

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Stand the fan up on a table pointing down the hall and tape a masking tape line on the floor a long stride in front of it. One rule that does not bend all afternoon: nobody's hands go past that line, hers or yours.
2. Lay the test pile out behind the line. She sorts it into LIFTS and STAYS before a single thing is tested, and you write both lists on a sheet.
3. Drop each one into the moving air from behind the line, one at a time. The cotton ball and the napkin go up the hall, the coin drops straight down, and the sock divides the house.
4. Tear a napkin into long strips and tape them to the pencil. She holds it out at arm's length and watches every strip point the same way.
5. Tape six paper strips along the top of the doorway so the whole doorway moves, then walk her through it.
6. Parachute: one napkin, a short string taped to each corner, all four knotted onto the clothespin. She drops it from standing on a chair with you holding her arm, and it beats the same peg dropped bare.
7. Fan off. Everybody lies on the floor under the strips and watches them come to a stop.

> 💡 **Tip:** Run the fan on its lowest setting. Full blast blows the whole test pile off the floor at once and she never sees one thing at a time.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- One paper fan each, folded from a sheet, and no electric fan at all.
- Blow the cotton ball across the table with a straw and race two of them.
- Tape strips to the back of a chair and pull it up and down the hall.
- Cold cloths, the darkest room, and a long book instead of any of it.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** The fan is the hazard of the day and he is 14
> months, so he does not share the hall while it is on. Give him the far room
> or your hip. Once the fan goes off, lay him under the paper strips and pull
> one across his face with your hand for the last ten minutes. He will carry a
> strip off, and it is paper, so let him.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Naming clouds is the first time the sky has parts.**
> The sky reads to Azlyn as one object, a lid over the day that is blue or is
> not. Hand her three names and it comes apart into pieces she can point at:
> the thin streaks up high, the cotton ones with gray undersides, the tall
> piles that grow all afternoon and turn into tonight's storm. Naming is how a
> three-year-old cuts anything into parts, and the sky is the largest thing
> anybody has offered her names for. She will use them wrong for weeks. Keep
> using yours, take hers as they come, and by the end of the summer she is
> telling you what the weather plans to do before you have looked up.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** A box fan on the floor sits at Kreston's exact height and the
> gaps in the guard take a 14-month-old's fingers. Stand it up on a table or a
> dresser, tape the cable flat along its whole run so nobody trips it or hauls
> it down, and keep him out of that room while it is turning. Shaving foam
> stings hard in eyes and is not for his hands or hers to rub a face with, so
> wash both pairs of hands before anyone touches their eyes and keep the can up
> high. Heat is the real hazard today. Water in front of both of them every
> hour whether they ask or not, and a hot, flushed, quiet child who stops
> sweating needs cooling and a call, not a nap.

## 🤒 Sick Day 1 — Flat on the Sofa

This is the bad one. She has a fever, she is not eating, and she has not
lifted her head off the cushion since seven. There is no schedule on this page
because a clock is no use to you today. The day has a rest block in the middle
of it and everything else bends around that. Nothing here needs cutting,
baking or cleaning up, and all of it can be abandoned halfway through without
anybody minding.

### 🌙 Before She Comes Down
> Make the sofa up before she gets to it: a folded towel over the arm, a cool room with the blinds down, a pitcher of water and a cup with a straw, a bowl within reach in case, and a stack of books on the floor beside her rather than in her hands.

### 🕗 The Shape of the Day
- **Whenever she wakes.** Straight to the made-up sofa, a drink in front of her, and no questions past the one on the envelope.
- **Once she has settled.** The faces go on the envelope, and you ask her the first time.
- **When she asks for food.** Take what she asks for and hold everything else back. Dry toast, a popsicle, cold fruit, nothing on a schedule.
- **The long middle.** The rest block, and it takes as much of the day as it wants.
- **Before the afternoon dip.** One slow story on your lap, then drinks again.
- **The last stretch.** Lights low, the fan across the room, and bed early with the door open.

### 🙂 Once She Has Settled: The Five Faces on the Envelope

**🧰 You need:** 1 envelope or the back of a cereal box · 1 marker.

Draw five faces in a row along the envelope, left to right. The first one is
grinning, the last one is crying, and the three in between move between them.
Number them one to five underneath and give her nothing else to do with it
than point.

Ask her once, then leave the envelope propped on the arm of the sofa where her
hand falls. Ask again after the rest block and once more before dinner, and
write the number and the time along the bottom edge each time. She points at
the crying face for the first three hours and that is your answer, not an
exaggeration. Anything she says on top of the pointing goes on the envelope
too, in her words.

### 😴 The Rest Block: The Hours That Do the Work

This is the center of the day and the only part of it that matters. Blinds
down, big light off, fan on low pointing across the room rather than at her,
one thin blanket within reach so she can push it off. Sit where she can see
you, with your hand on her back if she wants it there and off her if she does
not.

She may sleep for three hours. She may lie there awake and blank for two.
Both count, and neither one needs improving. Keep the drink topped up and
within reach, and resist filling the quiet with anything at all. A sick
three-year-old asleep on a sofa in a cool room is the entire plan working.

### 📚 Before the Afternoon Dip: The Slow Story She Finishes

Pick the book she has heard four hundred times, sit her across your lap, and
read it at half speed. Stop before the last word of every line and wait. She
supplies it with her eyes shut, which costs her nothing and gives her
something to be good at on a day when she has been good at nothing.

If she has no voice left in her, read the whole thing yourself and let the
waiting go. Two books like that, then the drink again.

### 🎯 Four Things That Work Lying Down
- Naming everything she can see from where her head is, ceiling first.
- One hand in a bowl of cool water on a towel on your lap.
- Your phone on the arm of the sofa, playing the songs she picks.
- A cold washcloth over her forehead, taken off and put back by her.

### 👶 Where Kreston Goes

He is 14 months, he walks, and he will climb onto the sofa and onto her within
the first ten minutes. If there is anybody who can take him for a few hours,
today is the day to ask, and ask early rather than at two o'clock when you are
out of road.

If he stays, give him the far end of the room with a laundry basket of safe
things to fill and tip out, and rotate him through the yard door for short
stretches while she sleeps. Keep her cup, her spoon and her washcloth separate
from his and wash your hands between them. He will come and pat her, and one
pat is fine. Standing on her is not, and he cannot tell the difference, so
that one is on you.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **A scale with pictures lets her rate something she can't measure.**
> Ask a three-year-old how she feels and you get *bad*, or nothing, or a word
> she once heard you use about yourself. She has the sensation and no way to
> size it. Five faces on an envelope hand her a ruler she can point at, and
> pointing costs her nothing on a day when talking is work. You get something
> you can compare: the face at eleven against the face at three tells you which
> way the morning went, and it beats your own read of her. Hospitals give
> children the same tool for the same reason. Ask three times, write the number
> down, and she starts reaching for the envelope herself.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** August dehydration moves fast in a child who is not drinking.
> Call your doctor the same day if she has not passed urine in eight hours, has
> no tears when she cries, has a dry mouth or sunken eyes, or is hard to rouse.
> Call straight away for a rash that does not fade when you press a glass
> against it, breathing that is fast or labored, a stiff neck, a fever in
> anyone under three months, a fever over three days, or a seizure. Small
> frequent sips beat a full cup she refuses. Cool the room rather than the
> child; no cold baths and no rubbing alcohol. Check the ingredients on any two
> medicines before you give both, because doubling the same one is the common
> accident.

## 🤒 Sick Day 2 — Better and Bored

The mend day, and the harder one. The fever broke overnight, she has decided
she is fine, and she is wrong. She has no stamina, no patience and a whole day
to spend. Everything on this page is done on a lap, on a sofa or with one
hand, and it can all be walked away from. There is still a rest block, and
today it is the part she argues about.

### 🌙 Before She Comes Down
> Freeze a cup of water solid overnight, so the ice comes out as one block too big to fit in a mouth. Stand a tray, a shallow bowl and a dish towel by the sofa, and put the fan on the far side of the room.

### 🕗 The Shape of the Day
- **Whenever she wakes.** Bigger breakfast than yesterday, and let her ask for it rather than offering it.
- **The first good hour.** Take it. The ice tray on her lap, and the whole morning can be that.
- **When she starts wandering.** She is done, whatever she says. Wind it down before she falls apart.
- **After lunch.** The rest block, and it happens whether she believes in it or not.
- **The long afternoon.** The window list, drinks, and one thing she chooses.
- **Before dinner.** Bath, early bed, and tomorrow still counts as a recovery day.

### 🧊 Whenever She Wakes: The Ice That Melts in Her Hand

**🧰 You need:** 1 block of ice turned out of a cup · 1 shallow bowl · 1 tray · 1 dish towel · 1 teaspoon of salt · 2 drops of food coloring · 1 small pitcher of warm water.

Dish towel on the tray, tray on her lap, bowl in the middle, ice block in the
bowl. That is the whole setup and it is done in a minute.

She holds it until her fingers say stop, then puts it down and picks it back
up. Sprinkle a little salt on the top and leave it. Salt eats channels into
the surface, and the drops of coloring run down those channels and show her
where the block is going first. Give her the pitcher of warm water last and
let her pour it over the top, a little at a time, until there is nothing left
to hold.

If she loses interest after four minutes, that is four minutes and it cost you
one cup of water frozen overnight. Leave the tray there. She comes back to it.

### 😴 The Rest Block: The Afternoon Nobody Skips

She will tell you she is not tired. Put her on the sofa anyway with the blinds
down and the fan across the room, and set the terms low: she does not have to
sleep, she has to lie down. Take one book, read it at half speed, and stop
reading before she stops listening.

Most mend days she is asleep inside fifteen minutes. The ones where she is
not, she lies there for an hour looking at the ceiling and gets up steadier
than she went down. A day back too early buys you two more days on the sofa
later in the week, so hold this hour even when she has spent the morning
looking well.

### 🪟 The Long Afternoon: The List of Everything Out the Window

**🧰 You need:** 1 sheet of paper · 1 pen · the sofa nearest a window.

She lies down where she can see out. You sit with the paper. She calls out
what she can see and you write every one of them down in her words, numbered
as they come. Cars, a bird on the wire, next door's flag, the man with the
dog, a cloud shaped like something.

Read the list back at twenty. Then start a second list of what has changed
since the first one, which she will find funnier than it is. Pin both to the
fridge, because tomorrow she will want to check whether the man with the dog
comes past at the same time.

### 🎯 Four Things She Can Do From the Sofa
- Sorting a bowl of clean socks into pairs on her stomach.
- A flashlight and the ceiling, with the blinds down.
- Telling you what to draw while you hold the paper up.
- One tray of dry crackers she is allowed to eat crumb by crumb.

### 👶 Where Kreston Goes

He is 14 months and next in line for this by the look of it, so today is
about keeping the two of them apart without leaving either one alone. Set him
up on the floor within sight of the sofa rather than on it: he stacks two
blocks, he tips out anything you fill, and a basket and six safe things buys
twenty minutes at a stretch.

Cups, spoons and towels stay separate and go straight in the wash. He will
want on that sofa and he will want whatever she is holding, so give him his
own version of it, a dry bowl and a spoon while she has the ice, and keep the
melt tray up on the table between turns. He gets his own quiet hour at the
same time as hers, and you get twenty minutes.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Cold is easier to investigate than hot, because she can hold it.**
> Heat comes to Azlyn as a rule rather than a material. The stove is hot, the
> concrete is hot, and every sentence she has heard about either one ends in *do
> not touch*. Cold she gets to hold. A block of ice in a bowl on her lap
> answers her, changes while she works on it, numbs her fingers until she puts
> it down, and asks nothing of her. She can push it, drip warm water over it
> and watch salt cut a channel through it. Half her idea of temperature gets
> built out of the half she is allowed to handle, which is why a bowl of ice on
> a bad afternoon does more than a talk about hot.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Freeze the ice in a cup so it turns out as one block too big
> to fit in a mouth. Loose cubes are a choking hazard for both of them, and
> Kreston at 14 months will take one off a tray at his own height in seconds,
> so the tray goes up on the table the moment she puts it down. Salted ice gets
> cold enough to hurt bare skin held against it, so let her put it down when
> she wants to and keep the block off her stomach and out of her mouth. Keep
> pushing fluids today; a child who feels better and stops drinking is the one
> who ends up back on the sofa. Send her back to normal food and normal running
> about a day later than she asks.

## 🎲 August — Extra Ideas

> *One line each, no steps. For the morning you open the book with ten minutes
> and no plan, or the afternoon the heat took the whole thing apart.*

### 🎲 Alternative Activities

- **The Cicada Shell Hunt**, along tree trunks and fence posts at her head height, lined up on a plate at the end.
- **The Fridge Picnic**, everything cold in there on one tray, eaten sitting on the kitchen floor.
- **Half a Lemon Each**, squeezed by hand into cold water with as much sugar as she argues for.
- **The Bug Under the Glass**, a clean jar set over something on the path, a good look, then a lift and a wave.
- **Breakfast Outside at Seven**, the table carried out while the concrete is still cool and nobody has dressed.
- **The Basket of Warm Laundry**, tipped onto the floor for her to find every pair in it.
- **Reading in the Bathtub**, an inch of cool water, no soap, two books that can take a splash.
- **The Nap Under the Table**, pillows pushed underneath and the fan pointed at the gap.
- **Counting the Cars by Color**, from the shaded step, one tally mark each on the back of an envelope.
- **The Long Grass Barefoot**, shoes off on the shaded lawn at six, naming what her feet find.
- **The Cold Bag Race**, unpacking the groceries with the freezer things put away first and fast.

### 🎨 Alternative Arts & Crafts

- **Sun Prints on Colored Paper**, leaves laid out on a sunny sill and lifted two days later to find their shadows.
- **The Paper Bag Sun Hat**, folded, taped to fit her head and drawn all over.
- **Sidewalk Paint**, cornstarch, water and food coloring in muffin cups, brushed on thick and hosed off at five.
- **The Corn Husk Doll**, husks off the cobs from dinner, twisted and tied with yarn.
- **The Torn Paper Sunset**, magazine strips torn along their length and glued down in bands.
- **Seed Mosaic on a Paper Plate**, melon and sunflower seeds saved from the week and glued in rings.
- **The Pressed Flower Card**, whatever is out in the yard flattened under the heaviest book in the house.
- **Flyswatter Painting**, a spoonful of paint on a tray, one clean swatter and a sheet taped to the fence.
- **The Foil Sun Catcher**, a strip of foil scrunched and smoothed again, hung where the light hits it.
- **Chalk Dust on Wet Paper**, a sheet soaked in the sink and chalk dragged across it while it drips.
- **The Cicada Shells on a Card**, the morning's finds glued in a row with a word from her under each one.

### 👶 Kreston's Turn

- **Two Blocks and a Wait**, one stack built for him, the second block handed over, and nothing said.
- **The Fat Crayon and the Taped Sheet**, a big page taped flat to the floor so his whole arm can work.
- **Six Cold Things in a Basket**, wide jar lids and metal spoons out of the fridge, handed to him one at a time.
- **His Three Words Back**, whichever ones he uses today repeated to him inside a whole sentence.

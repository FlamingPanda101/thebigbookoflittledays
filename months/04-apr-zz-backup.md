# 🌧️ April — Backup Days & Extra Ideas

April hands you two kinds of ruined day. On one the weather takes the morning:
showers coming through every hour, wind that leans on the house all day, or a
cold snap overnight that takes back everything last week gave you. The week's
pages want a garden, a walk or a dry path you no longer have. On the other
somebody is ill and the whole day shrinks to the size of the sofa. These five
pages cover both, and the last one is for the morning you open this book with
ten minutes and no plan. Everything here comes out of your own cupboards and
the recycling, because on a day like this nobody is going to the store either.

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 1 — The Walk That Stayed Indoors

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Leave the masking tape, a marker, three slips of paper and a stack of plain paper on the kitchen table, stand six empty bottles or cans from the recycling inside the hall closet, and put her boots by the front door where she will see them.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: Counting Out the Route
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: Three Hundred Steps to the Duck Pond (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Six Windows and the Open Door (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)

- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: The Paper Road (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🎳 Rainy Hour: Bottle Bowling Down the Hall (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: The Forks
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: The Tape Still Down
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: A Hot Bath With the Rain on the Glass
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Counting Out the Route

**🧰 You need:** masking tape · 1 marker · 3 slips of paper · her boots by the door.

1. Ask her where you would have gone today. Take the first answer, even the pond you went to on Sunday, and say out loud that the rain has it.
2. Count her steps from the front door to the kitchen door with her, out loud, and let her hear how few of them there are. Then tell her the pond is three hundred.
3. Write **100** on each of the three slips and put all three in her pocket. She hands you one back every time the count gets there.

### 🎨 The Main Event: Three Hundred Steps to the Duck Pond

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 roll masking tape
- The 3 paper slips marked 100
- 1 chair, 1 coat rack and 2 teddies, as the three stops
- 1 envelope with something in it
- 2 rolled pairs of socks

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Tape a route through the downstairs: out of the hall, around the table, past the sofa, back to the front door. That loop is the walk, and she picks which way it runs.
2. Set the three stops on the loop. The coat rack is the big tree, the chair by the door is the mailbox, the two teddies at the far end are the ducks on the water.
3. Boots on, both of you, and walk it counting from one. Say the numbers yourself and let her join in where she can. She hands over a slip at every hundred.
4. Every lap she does the three jobs: a flat hand on the tree, the envelope posted at the mailbox and fished out again, the socks thrown to the ducks and carried back.
5. Keep going until the last slip is out of her pocket. Three hundred comes in at around six laps and takes longer than either of you expects.
6. Sit down on the floor at the end with the three slips in front of her and ask how far the pond is. Her answer is the whole point of the morning.

> 💡 **Tip:** Do the counting out loud yourself. She loses the thread somewhere in the thirties and picks it back up off your voice, and the walk keeps going.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Six Windows and the Open Door

Go window to window and look out of each one for a minute. Mid-April changes
the view faster than any other month, so most of them have something new in
them: the grass gone shaggy, buds broken on the tree, water standing where none
stood on Tuesday. Finish at the front door. Open it, stand together on the mat,
and let her hold a hand out into the rain until it runs off her fingers.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 10 months and cruises the furniture, so
> run the taped route past the sofa and the low table and he walks a stretch of
> it hand over hand while she counts. He points at the rain on the glass and
> waits for a word, so give him one at every window. At the open door he goes
> still and listens. Keep the envelope, the slips and the tape ends off the
> floor while he is down there.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Paper Road

Her legs did three hundred steps this morning. The same walk goes onto the
table this afternoon, small enough to cross with two fingers.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 long strip of paper, or 4 sheets taped end to end
- Masking tape
- Crayons and 1 marker
- 2–3 small figures or animals
- 1 toy car
- The three paper slips from this morning

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Tape the strip down the middle of the table, both ends stuck flat so it cannot roll itself back up.
2. Start at the left-hand end with your front door and let her draw it. She decides how big it goes.
3. Work along the paper in her order: the tree, the mailbox, the pond, and whatever else she remembers passing. Take her order even where it is wrong.
4. Write what she calls each one underneath in her own words. **The pond with the mean duck** goes down as she says it.
5. Stand the spare figures at the pond and walk one of them along the road with two fingers, stopping where she stopped this morning, and count his steps out loud. He gets there in about twenty.
6. Lay the three slips beside him. Three hundred of hers, twenty of his, and the same pond at the end of both.
7. Leave the road taped down through dinner and let her drive the car up and down it.

> 💡 **Tip:** Let her draw the pond bigger than the house. Size on her map is about how much a place matters, and tidying the proportions costs you the drawing.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- Draw the whole road on the back of a cereal box and stop there.
- Lay the crayons end to end down the table and count how many make a road.
- Mail the three slips under her bedroom door and go in and get them.
- Skip the paper and drive a toy car around the taped route on the floor.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** Sit him at the far end of the table in the high
> chair with a sheet of his own and one fat crayon. At 10 months he holds it
> in his fist, bangs more than he draws, and looks surprised at the mark. He
> points at what her hand is doing, so name it back: drawing, taping, the car.
> Press every tape end flat before he goes down on the floor, because a lifted
> strip is the first thing he takes.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Big numbers mean nothing until she's walked them.**
> Azlyn can say three hundred. The word sits in her mouth with nothing behind
> it, the same as a thousand or a hundred and six. Numbers get their weight from
> something she has done with her body, and three hundred steps through her own
> hallway leaves one in her legs by lunchtime. Children build a rough sense of
> quantity long before they can count with any accuracy, and it grows out of
> amounts they have carried, poured or walked. She will not be able to tell you
> what three hundred is. She will know the pond is a long way and that the
> mailbox comes first, and the counting she learns at five lands on top of that.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Press every end of the tape route flat to the floor and check
> it each time you pass. A lifted strip is a trip at a run for Azlyn and goes
> straight into Kreston's mouth. Shut and latch the front door the moment the
> two minutes on the mat are up, and never leave it standing open with him
> loose in the hall. Socks and an envelope are the only things thrown today.

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 2 — The Wind That Never Lets Up

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Stand two clean jars the same size and a bag of dried beans on the counter, empty the laundry basket, and put a roll of masking tape beside them. Everything else today is already in the house, and the heat goes on before you come down.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: Choosing the Six Games
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: Six Games, Two Jars (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Warmest Place in the House (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: The Shop That Takes Beans (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🎵 Loud Hour: Every Fast Song in the House (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: The Forks
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: Re-pricing the Shelf
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: Bath, and Pajamas Warm Out of the Dryer
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Choosing the Six Games

**🧰 You need:** 1 sheet of paper · 1 marker · the two jars and the beans.

1. Fold the sheet into six boxes and open it flat. Six games today, and she picks them.
2. Walk the downstairs together and take six things two people can do indoors: throw, jump, balance, carry, roll, stand still. She draws one in each box, however it comes out.
3. Stand the two jars on the table with the beans between them. One bean is one point, one jar is one person, and tell her that now so the rules arrive before the noise does.

### 🎨 The Main Event: Six Games, Two Jars

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 2 clean jars, the same size
- Dried beans or dried pasta, a large handful
- 1 roll masking tape for the lines
- 1 laundry basket
- 2 rolled pairs of socks
- 1 hardcover book
- 1 tablespoon and 1 orange, or an onion or a potato if the fruit bowl is empty
- 1 ball, bigger than her fist
- The sheet of six games

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Tape a line across the floor at one end of the longest room and stand the basket four of your paces away from it. That line starts every game, and her sheet of six goes down beside it so you both know what is left.
2. Game one is the socks into the basket, three throws each from the line. One bean into your own jar for every one that lands.
3. Game two is the standing jump. Both of you go from the line and mark the back of your heels with a strip of tape. The further mark takes the bean.
4. Run the other four the same way: the book carried across the room on her head, the orange walked there and back on the spoon, the ball rolled nearest the far wall, the one-leg stand counted out loud.
5. Play your own turns at your real speed on two of the six, and let her watch you drop whatever is on the spoon.
6. Tip both jars out at the end and lay the beans in pairs, hers against yours, until one line runs out. The longer line won and nobody counted a thing.

> 💡 **Tip:** Let her win the ones she wins and lose the ones she loses. A three-year-old will argue with you about the last four throws and will not argue with a jar.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Warmest Place in the House

Go around the house with her hand as the thermometer. A palm flat on the window
glass, on the floor by the front door, on the vent, on the inside of the closet
wall, on the fridge, on the rug where the sun lands. She calls each one warm or
cold before you say a word. April turns cold overnight and the house takes a
day to catch up, so half of these have changed since Sunday. Rank them together,
carry a blanket to the winner, and stay there for five minutes while the wind
works on the windows.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 10 months, and a dried bean is the exact
> size that blocks his airway, so he spends the games hour in the high chair
> beside the action or behind a gate with two plastic beakers and a wooden
> spoon. He rides the warm-and-cold lap on your hip with a palm on the glass,
> and he goes still when a gust hits the window the way he does at running
> water. He understands **no** this month and will look at your face and reach
> for the jar anyway.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Shop That Takes Beans

The beans stop being points and turn into money. This one runs at a table with
everybody sitting down, which is what the afternoon needs after that morning.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The beans, in one bowl
- 8–10 things off the shelves: a can, a box of pasta, a roll of tape, two socks, a book
- Sticky labels, or scraps of paper and masking tape
- 1 marker
- 2 paper bags
- 1 tray as the counter
- 2 cups, one for each purse

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Clear one end of the table for the counter, stand her chair behind it and line the goods up along the tray.
2. Price everything at one, two or three beans and no higher. You write the number, she sticks the label on and says it out loud as it goes down.
3. Count twelve beans into each cup and give each of you a paper bag. You shop first, one thing at a time into your bag, and she checks every payment before it goes in the register.
4. Swap over. She shops with the other bag, you keep the counter, and you refuse the sale when she puts two beans down for a three-bean can. Wait while she fixes it.
5. Sell her out. Once the shelf is bare, restock it together and let her set the new prices, which will be higher than yours.
6. Run one round where she gets three beans and wants five things, and let that sit there without solving it.
7. Count every bean back into the bag at the end, and the jars go up on the shelf with them.

> 💡 **Tip:** Keep every price at three or under. A price she can count on one hand keeps the shop open for an hour, and a five-bean can shuts it inside four minutes.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- One shelf, three things, three prices, and stop there.
- Deliver the shopping to every door in the house in the laundry basket.
- Sort the goods into what you eat and what you wear.
- Play the whole shop with words and no beans, and see whether she notices.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** He is the customer who clears the bottom shelf, which at 10 months is a real
> job and worth handing him on purpose. Put three of the goods low down where he
> cruises past and let him take them off and pass them over. He points at what he
> wants named, so name the can, the box and the sock every time. Sweep the
> floor and count the beans back into the bag before he goes down there.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Scorekeeping turns a chaotic game into a calm one.**
> Two people and one basket ends in a fight about who threw what. A bean dropped
> in a jar settles the same question without either of you having to remember
> anything, and Azlyn holds almost nothing in working memory at three, so an
> argument about the last four throws is one she cannot win on the facts. The
> jar puts the fact outside her head where you can both see it. She also gets
> the shape of a game out of it: rules first, turns in order, an ending that
> arrives whether she likes it or not. Keeping score is what lets her lose a
> round and stay in the game, and that is the piece worth having by five.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Dried beans are a choking size for a 10-month-old with a
> working pincer grip. Kreston stays in the high chair or behind a gate the
> whole time any of them are out of the jars, and you count them out and count
> them back before he goes on the floor. Point the throwing line away from the
> window and the television. Nothing gets thrown or rolled while he is down.

## 🤒 Sick Day 1 — The Day the Fever Runs It

This is the day she is hot, floppy and wants you in the room. There is no
schedule on this page, because a fever runs the day and you follow it. Nothing
here has to be finished, nothing needs cutting, baking or clearing up, and every
one of these can be abandoned halfway with nothing lost. Your job today is
fluids, a lap and the same face every time she opens her eyes.

### 🕗 The Shape of Today

- **Whenever she wakes:** downstairs in her pajamas with her own blanket. Build the corner before anything else happens.
- **After the first long sleep:** one small thing, ten minutes at most, and only if she asks for it.
- **When she asks for food:** give her whatever she names, in a small amount, and take the no without comment.
- **Before the afternoon dip:** the rest block, and the rest of the day bends around it.
- **When the light goes flat:** one story, an early bath if she wants one, and bed a good hour before her usual.

### 🛋️ The Corner She Stays In

Take one end of the sofa and make it hers for the day. Her own blanket, one
pillow, the toy she has slept with all winter, and a bowl on the floor within
reach of her head. Pull the curtain half across so the room goes dim without
going dark. Put her cup on a low table where she can get it herself and refill
it every time you walk past. She will not move off that end of the sofa all
day, and it saves her deciding anything.

### 💧 A Sip Every Time the Song Ends

Fluids matter more than food today, and asking her to drink gets you a no every
time. Put a song on instead and hand her the cup at the end of each one. Small
sips beat a full cup, and cold water, watered juice, a popsicle or a spoonful
of ice cream all count as fluid. Keep her cup and her spoon separate from
everybody else's from the first hour, because that is the part you will be
glad of on Thursday.

### 😴 The Rest Block: Sleep Is the Activity

Sleep is the middle of this day and the point of it. Lie her down under the
blanket at whatever hour she starts to go quiet, put the lights out and lie down
yourself if Kreston will allow it. She may sleep two hours, which is the best
thing that happens today. If she surfaces and cannot go back under, leave her
lying there with an audio story or the radio on low and no expectation that she
does anything with it. Getting up is what starts the crying.

### 🤲 Counting in Squeezes

She keeps one hand in yours and everything else stays where it is. Squeeze her
hand three times and wait. She squeezes three back, then sends you a number of
her own and you send it back. Go up to five and no higher, and let her catch
you getting it wrong. This costs her nothing, asks for one hand and no eyes at
all, and it runs as long as she wants it. It stops the second she lets go, and
it is the one thing on this page that works while she is crying.

### 🪟 The One Thing That Changed Outside

Once each time she surfaces, go to the window yourself and bring one thing back
to her in words. April gives you something new every single day, so there is
always an answer: the grass longer, the tree further on, a bird carrying
something, water standing on the path. Let her guess first and give her three
tries. She never lifts her head off the cushion, and on a day like this it
counts as having been out.

### 📖 The Story She Hears With Her Eyes Shut

Keep the lights off and the book out of it, because holding one up is work and
looking at a page is more work. Use a story she knows and put her in it, walking
to the pond, feeding the ducks, meeting the neighbor's cat. Go slow and repeat
whole lines. She will correct you when you get a detail wrong, which is the best
sign of the day and worth getting wrong on purpose once or twice.

### 👶 Keeping Kreston Out of It Today

He is 10 months, he cruises, and he will head straight for her face and her cup.
Keep him off both. He needs floor space away from the sofa, so gate one end of
the room and put a box of his own things in it, and take her cup up out of
reach every time you stand. Wash your hands between the two of them, more often
than feels necessary. If anybody can take him out for an hour this afternoon,
say yes to it, because his day gets thin today and a walk in the stroller with
somebody else fixes it.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Fluids in, and pee out. No wet diaper or no trip to the
> bathroom in eight hours means you call the doctor today. Call today, and do
> not wait for tomorrow, for a fever over 102°F that will not come down, a
> stiff neck, a rash that stays visible when you press a glass against it,
> breathing that sucks in under her ribs, or a child you cannot wake. Never
> give aspirin to a child. Kreston is under one, so he gets nothing off her
> cup, her spoon or her hands.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Telling a story with no pictures forces her to say what she means.**
> With her eyes shut there is nothing to point at, so the story runs on words
> alone. A picture does half the work for her, since she can put a finger on
> it and say "that one." A story in the dark makes her name the pond and the
> ducks. Getting a detail wrong on purpose pulls those words out of her: she
> hears the mistake, holds the right version in her head, and has to find
> language for it before you can put it back. All of it happens with the
> lights off and her eyes closed, and that is why it still works today.

## 🤒 Sick Day 2 — The In-Between Day

She woke up better and she is not better. The fever has gone, the appetite is
half back, and by nine o'clock she is bored, whiny and following you room to
room. Going out is still off, and a full day would put her back on the sofa
tomorrow. Everything on this page happens sitting down, needs one hand, and can
stop dead the moment she has had enough.

### 🕗 The Shape of Today

- **Whenever she wakes:** dressed, but in soft clothes, and breakfast wherever she wants to eat it.
- **Through the morning:** one thing at a time with a gap after it, and no second thing until she asks.
- **When she asks for food:** a proper meal if she wants one, and half of it back in the fridge if she does not.
- **Before the afternoon dip:** the rest block, and hold this one even though she will fight it.
- **Late afternoon:** the phone call, then a bath and bed at the usual time.

### 🦷 What the Teeth Are For

Sit her on your lap with a mirror and let her look at her own teeth first. Run
a finger along them with her: flat slabs at the back for grinding, points at the
front for tearing off a bite. Then open any animal book you own and go through
it asking one question at each picture, which is what that animal eats. Take her
answer, then look at the mouth together and find the evidence. A rabbit, a dog,
a horse, a bird with no teeth at all. Nothing gets set up and nothing gets
cleared away, and she can stop after four pages.

### 🧦 Pairing the Socks From a Lap

Bring the clean laundry basket to the sofa and tip the socks onto the cushion
beside her. She pairs them, one at a time, at whatever speed she has today, and
you take the pairs as she finishes them. A recovering child wants a job with a
visible end and no standing up in it, and this one has both. She will find the
odd one and want to know where it went, so let that be the interesting part.

### 😴 The Rest Block: The Lie-Down She Argues About

She is well enough to refuse this and not well enough to skip it. Take the
argument out of it: the lie-down happens on the sofa with the curtains half
shut, she keeps her socks on, and she does not have to sleep. Put an audio
story on, lie down at the other end yourself, and say nothing for forty minutes.
About half the time she goes under. The other half she lies there and comes out
of it steadier than she went in, which was the point either way.

### ☎️ Telling It Down the Phone

Call a grandparent, or Joseph, and hand her the phone. She tells them what she
has got, how many days she has had it and what she threw up on. This is the
best twenty minutes of her day and it costs you nothing but the call. Stay
beside her and stay out of it. Whatever she says is the version of the week
that has stuck, and it will not be the one you would have given.

### 🍲 The List of What She Wants Tomorrow

Sit with a scrap of paper and ask what she wants to eat when she is better. Write
every answer down word for word, including the ones nobody is cooking. She draws
one of them at the bottom. Stick the list on the fridge, and buy or cook one
thing off it tomorrow so the list turns out to have been real.

### 👶 Kreston at the Other End of the Sofa

She wants to be near him today and he wants everything she is holding. Sit him
on the floor at the far end of the sofa with the sock basket between them, give
him two socks of his own to pull apart, and keep her hands out of his mouth
until she is a day clear. He is 10 months and cruises the length of the
cushions, so he arrives at her end on his own feet whether or not you planned
it. Her best twenty minutes with him is reading him a board book from where she
is lying, which serves both of them and costs her nothing.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Twenty-four hours clear of a fever and of throwing up before
> she goes near other children, and the clock starts at the last one and not at
> bedtime. Keep the fluids going even though she looks fine, because the day
> after is when the tank runs low. Call the doctor if the fever comes back
> after a clear day, if a cough is still there in two weeks, or if she goes
> quiet and floppy again after a morning of being herself.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Teeth are evidence, and reading evidence is the whole of paleontology.**
> Azlyn looked in a mirror this morning and found flat slabs at the back of her
> mouth and points at the front. Every animal in that book carries the same
> story in its jaw, and she can read it off a picture: points and gaps for meat,
> broad grinding surfaces for grass. A scientist with a jawbone in a drawer
> works the same way, with the animal itself gone for sixty million years.
> Naming what she sees and then saying what it means is a two-step move she can
> make at three, on a sofa, with one hand. The dinosaur skulls she meets at
> seven are the same puzzle with more of the pieces missing.

## 🎲 April — Extra Ideas

For the morning you have ten minutes, no plan and a wet garden. No steps, no
shopping, and nothing here takes longer to set up than it takes to do.


> ⚠️ These run under the same rules as the rest of the month: an adult within
> arm's reach of any water, small parts up off the floor while Kreston is on
> it, and anything taped across a doorway down and in the trash before he
> crawls through.
### 🎲 Alternative Activities

- **Naming the Puddles:** every puddle between your door and the corner gets a name and a size word.
- **The Two-Minute Rain Run:** out in whatever she has on, once around the house, back in, towel over her head.
- **Barefoot on Four Floors:** carpet, tile, wood and doormat, named with her eyes shut.
- **The Shoe Line, Biggest to Smallest:** every shoe in the house laid along the hall in one order.
- **A Ribbon Out the Window:** tied to the latch at breakfast and checked again at every meal.
- **The Stairs, Counted Both Ways:** counting up on the way up, counting back down on the way down.
- **One Thing in a Sock:** one object dropped into a long sock, named by hand before it comes out.
- **Everything Green She Can Carry:** one pile in the middle of the floor, then all of it back where it came from.
- **The Windowsill Cafe:** two cups, two chairs at the glass, and whatever goes past.
- **The Drawer of Spoons:** tipped onto a dish towel and sorted back into families.
- **Warm Hands on Cold Glass:** hands in warm water, then flat on the window, and the print that comes up.

### 🎨 Alternative Arts & Crafts

- **Blowing the Paint Across the Page:** a puddle of watery paint, a drinking straw, and how far she can push it.
- **A Nest for the Ones Outside:** yarn scraps, dryer lint and short bits of string on a saucer by the step.
- **Printing With the Bottom of a Boot:** paint on the sole, then walk it across a long sheet of paper.
- **The Longest Snake in the House:** playdough rolled thin until it runs off the end of the table.
- **Two Tubes and a Piece of String:** binoculars for the street, colored in before they get used.
- **The Drawing You Both Work On:** one sheet, a crayon each, and nobody says what it is going to be.
- **Newspaper Hats for Everybody:** one fold each, and a name on every brim.
- **Foil Pressed Flat:** a smooth sheet and a blunt pencil, and every mark she makes stays there.
- **Blue and White and Nothing Else:** one painting with two colors and no others on the table.
- **The Card That Goes in the Mailbox:** her drawing, your words underneath, walked to the box and posted.
- **A Picture With No Brush:** a sponge, a cotton ball and both thumbs.

### 👶 Kreston's Turn

- **The Cruising Lane:** two chairs pushed together, backs out, so he sidesteps the whole run with a hand on each.
- **In and Out of the Big Pot:** a saucepan and four things too big to swallow, taken out and put back for as long as it holds him.
- **The Point-and-Name Lap:** carried around the room, with a word for whatever his finger lands on, including the fourth time.
- **Paper He Is Allowed to Wreck:** one whole newspaper page, too big to get in his mouth, and the noise is the whole point.

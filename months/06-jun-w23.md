## Week 23: Colors of Summer ☀️
**📅 June 4 – June 10, 2027**

---

### 🎯 What This Week Is About

Color is the one thing Azlyn has all the words for, so this week takes it
apart. She mixes red into five pinks a dot of white at a time, freezes color
and drags it across paper, drops it onto wet paper and gets a circle every
time, leaves it out in the sun until it fades, and pours it bowl to bowl until
all three go brown. Every day she guesses first and looks second, and you
write down both.

Kreston turns 1 on Saturday. Day 156 gives him a banana cake he is allowed to
wreck, one candle for ten seconds, a walk down the block on one finger, a
pencil line on the door frame, and the museum after lunch. Azlyn chooses his icing color
at breakfast and then has to keep her hands off the cake, which is the hardest
thing anyone does all week.

---

### 🧒 What Azlyn Will Learn

- **Color is something you make.** Red walks to pink in five steps, one dot of white at a time.
- **Guess first, look second.** She calls what the cabbage water will do before the lemon goes in, and both answers go on the paper in the same pen.
- **Do it twice.** The same red ice cube down a second sheet, to find out whether the world repeats itself when nobody changes anything.
- **Counting with a reason.** Five blue and two red, dropped in with a teaspoon and spooned back out, gives her a color she can build again from her own card.
- **Things move without being touched.** The sun pulling color out of paper over four hours, a balloon lifting paper it never reaches.

---

### 👩 What You'll Learn

- Appreciation is different from praise, and children need more of the first
- Things that break the rules are the best teachers
- Repeating an experiment is what makes it an experiment
- "Why is it always round?" is a question worth leaving open
- Same object, different result, and the variable is time
- Being wrong in public is a skill, and it's built cheaply here
- Invisible forces are a big conceptual step

### 🎨 The Main Event: Five Steps from Red to Pink

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 6 strips of thick card cut from a cereal box
- Washable poster paint in red, blue and yellow
- White poster paint
- 1 old plate as a palette
- 1 brush
- 1 jar of water
- 1 cloth
- 1 marker

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Rule three of the strips into five boxes each with the marker. Put the other three strips to one side, blank.
2. Box one on the first strip gets red straight from the pot, no white at all.
3. Scrape a little red onto the plate, stir in a dot of white, and fill box two. More white for box three, more again for four and five, until the last box has almost no red left.
4. Rinse the brush in the jar, dry it on the cloth, and run the whole ladder again with blue, then with yellow.
5. Stand all three strips along the windowsill. Ask which two boxes are hardest to tell apart and let her hold the strip to the light.
6. Check the red strip with a finger at 10:15. It dries first, and it goes out with her.

> 💡 **Tip:** Add the white in doses smaller than feel worth doing. A big spoonful jumps her from red to pink and loses the three steps in between.

### 🌍 Around the World: Colors Mean Different Things 🎨

Color carries a different message depending on where you stand. Across much of
East Asia white belongs to mourning and funerals, while red belongs to
weddings, luck and money. Languages divide color up in their own ways too:
Russian keeps separate basic words for light and dark blue, the way English
keeps red and pink apart, and the Japanese word *ao* covers a stretch English
splits between blue and green.

> **✨ Try it:** Cook plain rice for dinner and stir a pinch of turmeric
> through half of it. Yellow rice sits on celebration tables from Spain to
> South Asia. Put both bowls out and let Azlyn choose.

> *Did you know?* The go light on a Japanese traffic signal is called blue.

> **Appreciation is different from praise, and children need more of the first.**

> before you stopped, or there are three reds here and this morning I could
> see one. Children given descriptive feedback stay with hard work longer and
> hold up better when it goes wrong, because their read on it sits in the work
> rather than your face. Thank her for the board on the wall too, which
> appreciates her rather than the paint.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Kreston is 11 months and mouths everything he can reach. Walk

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Smash Cake

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 ripe banana
- 1 egg
- 4 tbsp self-rising flour
- 3 tbsp plain yogurt
- 1 mixing bowl
- 1 small pan, ramekin or oven-safe mug
- 1 spatula, 1 fork
- 1 tray

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. She mashes the banana in the bowl with the fork until she cannot find a lump.

4. Scrape it into the pan and bake it at 350°F for twenty-five minutes. You open the door and you carry the pan, both times.
5. Turn it out onto the tray and stand it somewhere cold. Cream cheese slides off a warm cake, so it has until one o'clock. Washing the bowl is her job while it cools.

> 💡 **Tip:** Build it for wrecking. Anything you tidy up turns it into a cake somebody wants kept, and this one has about eleven minutes to live.

A sheet on the floor, a cake on the sheet, and a one-year-old who has never

### 🎨 Second Main Event: Five Blue, Two Red

Everything in this one gets counted, and a color she invents gets built twice.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 6 clear cups or jam jars
- Water
- Food coloring in 3 colors
- 1 teaspoon
- 6 cards and 1 pen for the recipes
- 1 tray
- Paper towels

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Paper towels down first, tray on top, six cups on the tray, each half full of water from the tap.
2. She invents the first color. Drops go in one at a time and she counts them out loud: five blue, two red.
3. Write the numbers on a card while she watches. Whatever she calls the color goes on the card too.
4. Second cup, and she builds it again from her own card. You read the numbers back and she counts the drops in.
5. Hold the two cups to the window side by side. If they do not match, count what went in and hunt for the missing drop.
6. If one comes out too strong, she spoons some off with the teaspoon and tops the cup back up with water.
7. Three recipes is plenty, and that is your six cups. Lay the cards along the table with their cups behind them and photograph the row.

> 💡 **Tip:** Let her name them. Dragon and sad blue and Kreston beat dark purple, because a name she invented is one she can find again a week later.

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Repeating an experiment is what makes it an experiment.**
> Running the red cube twice looks like an afternoon of the same thing. Azlyn
> is doing what every scientist does before a result counts, checking that the
> world behaves the same way when nobody changed anything. A three-year-old
> has no reason to assume it will. Her days are full of one-offs, and the idea
> that a cause repeats gets built out of cases she watched happen twice. The
> second run is also the only thing she can compare the first against, so it
> is where small differences show up. Say the numbers out loud both times and
> let her call the match.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Colored ice cubes look like something to eat, and a cube is
> the wrong size and shape for a one-year-old. Keep the metal tray up on the
> kitchen table, count them out and count them back. Craft sticks stay off the
> floor. Wet paving is slippery under bare feet, so shoes on for the painting.

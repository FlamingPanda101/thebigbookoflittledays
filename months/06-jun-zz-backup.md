# 🌧️ June — Backup Days & Extra Ideas

These pages are for the mornings the plan does not survive. Two of them are
for weather that keeps everybody in, which in June means heat as often as
rain, and both are written for a day when going out is off the table. Two
more are for the days somebody is sick, and those run on a different clock,
because a sick day has no nine fifteen in it. The last page is a list to read
standing up, for the ten minutes when you want one idea rather than a whole
day.

---

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 1 — Too Hot to Go Out

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Fill two pitchers with water and stand them in the fridge, and dig out a small screwdriver and two dead ballpoint pens.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: Shutting the House Against the Sun
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: The Taking-Apart Table (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Coldest Floor in the House (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: The Rope She Twists Herself (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🧊 The Cold Hour: A Bowl of Ice and Two Spoons (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: The Cold Drinks Round
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: The Parts Tray
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: A Cool Bath and the Dark Room
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Shutting the House Against the Sun

**🧰 You need:** every curtain and blind on the sunny side · 1 baking tray · her.

1. Walk the house with her and shut everything on the side the sun is on. Tell her the plan out loud: the house stays dark and shut all morning, and the two of you stay in it.
2. Send her hunting for six things that look like they come apart. She comes back with a pen, a clothespin, an old flashlight, a whisk.
3. Lay the six in a row on the tray and put it in the middle of the table. Nothing gets opened until 9:15.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Taking-Apart Table

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 2 dead ballpoint pens, the kind that unscrew
- 1 wooden clothespin
- 1 old flashlight, batteries out
- 1 whisk, 1 tape dispenser, or anything else off the tray
- 1 small screwdriver (**adult only**)
- 1 baking tray, to hold every part
- 1 sheet of paper, 1 pencil, 1 marker

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Pen first. Unscrew it over the tray and lay the parts out in the order they came off: cap, barrel, ink tube, spring, tip.
2. Hand her the name of each part as it lands, then say it again when she picks it up. **Spring** is the one she keeps.
3. Second pen, and this one is hers to open on her own. Sit on your hands, and let her turn it the wrong way for a while.
4. Clothespin next. Two wooden legs and a spring, and the word she learned five minutes ago turns up inside something else. Then the flashlight, which is yours to open with the small screwdriver while she holds each piece and says its name back to you.
5. Draw around every part on the paper with the pencil and write its name beside the outline in marker. That sheet is how she gets the pen back together.
6. Rebuild one pen together, then let her pick one more thing off the tray, the whisk or the tape dispenser, and pull that one apart last. The rest stay in pieces on the tray until 4:15.

> 💡 **Tip:** Give her the words and stop there. She wants the name of the small coiled thing more than she wants to know what it does, and the explanation keeps for a year or two.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Coldest Floor in the House

Shoes and socks off, and walk the house barefoot hunting for cold. The tile by
the back door, the bathroom floor, the strip of hall the sun has never reached,
the inside of the cupboard under the stairs. She reports on each one and you
put a hand down beside her feet. Let her pick the coolest room in the house,
and move the afternoon into it.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 12 months and puts a hand out for
> whatever is on the table, so the tray of springs and pen tips stays pushed to
> the far side and never comes down to him. Give him a metal bowl and a wooden
> spoon at the other end, which is his version of finding out what a thing
> does. Strip him to a diaper and offer his cup every time you pass him. He
> drinks from it without help now, and at a year he heats up faster than she
> does.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Rope She Twists Herself

The morning pulled things to pieces. This afternoon she starts with a flat
sheet and finishes holding something that did not exist at lunchtime.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 old bedsheet or a worn t-shirt, or 6 plastic grocery bags
- 1 pair of adult scissors (**adult only**)
- Thick wool or garden twine, 2 yards, if you would rather not cut up a sheet
- 1 door handle or a chair back, to anchor the strands
- 1 marker
- 1 shopping bag loaded with books, as the weight to test it against

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Cut the sheet into strips two fingers wide, as long as the sheet will give you. You cut, she counts them into a pile and says when there are six.
2. Knot two strips together at one end and hook the knot over a door handle. She takes one strand, you take the other, and you both stand back until the pair pulls straight.
3. Twist. Both of you turn the same way until each strand is tight enough to want to curl back on itself. Say the direction out loud every few turns.
4. Walk your two hands together and let go of the middle. The strands wrap around each other on their own, and her face does the rest.
5. Knot the loose end, take it off the handle, and let her pull on it with both hands as hard as she can.
6. Hang the bag off it and add one book at a time until it fails or she runs out of books.
7. Measure the rope against her and mark her height on it with the marker, then coil it and hang it high, and tell her why it goes up there.

> 💡 **Tip:** Both of you twist the same way. One strand turned backwards cancels the other and the cord falls apart in her hands, which is worth doing on purpose once she has a good one.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- Twist one short cord out of garden twine and stop there.
- Braid three strips flat on the floor instead of twisting two.
- Tie the finished rope between two chairs and hang socks off it.
- Pull the sheet into strips by hand along a snipped edge, no cutting at all.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** Long strips of fabric stay off the floor while he
> is on it, so give him three folded washcloths and a big plastic bowl to post
> them into and tip back out. At 12 months a one-step instruction lands, so
> hold your hand out and ask him for one washcloth at a time from where he
> sits. Cup of water on the hour, and he plays on the coolest floor in the
> house.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Making a material from scratch is rarer than making a thing.**
> Azlyn makes things most days of her life. A rope is a different order of
> object: she started with a flat sheet and ended up holding something with a
> property the sheet never had. Materials reach her finished, and the making of
> them sits behind a door she has no picture of. Twisting two strands and
> watching them lock together puts one whole step of that in her own hands. She
> meets the same idea in paper, in cloth, in flour. Hang the rope where she can
> reach it and leave it up for a week, because the strength she built into it
> is the part she will keep testing.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Check the flashlight for a coin-shaped button battery before
> she opens anything, and take every one of them out of the room. A swallowed
> button battery burns through the throat inside a couple of hours and is an
> emergency room trip that hour, not a wait and see. Springs, pen tips, ink
> tubes and screws stay on the tray, the tray stays on the table, and you count
> the parts back before Kreston is on the floor. Drinks go around every hour
> whether or not anybody asks. The scissors stay with you.

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 2 — The Storm That Sits Over the House

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Stand a heavy bowl, the rolling pin, the sieve and a bag of rolled oats on the counter, and put an old towel by the kitchen table.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: Three Bowls of Grain
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: Grinding the Oats by Hand (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Counting to the Thunder (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: The Laundry Done by Hand (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🧺 Hanging It Up Around the House (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: Sweeping Under the Table
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: The Sieve and the Bowl
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: Rain on the Window, Two Books
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Three Bowls of Grain

**🧰 You need:** 3 small bowls · rolled oats · rice · all-purpose flour.

1. One thing per bowl, laid out in front of her, and a finger into each before anybody says a word.
2. Ask her which two are the same thing and let her argue it out. She will pair the oats with the rice, because both of them rattle.
3. Tell her the flour started as something with a rattle in it, and leave that where it lands until 9:15.

### 🎨 The Main Event: Grinding the Oats by Hand

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- Rolled oats, 1 cup
- 1 heavy bowl with high sides, a deep mixing bowl or a mortar
- 1 rolling pin, or the end of a wooden spoon, or a jam jar with a heavy base
- 1 sieve and 1 more bowl
- The bag of all-purpose flour from the Opening Activity
- 1 tablespoon
- 1 kitchen timer, or the one on your phone
- Paper and 1 pen for the count

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Half a cup of oats into the heavy bowl. She pushes and turns with the end of the rolling pin, and the first thing she learns is that oats jump.
2. Set the timer for two minutes and let her work until it goes. Then both of you stop and look at what is in there.
3. Sieve the lot over a second bowl. The flour drops through and the rest stays behind. Tip what stayed behind back into the heavy bowl.
4. Run that loop four times, adding each sieving to the same bowl and marking a line on the paper for every round.
5. Hold the finished flour up in the tablespoon. Half a cup of oats gives her about two spoonfuls, and the gap between those two amounts is the whole morning.
6. Open the bag of all-purpose flour beside it. A finger in each, and a straight question: which one took longer?

> 💡 **Tip:** Use a bowl with real sides. A flat plate sends oats across the kitchen in four seconds and the activity becomes sweeping.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Counting to the Thunder

Set two chairs a body's length back from the window and sit there with her.
Count out loud from the flash and stop counting when the rumble arrives. Five
seconds is about a mile, so write each number on a scrap of paper and lay them
in a row on the floor. The numbers shrink while the storm comes on and grow
while it leaves, and she can see that on the paper before she can feel it in
the sky. Once the count holds above ten the storm has moved off, and the two of
you can carry the chairs up to the glass and watch the rest of it go.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 12 months and takes his reading of a
> bang off your face before he decides anything about it. Sit him on your knee
> back from the glass and stay loose in the shoulders through the loud ones.
> Give him a spoonful of cooked oatmeal on his tray at the table while Azlyn
> grinds, and his own metal bowl and spoon at the far end, well clear of the
> rolling pin.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Laundry Done by Hand

Grinding was work with a hard result. The afternoon runs the same argument
through warm water, and she gets to see the dirt come out.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 2 dishpans or big mixing bowls, on the table
- Warm water for both bowls, drawn before the storm sits overhead, and 1 squirt of dish soap
- 8–10 small things worth washing: socks, washcloths, dish towels, her own t-shirt
- 1 old towel under the bowls and a second one folded beside her
- String across the kitchen, a drying rack, or two chair backs
- 8 clothespins

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Fill both bowls in a lull or before the storm settles overhead, one warm and soapy, one warm and clean for rinsing, and carry them to the table with the towel underneath. The sink is finished with for the afternoon. She kneels on a chair to reach.
2. One sock to start. In, squeeze, rub the toe against itself, and up out of the water twenty times.
3. Into the rinse bowl next. Hold it up between the two of you and watch the soap leave it.
4. Wringing takes more grip than she has, so she twists one end while you twist the other over the folded towel and count the drips.
5. She pegs each one along the line and decides the order. Hang the heavy things at the ends where the string sags least.
6. Keep going until she stops. Six things washed by a three-year-old is a full afternoon.
7. Tip both bowls out once the storm has moved off, stand them upside down and count what is hanging. Count it again at bedtime and see what has dried.

> 💡 **Tip:** Warm water, not hot. Her hands are in it for an hour, and she will tell you it has gone cold long before it has.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- One sock, one bowl, and stop when the sock is clean.
- Wash one window from the inside with a cloth and a bowl of warm water.
- Hang everything on the backs of the dining chairs and skip the line.
- Scrub the muddiest pair of shoes in the house with an old toothbrush.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** No bowl of his own on the floor today. Give him a
> wrung-out washcloth on his tray and one instruction, *wipe*, which he can
> follow at 12 months and will run twenty times over. Later he gets the empty
> clothespin bag to fill and empty where he sits. Hand him one clothespin at a
> time and take it back before it goes in his mouth.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Grinding grain by hand is how she finds out that food used to be work.**
> Flour arrives in this house in a bag, and the bag is where the story stops.
> Half a cup of oats and twenty minutes of pushing gives Azlyn two spoonfuls
> and an ache in her wrist, and the ratio between those two things is the
> lesson. For most of human history somebody spent a good part of every day
> doing what she did before ten o'clock, and most of them were women and girls.
> She has no use for the history yet. She has use for the arm. Effort is how a
> three-year-old works out what a thing is worth, and she eats what she ground
> with an attention no store loaf has ever earned.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Stay in until half an hour after the last rumble, and settle
> the question of going out to look before she asks it. A strike travels
> indoors through plumbing and wiring, so the bath waits and nobody stands at
> the faucet while the storm is overhead. Fill the laundry bowls in a lull or
> before it settles in, then leave the sink alone until it has moved off. Keep
> both children a body's length back from the glass while the flash-to-rumble
> count sits under five, and do the counting from a chair or the doorway. The
> rolling pin lives on the table and goes back to the table between grinds,
> because Kreston is 12 months old and the table edge sits at his eye level.
> The laundry bowls stay up on the table too, never on the floor.

## 🤒 Sick Day 1 — The Day She Stays Down

Nothing on this page has a time on it. She is flat, the plan is canceled, and
the only two jobs today are fluids and company. Everything below can be
abandoned in the middle without anybody minding.

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Nothing to set up. If she went to bed hot, put a pitcher of water, a stack of washcloths and a spare blanket on the arm of the sofa before you go up.

### ⏰ The Shape of the Day
- **Whenever she wakes**, blanket to the sofa, no getting dressed, something to drink within her reach.
- **The first stretch**, nothing asked of her at all. Sit down yourself while you can.
- **When she asks for food**, dry toast or nothing, and neither answer is a problem.
- **The long middle**, the rest block, and the rest of the day bends around it.
- **After the first long sleep**, the naming game, run from where she is lying.
- **Before the afternoon dip**, one book she picks, read twice.
- **The end of the afternoon**, drawing on her back with the lights low.
- **Bed early**, the same books in the same order, nothing new in the room.

### 🛋️ The Rest Block

The rest block is the day, and everything else is what happens around its
edges. Make one place on the sofa and keep her in it: her pillow from
upstairs, a blanket she can push off, a cup on the floor where her hand
reaches, and the curtains half shut against a bright June morning. She will
sleep in stretches at hours that make no sense. Let every one of them run to
its own end. Sit in the room while she sleeps if you can manage it, because
she surfaces, checks that you are still there, and goes back down faster when
the answer is yes. The television is allowed and it is not the plan, so put it
on for a stretch and take it off again, or the afternoon has nothing left in
it.

### 🔦 Naming What She Can See From There

She stays flat and the two of you take one ordinary object to pieces with
words. Start with the lamp: shade, bulb, cord, switch, the collar that holds
the shade on. Then her own hand: palm, knuckle, wrist, the crease at the
bottom of her thumb. Then a shoe, a door, the window. Give her the word, let
her say it back or ignore it, and move on. Ten minutes of this is plenty, and
the only material is your voice.

### 📚 The Pile She Chooses From

Bring eight books down and put them on the floor beside her within arm's
reach, then read whatever she hands up. She will pick the same one four times.
Read it four times. A book she has memorized asks nothing of a sick brain and
gives her the one thing she can control today, which is what happens next.

### 🖐️ Drawing on Her Back

Sit behind her and draw a shape on her back with one finger: a circle, a line
down, a big letter A, a rain shower with your fingertips. She guesses. Then
she does one on your arm and you guess, and you get it wrong on purpose more
often than you get it right. It costs nothing, it works lying down, and it
carries the touch she wants without either of you having to talk.

### 👶 Where Kreston Goes

A sick sibling changes his day more than hers. If there is anybody who can
take him for a few hours, today is the day to ask, and take the offer without
apologizing for it. If he stays, keep him off her: his floor is the far end of
the room with the door gate up, his own cup and his own washcloth, and nothing
of hers goes near his mouth. At 12 months he comes over to see her every few
minutes, walking or on all fours, so set something up at his end that holds
him. A stack of plastic containers to pull apart and press back together will
run a long time, and it keeps him near her without landing on her.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Naming the parts of something ordinary makes it interesting again.**
> Azlyn has looked at that lamp her whole life without once looking at it.
> Handing her the words breaks it open: shade, bulb, cord, switch, the collar
> under the shade. A named part turns into something she can hunt for in other
> objects, and she starts finding switches and cords around the room without
> being asked. Naming carries attention with it. Most of the vocabulary she
> owns at three arrived this way, an adult putting a word on a thing while she
> was already looking at it. On a day when she cannot get off the sofa, the
> lamp and the window and her own hand are the whole available world, and every
> one of them has parts.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Write the time of every dose on a piece of paper on the fridge
> along with what you gave. On a long day with two adults going in and out, the
> dose given at eleven gets given again at twelve from memory. Medicine goes
> back up on a high shelf between doses, because Kreston is up on his feet at
> the furniture now and a bottle on the arm of the sofa sits at his eye level.
> Call the doctor rather
> than sitting on it if she will not drink, if she has not peed since the
> morning, if a fever holds past what you expected, or if she looks wrong to
> you in a way you cannot name. Nobody has ever been told off for calling.

## 🤒 Sick Day 2 — Bored, and Not Better Yet

This is the harder of the two days. She has enough in her to want something
and not enough to do it, and she will ask for the garden about forty times.
Everything here happens on a sofa or a lap, and the answer about the garden
stays no.

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Nothing to set up. If there is heavy cream in the fridge, stand it at the front and put a clean jam jar with a tight lid beside it.

### ⏰ The Shape of the Day
- **Whenever she wakes**, downstairs in pajamas, and say out loud that today is another indoor day so it stops being a negotiation.
- **The first stretch**, the jar, which takes as long as it takes.
- **When she asks for food**, more than yesterday and less than she thinks.
- **The long middle**, the rest block, shorter today and still the center of it.
- **After the rest**, the bag of things she has not seen since March.
- **Before the afternoon dip**, the list, dictated from where she is sitting.
- **The end of the afternoon**, a window open, a blanket, and a look at the street from indoors.
- **Bed at the usual time**, because tomorrow is the day she goes back out.

### 🛋️ The Rest Block

She will tell you she does not need it. Put it in the middle of the day
anyway, and drop the word rest: pull the curtains, lie down beside her with a
book, and let the room go quiet for forty minutes. A child on the mend runs at
full speed for an hour and then falls apart at four o'clock, and the block in
the middle is what buys you a calm evening. If she sleeps, she needed it. If
she lies there and talks at you for the whole forty minutes, that counts too.

### 🫙 The Jar That Changes While She Holds It

Half fill the jam jar with heavy cream, screw the lid down hard, and give it
to her. You do most of the shaking today. She takes it back when she wants it
and hands it over the second her arms give out, and neither of you keeps count.
Nothing happens for a long stretch, and then it happens inside about four
seconds: the sloshing stops, a lump appears, and the jar goes quiet. Open the
lid, let her look in, and stop there. The butter keeps in the fridge until she
is well enough to want it on toast. An empty fridge runs the same activity on
staples, a jar half full of dry rice shaken until the sound changes, or an inch
of water and a squeeze of dish soap shaken up to a head of foam. She may have
done the cream in February. She will remember none of the numbers and all of
the moment it turned.

### 🧺 The Bag of Things She Has Not Seen Since March

Go through the closet and the back of the toy box and fill a bag with anything
she has not laid eyes on for three months. Tip it out on the sofa beside her
and leave it there. Old things come back as new things, and she can play with
them at half power without needing you to explain anything. Anything she
ignores goes back in the bag for the next sick day.

### 📋 The List of What She Will Do When She Is Better

Give her a pen and take the writing yourself. Ask what she wants to do the day
she goes back out, and put down every answer word for word, in her order,
including the ones that make no sense. Read the list back to her twice. Tape
it low on the fridge where she passes it, and cross each line off with her
over the next week, because a list she dictated on a bad day and completed on
a good one is worth more than either half on its own.

### 👶 Where Kreston Goes

He is either coming down with it or he is climbing on her, and neither one
suits her today. Keep their cups apart, wash your hands between the two of
them, and expect to lose. Give him his own version of what she is doing: a
plastic bottle with two spoons of water in it and the lid taped down with a
strip of packing tape, to shake beside her while she works the cream, a
folded blanket of his own on the floor by the sofa, and his cup within reach.
At 12 months a one-step instruction lands about half the time, so *give it to
Azlyn* is worth saying, and it gets him near her with
something in his hands instead of on top of her.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Waiting for cream to turn into butter is a bet she'll want to see through.**
> Nothing happens in that jar for a long stretch, and then it all happens at
> once. Azlyn shakes it, hands it back, asks twice, and stays with it anyway,
> because she has put something in and wants to see what comes out. Waiting has
> a floor and a ceiling at three. She cannot hold out for a week, and she can
> hold out for eleven minutes when the thing she is waiting on sits in her lap
> where she can hear it changing. Being ill strips away most of what she gets
> to affect. The jar hands one thing back, and she gets to call the moment it
> turned.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Standing water waits for a whole ordinary day. A child on the
> mend is unsteady and off her food, and the kiddie pool and the dishpan both
> stay empty and turned over until she has had one full day back to herself.
> The jar lid comes off the floor before Kreston is down on it, because a
> screw-top lid is a choking size at 12 months. Hand washing after every trip
> to her and before you touch his food buys you a day or two of stagger rather
> than both of them at once.

## 🎲 June — Extra Ideas

### 🎲 **Alternative Activities**

- **Barefoot on Six Surfaces**, grass, paving, doormat, tile, carpet and gravel, named as she crosses each one.
- **One Hand Cold, One Hand Warm**, two bowls and a third in the middle that both hands go into at the end.
- **Melting an Ice Cube in Her Fist**, one cube each, and the first hand to come up empty wins.
- **The Hallway Bowling Alley**, six empty bottles at one end and a soft ball at the other.
- **The Upside-Down Afternoon**, dinner food at four and pajamas straight after it.
- **Hide the Timer**, wound up, hidden in one room, and found by the ticking.
- **The Great Shoe Sort**, every pair in the house paired up and lined out by size along the hall.
- **Counting the Steps to the Corner**, walked out at eight in the morning before the heat arrives.
- **The Watering Can Round**, every pot in the yard done before the sun gets on them.
- **The Fridge Inventory**, she names everything on one shelf out loud while you write it down.
- **Grandma on Speakerphone**, she does the talking and you stay out of it.

### 🎨 **Alternative Arts & Crafts**

- **The Paper Bag Cut Flat**, a bigger sheet than any she owns and crayons in the middle of it.
- **Chalk on the Shady Wall**, one wall she is allowed to draw on and a bucket of water to take it off.
- **Painting the Path With Water**, one wide brush and a bucket, and the whole thing gone by lunch.
- **The Sticker Made of Tape**, a small drawing on a strip of masking tape, stuck where she says.
- **A Crown From a Cereal Box**, cut to fit her head and taped at the back.
- **Fingerprint Bugs**, one thumb, washable paint, and two legs drawn on each.
- **The Straw Necklace**, drinking straws cut into lengths and threaded onto wool, up out of reach when she finishes.
- **A Cover for the Book She Loves**, brown paper, her title, her name.

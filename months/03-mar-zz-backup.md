# 🌧️ March — Backup Days & Extra Ideas

March hands you three good afternoons and then takes them back. These pages are
for the mornings the plan does not survive the weather, and for the mornings
somebody wakes up hot and gets no further than the sofa. Two of the days here
need nobody to leave the house, and two of them need almost nothing from either
of you. The last page is for the ten minutes when you open the book with no
plan and a child already asking.

---

<a id="mar-weather-1"></a>

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 1 — The Visitor Who Got Rained In

Cold rain, the kind that comes sideways and turns the yard to soup. Nobody is
going out and nobody is coming over. Today the house gets a guest instead, and
Azlyn has to explain the whole place to him.

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Sit a soft toy on the mat by the front door with a paper bag beside him, so she finds him there before you are up. Put a shoebox, a ball of string and a roll of masking tape on the counter for the afternoon.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: The Visitor at the Door
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: Showing Him How It All Works (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Long Way to Every Room (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: The Bed He Has to Fit In (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: Drying the Windowsills
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: The Box and Everyone Who Fits In It
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: A Book He Has Never Heard
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: The Visitor at the Door

**🧰 You need:** one soft toy she has not played with in weeks · a paper bag.

1. Let her find him. Tell her he came in out of the rain, his home is a long way off, and he cannot leave until it stops. Point at the window. The window is doing the arguing for you today.
2. Ask her three questions and take the first answer she gives: his name, where he came from, and one thing he has never seen before.
3. Write his name on the bag. Let her carry him around while you clear breakfast, and listen. She will start explaining the toaster to him without being asked, which is the 9:15 activity beginning an hour early.

### 🎨 The Main Event: Showing Him How It All Works

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The visitor
- 1 sturdy step stool
- 1 dish towel for spills
- 1 plastic cup
- Your worst pretend voice

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Tell her the visitor needs to know how six things in this house work, and let her choose all six. Faucets, light switches, the toilet flush, the fridge door, the front door lock and the washing machine dial are the usual six, and hers may be stranger.
2. At each stop she does two jobs in order: tell him what it is, then make it work in front of him. Hold the toy at her height so he can see.
3. You are the visitor. Ask one stupid question per stop in his voice. *Does the water live in there? Where does it go after?* She has to answer him, and her answers are the point of the day.
4. At the sink, set the faucet to cold before she climbs the stool, and stand behind her. She fills the plastic cup for him, tips it back out, and dries the counter with the dish towel so the visitor sees the whole job.
5. At the end, ask her which one was hardest to explain. Go back to that one and let her try it again. She will do it better the second time.
6. Last lap, at speed: she names all six in the order she picked them. Count them off on her fingers with her.

> 💡 **Tip:** Leave her explanations alone. *The water comes down from the sky into the pipe* is close enough to true, and correcting it costs you the rest of the tour.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Long Way to Every Room

Rain on a window is worth ten minutes of watching and no more. She has a full
hour of legs in her and no yard to put it in, so give her the visitor to carry
and one rule: every room in the house, and no room twice. Every time you call
**switch**, she changes how she travels. Stomping, tiptoeing, crawling on all
fours with him balanced on her back, sideways along the wall, backwards with
one hand on the doorframe. Count the rooms out loud as she clears them and let
her decide whether the hallway counts as a room. Finish with the same lap done
as slow as she can manage, which is harder than the fast one and settles her
before snack.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 9 months and pulling himself upright on
> anything that holds still. Let him do his own tour along the sofa and the
> coffee table while she does hers. Move the sharp-cornered furniture out of
> the route first and put the fire guard or the stair gate across the stairs
> before either of them starts, because today they are both moving through the
> whole house. He waves now, so have her wave at him from each doorway and wait
> for the wave back. He will come to the doorframe to get it.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Bed He Has to Fit In

The visitor is staying the night, since the rain has not let up. Morning was
talking and walking. This afternoon she measures, cuts and pads.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The visitor
- 1 length of string, about 3 feet
- 3 boxes of different sizes from the recycling, one a shoebox
- 1 roll masking tape
- Scissors (adult only)
- 1 washcloth, 1 clean sock, 1 dish towel
- Crayons

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lay him flat on the floor and lay the string beside him. Pinch the string at his head and his feet, and cut it there. That piece of string is now the visitor.
2. Hand her the string and send her to the three boxes. She holds it across each one: too short, too long, right.
3. Cut the chosen box down to bed height yourself while she holds it steady. Tape the raw edge over so it stops scratching.
4. She makes it soft. Washcloth for a mattress, the rolled sock for a pillow, dish towel folded over for the blanket. Let her put him in and take him out until she is satisfied with the depth.
5. Crayons on the outside, and tell her he will be looking at the inside all night, so the inside earns some color too.
6. She picks where the bed goes and tells him one last time where the bathroom is.

> 💡 **Tip:** The string is doing the teaching. Measuring with an object instead of a number is where measurement starts for her, and she will use string on other things for weeks after this.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- Make him a raincoat out of a plastic bag and a rubber band.
- Draw the route of the morning tour on one long strip of paper, stop by stop.
- Set up the box bed as a hospital and let her decide what is wrong with him.
- Sort the recycling boxes into a row by size, biggest to smallest, and leave the row up.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** Give him his own box, the biggest one, laid on its
> side on the floor. At 9 months he will pull up on the edge of it, drop things
> in, and lean over to find them again, which is the same investigation Azlyn is
> running with the string. Keep the string, the crayons and the masking tape
> rolls off the floor whenever you are not looking at him. His pincer grip is
> good enough now to pick up things you cannot see from standing height, and
> everything he picks up goes in his mouth.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Giving a tour is the best test of understanding there is.**
> Azlyn can tell you what a faucet is. Explaining one to somebody who has never
> seen it asks for something harder. She has to guess what the listener already
> knows, put the parts in an order, and notice the spot where her own
> explanation runs out. Researchers call it the protégé effect, and it shows up
> whenever children teach younger children: the tutor gains more than the
> student does. A soft toy works because he never gets bored and never argues.
> Listen for the moment she stops and says *wait*. That pause is her finding a
> hole in her own model, and she will fill it herself if you stay quiet for
> four seconds.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Cold faucet only when she is on the step stool, and stand
> behind her the whole time she is up there. Kreston is pulling to stand today,
> so the stair gate goes across before the tour starts and the stool goes away
> the second she steps off it. Never leave him alone in a room with the string,
> the tape rolls or the crayons.

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 2 — Warm Water and a Gray Sky

Rain since before it got light, and the yard is a swamp. Everything that lives
by the back door is filthy. Today the house does not go out to the mud, so the
mud comes in and gets washed off at the sink.

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Put two dishpans, an old towel, a nail brush and an old toothbrush beside the sink, and set the bag of clothespins and a marker beside them for the afternoon. Stand the muddy boots on newspaper in the middle of the kitchen floor where she cannot miss them.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: The Grubby Parade
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: Dirty End, Clean End (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Ten Trips to the Drying Rack (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: Clipped Up in Size Order (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: The Washing Bowls Back Under the Sink
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: The Cave Under the Kitchen Table
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: The Long Bath, No Hurry
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: The Grubby Parade

**🧰 You need:** 1 laundry basket · 1 old towel spread on the kitchen floor.

1. Hand her the basket and one instruction: find everything in this house with mud on it. Boots, the stroller wheels, the plastic animals that got left out, the trowel, the doormat, the ball.
2. Lay it all out on the towel in a line, dirtiest at one end. She will hit two things that look about the same, and sorting that out is the work. Let the argument run and do not settle it for her.
3. Count the line together. Tell her the number, and tell her every one of them is getting washed at 9:15.

### 🎨 The Main Event: Dirty End, Clean End

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 2 dishpans or large mixing bowls
- Warm water, plus a squirt of dish soap in one bowl
- 1 nail brush and 1 old toothbrush
- 1 old towel spread on the floor
- 1 dish towel for drying
- 1 step stool
- An apron, or clothes you have given up on

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Apron on her first, or the clothes you have given up on. Set the two bowls at one end of the towel, soapy first and rinse second. Keep the far end of the towel empty and tell her that end is the clean end.
2. Run one boot through the whole route while she watches: into the soap, scrub, into the rinse, out onto the clean end. Then hand her the nail brush and stop demonstrating.
3. Work down her line in the order she set this morning. Pass her one thing at a time so the floor stays walkable. The old toothbrush is for the boot treads and the stroller wheels, where the nail brush cannot reach.
4. When the soapy water goes brown, carry it to the sink together. She climbs the step stool and pours it out herself. Refill it and let her look at how much of the yard came off six objects.
5. At the end, ask which one turned out to be dirtiest and whether her line this morning had it right. Move it if she wants to.
6. Everything on the clean end gets patted with the dish towel by her and carried to the drying rack, which is the next hour.

> 💡 **Tip:** Warm water, not hot. Her hands are in it for forty minutes and a three-year-old will keep going long past the point where her skin has had enough.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Ten Trips to the Drying Rack

Wet things are heavy, and heavy is what her body wants on a day stuck indoors.
Stand the drying rack at the far end of the house from the kitchen and make her
walk every item down there one at a time. Count the trips out loud. Ten trips
is the target and she will want twenty. Load her up on the last few, one boot
in each hand, and let her feel the difference between carrying a wet washcloth
and carrying a wet boot. Push the pace with a countdown if she flags. Heavy
work through the arms and legs settles a wound-up child better than any sitting
activity you could offer instead, and today she has nowhere else to spend it.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 9 months and hauling himself upright on
> whatever is nearest, which makes a drying rack the most dangerous object in
> the house today. Stand it against a wall, weight the base with a bag of flour,
> and keep him on the other side of a gate while it is loaded. Give him his own
> job on the floor near the towel: a shallow tray with an inch of warm water and
> two plastic lids. Stay within arm's reach of him the entire time that tray is
> out.

<div style="page-break-after: always;"></div>

### 🎨 Second Main Event: Clipped Up in Size Order

Morning was warm and wet and loud. This afternoon is a line, twenty clothespins
and a problem to solve.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 drying rack, or a length of string tied between two chair backs
- 20 clothespins, or binder clips and hair clips if the bag of pins runs short
- A basket of small clean laundry: washcloths, dish towels, socks, pillowcases
- The boots and plastic animals she washed this morning
- 1 marker

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Tie the line at her shoulder height so she can reach every inch of it without help. String between two chairs beats a rack for this.
2. Tip the basket on the floor and sort the pile into a size order first, smallest at one end, before anything goes up.
3. Clip them in that order, two clothespins each, one at each corner. Her hands will tire out around item six, so take the tight pins and leave her the loose ones.
4. Stand back with her and look down the finished line. Ask her to find the middle one, and let her put a marker dot on that clothespin so the middle stays findable.
5. Take three off while she covers her eyes, then hand them back and let her find the right gaps.
6. Give her the rest of the pile, the morning's boots and plastic animals included, and no instructions at all. She will invent an order of her own, and hers is worth more than yours.
7. Leave the line up overnight. Laundry in a March house takes until morning, and she gets to check it before breakfast.

> 💡 **Tip:** Clothespins are hard work for three-year-old fingers, and that is the reason to use them. Opening one takes the same pinch she will need for a pencil at five.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- Fold the dry washcloths corner to corner and stack them into a tower.
- Wash the plastic animals a second time in a bowl of bubbles, for the bubbles.
- Line the clean boots up by the door in size order, every pair in the house.
- Tape a sheet of paper to the window and trace the rain tracks down it with a crayon.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** Clothespins pinch and they fit in a mouth, so he
> does not get any. Give him a laundry basket half full of dry washcloths
> instead, and sit him beside it. At 9 months he pulls things out one at a time
> with a thumb and finger and drops them over the edge, and he will run that
> loop for twenty minutes. Empty every bowl of water the moment you step away
> from it. A baby who has learned to pull up can get his face into standing
> water in an inch of it.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Water is the most reliable regulator you have, and it works in either direction.**
> A wound-up child and a flat one need opposite things, and warm and cold water
> hand you both. Warm water on her forearms slows her down. The temperature sits
> close to her skin, the resistance is steady, and there is nothing in it to
> win. Twenty minutes at a bowl on a stool buys more calm than any instruction
> you could give her. Cold runs the other way. A cool washcloth on the back of
> the neck, or her hands under the cold faucet for a count of ten, brings back a
> child who has gone floppy and whiny at four in the afternoon. Neither one
> costs anything, and both are available on a day the door stays shut.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Warm water only, tested on the inside of your own wrist first,
> and set the faucet to cold before she climbs the stool. Kreston can drown in
> the inch of water left in a dishpan, so every bowl gets emptied the moment you
> leave the room, with no exceptions for a quick errand. Weight or block the
> drying rack before he pulls up on it. Keep the clothespins in the basket and
> off the floor.

## 🤒 Sick Day 1 — The Day It Peaks

She is hot, streaming, and has no interest in anything you can offer. There is
no schedule on this page and no clock times, because she will set both. Nobody
goes outside today. The rest block below is the day; everything else is
optional and can be dropped halfway through with no harm done.

### 🌙 Before She Wakes
> Make her spot on the sofa now: two pillows, the blanket she likes, a towel over the cushion under her head, a cup with a lid within her reach, and a bowl on the floor beside her in case. Put the tissues where she can get them herself. Fill a paper bag with ten things from around the house, none of them small enough to choke Kreston, and leave it behind the sofa, out of her sight until you want it.

### 🕰️ The Shape of the Day

- **Whenever she wakes.** Carry her to the sofa spot. No breakfast question yet. The cup, and nothing else.
- **After the first long sleep.** Fluids, one small thing from the bag, back down.
- **When she asks for food.** Take whatever she asks for and give it to her on the sofa.
- **The middle of the day.** The rest block. Curtains half shut, lights off, you on the sofa with her.
- **Before the afternoon dip.** A story with her eyes closed, or a movie, or nothing.
- **Early evening.** Bath if she wants it, bed an hour before you think.

### 🛋️ Where She Lands

Pick one spot in the house and keep her in it all day. Moving her from bed to
sofa to bed costs her more than it saves you, and a child who has one nest
knows where her cup is, where the bowl is and where you are. Keep it near where
you have to be. If the sofa is out of earshot of the kitchen, the day gets
harder for you by the hour.

Take the pressure off yourself early. A day of screens on a fever day has never
harmed a child and it buys you the hands to look after her. Put something long
and slow on, not something loud.

### 😴 The Rest Block

The middle of the day belongs to sleep, and it is the only thing on this page
that matters. She may nap for two hours, or she may lie still with her eyes
open for forty minutes, and both count. Draw the curtains halfway, kill the
overhead light, and lie down with her rather than leaving her to it. A hot
three-year-old settles faster with a hand on her back than with a door closed.

Guard it. Silence your phone, park Kreston somewhere safe first, and let the
laundry sit. Everything you skip today gets done tomorrow when she is a
notch better.

### 🤲 Three Things That Ask Nothing of Her

**The Bag You Bring to Her.** The bag you filled last night comes out from
behind the sofa: a wooden spoon, a hairbrush, a bangle, a postcard, a photo, a
tape measure. Hand her one at a time and say nothing about it. She looks,
keeps it on the blanket or gives it back. No questions and no quiz. This runs
for as long as it runs and stops the second she turns her head away.

**Drawing on the Back of Her Hand.** Washable marker, her hand flat on the
blanket, and you draw. A cat, a boat, a sun, her name. She does nothing at all
except hold still and watch, and the light pressure on her skin does more for
her than the picture does. Ask before each one and stop when she has had
enough.

**The Story She Can Close Her Eyes For.** Tell her a story about her own day,
starting from waking up, and put everything ordinary in it. She woke up hot,
she came to the sofa, the blanket was blue. Slow down as you go and add nothing
exciting. She can keep her eyes shut for the whole of it, and half the time it
turns into the rest block on its own.

### 🥣 What She'll Take

Fluids beat food today and it is not close. Water, watered-down juice, milk if
she keeps it down, ice chips if she will not drink. Offer a small amount often
rather than a cup she has to face. A three-year-old who eats nothing for a day
and drinks well is fine. One who will not drink is the one to watch.

Food, if she asks: dry toast, plain crackers, banana, yogurt, cold fruit. Do
not make anything that needs cooking or that you will mind throwing away.

### 👶 Where Kreston Goes

He cannot be on that sofa today. At 9 months he pulls up on the cushions, goes
for her face and her cup, and gets everything she has within a day or two of
touching it. Set his floor up in the same room but out of reach, gated or
across the rug, so she can see him and he cannot get to her. His waving is new
and reliable now, so let them wave at each other across the gap. That counts as
contact for both of them.

Get him out of the house for a stretch if there is a second adult, or a
grandparent who will take a walk in the wind with the stroller. An hour with
one child is a different day from an hour with two. Wash your hands between
them, every time, and keep her cup and his separate.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Naming the darkest day is better than enduring it.**
> A cold runs to a shape. Symptoms climb for two or three days, top out, then
> fade across the week that follows, so the darkest day of it sits somewhere
> you can point at. You already treat today as that day. Saying it out loud is
> what changes the hours. Tell yourself this is the top, tell her tomorrow
> will be easier, and the day stops running open-ended for either of you. A
> bad stretch with an edge on it is easier to sit inside, for her as much as
> for you. Cancel what the week had in it, plan nothing, and guard your own
> sleep, because it is holding the rest of this up.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** The bag is the hazard on this page. Nothing goes in it small
> enough to choke Kreston, because everything that lands on her blanket
> reaches the floor by evening. Count the ten back into the bag before he is
> down, and sweep the blanket and the carpet with your hand first. Call your
> doctor the same day for a fever in a child this age that passes 104°F, or
> any fever lasting more than three days. Go now, not tomorrow, for fast or
> labored breathing, a stiff neck, a rash that stays visible when you press a
> glass against it, a child too drowsy to wake properly, or no wet diaper or
> bathroom trip in eight hours. Never give aspirin to a child. Follow the
> dosing on the bottle by weight and write down the time of every dose,
> because you will not remember it by evening. Ice goes in only while she sits
> up, crushed small under the back of a spoon, and whole cubes stay in the
> freezer, because a cube slides back on a flat child and blocks her airway.

## 🤒 Sick Day 2 — Better, But Not Back

The fever broke overnight, she is bored by nine, and she is nowhere near ready
for a real day. Still no going out and still no clock times. Today has a rest
block in the middle of it, same as yesterday, and everything around it is a
shrunken version of a normal morning.

### 🌙 Before She Wakes
> Move her nest off the sofa and onto the floor, on a blanket with cushions, so she is at her own level and near her things. Fill the cup. Put a stack of catalogs and junk mail and a fat crayon beside it.

### 🕰️ The Shape of the Day

- **Whenever she wakes.** Dressed in clothes, not pajamas. It changes how she behaves all day.
- **After breakfast.** The smallest version of the morning, below. Ten minutes of each thing.
- **When she flags.** Stop before she asks you to. She will not ask.
- **The middle of the day.** The rest block, shorter than yesterday and still not skipped.
- **When she asks for food.** Lunch on the blanket, where she already is.
- **Late afternoon.** One quiet job with a visible end, then nothing.
- **Early evening.** Bed at the same time as yesterday, not the usual time.

### 🧺 The Smallest Version of the Morning

Give her the shape of a normal day at a tenth of the size. Ten minutes of
building, ten minutes of drawing, ten minutes of a song, and a sit-down in
between each one. The order matters more than the length, because the order is
what she has been missing since she went down. Announce each thing before you
start it and announce the end of it: *building now, then we stop.* Stopping on
time is the whole skill today. A recovering three-year-old will overshoot,
crash at two in the afternoon, and lose tomorrow to it.

Keep every one of them on the floor blanket. She stays where she is and the
activity comes to her.

### 😴 The Rest Block

Shorter today, and non-negotiable. Curtains half shut, a blanket, and you
beside her for the first ten minutes. She may not sleep, and lying down without
sleeping is still doing the work. Set an end to it that she can hear, a story
finishing or a timer, so she is not lying there wondering how long this lasts.

Bring her out of it slow. A drink first, then one small thing, then the
afternoon.

### 🛋️ Three Things She Can Do From a Blanket

**Circling What She'd Buy.** Hand her the catalogs and a fat crayon. Her job is
to circle everything in there she would have if she could. No cutting, no
sticking, no tidying up after. Come back every few minutes and ask her to show
you one thing she circled and why. She will talk more today than she has since
Monday, and talking is the sign she is on the mend.

**The Drawer She Empties and Refills.** Bring one drawer to her: the silverware
tray, the sock drawer, the drawer of chargers and rubber bands. She tips it out
on the blanket, sorts it however she likes, and puts it back. A job that
finishes where she can see it suits her right now, and it takes nothing out of
her. The silverware and the charger drawer come out only while Kreston is
gated or asleep. If he is on the floor with her, she gets the socks.

**Lunch Where She Is Sitting.** Sandwich cut in triangles, eaten on the blanket
with a dish towel under it for a plate. Sit down and eat yours beside her,
because a meal she has to travel to is a meal she will turn down today. Keep it
small; her appetite comes back a few days behind everything else, and a full
plate reads as a demand.

### 👶 Where Kreston Goes

He can come back in today, on the floor, at her level. Keep the cups apart and
keep him off her face for another day or two, and beyond that let them be near
each other. She has been away from him and he has noticed.

Give him a task in her sight line: a laundry basket he can pull up on, or a
plastic bowl with three lids in it for his thumb and finger to work at. At 9
months he will offer things to her and then take them straight back, and she
has enough left in her today to find that funny. Watch that he does not haul
himself up on her blanket and land on her, because he will try it within the
first ten minutes.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **The build-up is more than she can hold, and the crash afterwards is normal.**
> Her first good morning in days arrives with an appetite for all of it, and
> she has no way to meter that. Her temperature came back to normal ahead of
> her sleep, her appetite and her patience, so the reserve she spends this
> morning is thinner than it feels to her. The two o'clock crash is the normal
> end of a morning she enjoyed. You already stop each thing before she asks,
> and that keeps the crash small enough to sleep off. Take the tears at two as
> the receipt for a good morning, hold the rest block where it is, and put her
> down at yesterday's bedtime rather than the usual one.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** The sorting drawer is the hazard on this page. Forks, knives,
> chargers, rubber bands and coins reach the floor the moment she tips the
> tray, and Kreston is back on that floor today with a pincer grip and a mouth.
> Hand her the silverware or the charger drawer only while he is gated or
> asleep, give her the sock drawer any time he is beside her, and sweep the
> blanket with your hand before you let him near it. Call your doctor if the
> fever comes back after a clear day, if a cough gets worse after several days
> of getting better, or if she starts pulling at an ear, because each of those
> sits on top of the first virus. Preschool, the pool and anybody else's
> kitchen wait until she has gone a full day with no fever and no vomiting and
> has eaten a real meal. Keep her cup and her spoon apart from Kreston's for
> the rest of the week and wash your hands between the two of them.

## 🎲 March — Extra Ideas

Ten minutes, no plan, and a child already asking. Everything on this page uses
what is in the house or on the walk you were taking anyway.


> ⚠️ These run under the same rules as the rest of the month: an adult within
> arm's reach of any water, small parts up off the floor while Kreston is on
> it, and anything taped across a doorway down and in the trash before he
> crawls through.
### 🎲 Alternative Activities
- **The Deepest Puddle on the Street.** She stands a stick in each one and you rank them shallow, deeper, deepest.
- **Under the Umbrella, Listening.** She sits under an open umbrella in the rain with her eyes shut and names what she hears.
- **What the Wind Takes First.** Hold up a ribbon, a leaf and a paper towel off the back step and see which one goes.
- **Boot Prints Down the Hall.** A roll of paper down the hallway and one walk across it in wet boots.
- **The Warmest Spot in the House.** She hunts for it with the back of her hand and marks the winner with a sticky note.
- **Ten Things That Rattle.** She shakes her way around the kitchen cabinets and lines up the loudest five.
- **The First Warm Step.** On the first mild afternoon, shoes off on the back step for two minutes and nothing else.
- **Counting the Buds on One Branch.** Same branch every few days, and the number climbs all month.
- **The Race Between Two Raindrops.** Pick two on the window and call the winner at the sill.
- **Everything That Fits in One Boot.** She loads it, you both count what came out.
- **The Mud Footprint Guessing Game.** You press a print in the mud with a shoe and she works out whose it is.

### 🎨 Alternative Arts & Crafts
- **Rain-Spotted Paper.** Chalk dust or marker dots on paper, held out in the rain for five seconds and brought straight back in.
- **Three Jars of Mud Paint.** Dirt and water at three thicknesses, painted onto a flattened box with a stick.
- **The Wind Spinner Off a Cereal Box.** A spiral cut from card, hung on thread by the window that always leaks air.
- **Gray Day Paper Chain.** One strip for every gray day this month, in every gray she can find in the crayon box.
- **Tissue Paper on a Wet Window.** Colored tissue squares pressed to damp glass, backlit whenever the sun does show up.
- **The Egg Carton Bulb Tray.** Cups torn off, painted, upended and pushed into a tray as a row of crocuses.
- **The Puddle Painted From Memory.** Back from the walk, she paints the biggest one she saw, before she looks at a photo.
- **A Kite That Only Works Indoors.** Ribbons taped to a stick and run down the hall at full speed.
- **The Sock Puppet With a Cold.** One sock, a marker, a scrap of tissue, and a voice she has heard all week.
- **Stamping With the Bottom of a Boot.** Paint on the sole, one press per sheet, a whole line of them along the hall.
- **The Weather Wheel for the Fridge.** A paper circle in quarters, a card arrow, and one turn every morning.

### 👶 Kreston's Turn
- **The Cruising Rail.** Two chairs pushed until their edges overlap, so he can sidestep the whole length with a hand on each.
- **The Muffin Tin and Six Lids.** One plastic lid dropped into every cup, taken out again, and dropped back in with a thumb and finger.
- **Puffs on the Tray.** A handful of cereal puffs spread wide, worked at with thumb and finger, all through her main event.
- **Waving From the Window.** Whoever leaves the house waves from the path and waits there until he waves back.

## 🌟 Day 316: The Weather Station 🌟
**📅 Friday, November 12, 2027**
**Theme:** Weather & Rainbows

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting: What's It Doing Out There? (15 min)
- **9:15–10:30 AM** — Main Event: Build the Weather Station (75 min)
- **10:30–10:45 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:45–11:45 AM** — Get Outside: **Siting the Station** (60 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (before lunch)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep
- **12:30–12:45 PM** — 📚 Book Time (before nap)

### 🎨 The Main Event: Build the Weather Station
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- A clear plastic bottle, for the rain gauge
- Scissors — **adult cuts**
- A permanent marker and a ruler
- Small stones
- A plastic bag or light fabric, a stick, string, and ribbon — for the windsock
- An outdoor thermometer, if you have one
- A big sheet of paper, and a clipboard
- Markers

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Rain gauge first.** Adult cuts the top third off the bottle and inverts it into the base as a funnel. **Stones in the bottom for weight**, then pour water up to the top of the stones and call that the zero line.
2. **She marks the scale.** A line every centimetre up the side with the ruler, numbered. **The numbers won't be neat and that's fine.**
3. **Windsock:** a tube of bag or fabric, ribbons taped round one end, string at the other, tied to a cane. **Test it by running down the hall with it.**
4. **The chart is the important bit.** Six columns down the left — **Rain, Wind, Cloud, Cold, and a face for how it felt** — and the days across the top. Pin it somewhere she walks past.
5. **Agree the reading time**: straight after breakfast, every day, no exceptions.
6. **Take the first reading today**, together, slowly, so the routine is set properly.
7. **Say the rule out loud:** we write down what it *is*, not what we want it to be.

> 💡 **Tip:** Put the gauge somewhere open and away from the house wall or a tree, and mark the spot so it goes back in the same place. A gauge that moves gives numbers that mean nothing, and she'll spot the inconsistency before you do.

### 🌳 Get Outside: Siting the Station
Walk the garden or yard looking for the right spot — **open, flat, not under anything.** Test the windsock in three different places and find where the wind actually is. That hunt is a real piece of experimental design and she can do all of it.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston gets a ribbon on a stick of his own. Running with a streamer at 17 months is both a wind experiment and forty minutes of exercise.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **A measurement she takes every day teaches more than a fact does.**
> Told that it rains more in November, she learns a sentence. Reading her own gauge every morning for a week, she builds something better: an expectation, and then the experience of it being confirmed or broken.
> Daily measurement is also the cheapest way to teach that data is something you *collect* rather than something you're given. The routine matters more than the accuracy — same time, same place, written down whether or not anything interesting happened. Empty days are data too, and a chart with three zeros on it teaches something a chart with only exciting entries can't.

## 🌟 Day 317: Making Rainbows 🌟
**📅 Saturday, November 13, 2027**
**Theme:** Weather & Rainbows

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **9:00–9:10 AM** — **The daily reading** (10 min)
- **9:10–9:20 AM** — Morning Meeting (10 min)
- **9:20–10:30 AM** — Main Event: Making Rainbows (70 min)
- **10:30–10:45 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:45–11:45 AM** — Get Outside: **The Rainbow Hunt** (60 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (before lunch)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep
- **12:30–12:45 PM** — 📚 Book Time (before nap)

### 🎨 The Main Event: Making Rainbows
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- A prism, if you have one
- A clear glass of water and a small mirror
- An old CD or DVD
- A strong torch
- A spray bottle of water
- White paper and card
- A dark room, and a sunny window
- Crayons or paint

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **The CD first**, because it always works. Tilt it under a torch or a lamp and the colours run across it. **Let her tilt it herself and find the angle.**
2. **Then the glass and mirror.** Mirror propped at an angle inside a glass of water, torch or sunlight aimed at the submerged part, white card held to catch what bounces out. **Move the card around until the band of colour lands on it.**
3. **This takes fiddling and that's the activity** — she should be the one moving things until it appears.
4. **Then make it vanish.** Block the light, move the card, tip the mirror. **Appearing and disappearing on demand is what turns it from magic into something with a cause.**
5. **Name the order** while it's on the card: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple. **Always the same order, every time** — check it on the CD too.
6. **The spray bottle version:** if there's any sun at all, stand with your back to it and spray a fine mist in front of you. A real rainbow, made by her, in the garden.
7. **Paint one** in the right order from the card, not from memory.

> 💡 **Tip:** November sun is low and weak, which is annoying for the spray-bottle version and perfect for the window one — a low sun through a glass of water on a windowsill throws colours right across a room.

### 🌳 Get Outside: The Rainbow Hunt
Hunt for colours split by water and light: **an oily puddle on a road**, a wet windscreen, a spider's web with drops on it, a soap bubble. **Puddle rainbows are the easiest to find in November** and she'll never stop spotting them once she's seen one.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston gets a CD of his own to tilt and a spot on the floor where a rainbow lands. At 17–18 months he'll try to pick the colours up off the carpet, which is the correct response.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **A rainbow she can make and unmake stops being magic and starts being light.**
> A rainbow in the sky is a one-off event with no visible cause. It's genuinely reasonable for a three-year-old to file it under magic, along with everything else that happens without explanation.
> Control is what moves it out of that category. If she can make one appear by tilting a mirror and make it vanish by moving her hand, then it has a cause, and the cause is something she's holding. She won't understand refraction and doesn't need to — what she gets is the far more important habit of assuming that impressive things have causes that can be found, which is the difference between wonder and superstition.

> 💛 **From Joseph:** She spent ten minutes making it come and go, come and go, checking it worked the same way every time.

## 🌟 Day 318: Clouds & Rain 🌟
**📅 Sunday, November 14, 2027**
**Theme:** Weather & Rainbows

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **9:00–9:10 AM** — **The daily reading** (10 min)
- **9:10–9:20 AM** — Morning Meeting (10 min)
- **9:20–10:30 AM** — Main Event: Clouds & Rain (70 min)
- **10:30–10:45 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:45–11:45 AM** — Get Outside: **The Cloud Watch** (60 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (before lunch)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep
- **12:30–12:45 PM** — 📚 Book Time (before nap)

### 🎨 The Main Event: Clouds & Rain
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- Cotton wool balls, a bag of them
- Blue card, and glue
- A clear jar or tall glass
- Shaving foam
- Food colouring, blue or any dark colour
- A pipette or teaspoon
- A jug of water
- A cloth, because this one drips

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Three clouds, three names**, made from cotton wool on blue card:
   - **Cumulus** — pulled into fat lumps. The cauliflower ones.
   - **Stratus** — stretched flat into a grey sheet across the whole card. November's cloud.
   - **Cirrus** — pulled out thin and wispy, high up.
2. **Label each one** and put it on the wall by the weather chart. **From today, the Cloud row on the chart gets a name, not a scribble.**
3. **Then the rain cloud.** Fill the jar three-quarters with water. **Shaving foam on top** as a thick cloud layer.
4. **Drop coloured water onto the foam, a few drops at a time**, and wait.
5. **Nothing happens for a while.** Then the cloud gets too heavy to hold it and the colour comes streaking down through the water. **The waiting is the point** — clouds hold rain until they can't.
6. **Do it again with more foam** and see if it takes longer. It does.
7. Draw what happened in three pictures: cloud, cloud filling, rain.

> 💡 **Tip:** Add the colour slowly, drop by drop. Squeezing half the bottle in produces an instant mess and skips the only interesting part, which is the delay.

### 🌳 Get Outside: The Cloud Watch
Lie on a coat on the ground and look up for ten minutes. **Name the type first, then find shapes in them.** In November it's usually stratus — one flat grey lid over everything — and noticing that today's sky is *all the same cloud* is as good an observation as spotting a dragon.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston gets a tray of shaving foam to squash on the high chair table. It's clean, it smells odd, and it'll hold him for twenty minutes. **Keep it out of his mouth.**

> 🌍 **Around the World — Rain, Everywhere**
> In much of **India, Bangladesh and Pakistan**, the **monsoon** arrives in June and the whole year turns on it — the crops, the heat breaking, the rivers filling. It's genuinely celebrated: there are monsoon songs, monsoon poems, and monsoon food. **Hot fried *pakoras* eaten while it buckets down**, with sweet milky *chai*, is a tradition rather than a coincidence.
> **✨ Try it:** Make pakoras — gram or plain flour, water, salt, a pinch of chilli or turmeric, whatever vegetable you've got sliced thin, **fried by an adult**. Eat them at the window while it rains, with something hot to drink.
> *Did you know?* Some places in north-east India get more rain in a year than most of Europe gets in five, and people there have made bridges by training the roots of living fig trees to grow across rivers.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Naming clouds is the first time the sky has parts.**
> Until something is named, it isn't really separable. The sky is just *the sky* — a single background thing that's blue or grey. Three names change that: now there are objects up there, of different kinds, that come and go and can be identified.
> This is the general effect that makes field guides so useful with young children, and it works on almost anything — trees, birds, rocks, clouds. Naming isn't a substitute for understanding, it's the thing that makes understanding possible, because you can't notice a pattern in a category you don't have. Three cloud names is plenty. She'll use them for the rest of the week, and probably the rest of the winter.

## 🌟 Day 319: The Wind Scale 🌟
**📅 Monday, November 15, 2027**
**Theme:** Weather & Rainbows

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **9:00–9:10 AM** — **The daily reading** (10 min)
- **9:10–9:20 AM** — Morning Meeting (10 min)
- **9:20–10:15 AM** — Main Event: Make the Wind Scale (55 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:45 AM** — Get Outside: **Rating the Wind** (75 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (before lunch)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep
- **12:30–12:45 PM** — 📚 Book Time (before nap)

### 🎨 The Main Event: Make the Wind Scale
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- Seven pieces of card, numbered 0 to 6
- Markers and crayons
- A hole punch and a ring or string, to make it into a flip-book
- The windsock from Day 316
- Ribbon, tissue paper, and a feather
- A clipboard

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Explain the idea once:** you can't measure wind with a ruler, **so people invented a scale where you look at what the wind is doing to things.** That's a real thing sailors use.
2. **Make seven cards together**, and she draws each one:
   - **0 — Still.** Smoke goes straight up. The feather doesn't move.
   - **1 — A breath.** Leaves rustle, the ribbon lifts.
   - **2 — Breezy.** Small branches move, the windsock fills.
   - **3 — Windy.** Coats flap, hair goes in your face.
   - **4 — Strong.** Big branches move, umbrellas are difficult.
   - **5 — Very strong.** Whole trees swaying, hard to walk into.
   - **6 — Wild.** Bins over, twigs coming down. **Stay in.**
3. **Punch and ring them into a flip-book** she can carry outside.
4. **Test each level indoors** where you can: still air, a breath, a fan, a hairdryer on cold. **Match the card to what she sees the ribbon do.**
5. **Then take it out and rate today.**
6. **Rate it in three different places** — open ground, behind a wall, between two buildings. **Different numbers in the same garden is the surprise.**
7. **The number goes on the weather chart** in the Wind row.

> 💡 **Tip:** Ask her to justify the number — *how do you know it's a three?* — and accept whatever evidence she gives. Naming the evidence is the skill, not getting the number right.

### 🌳 Get Outside: Rating the Wind
Seventy-five minutes with the flip-book, rating everywhere: the garden, the street, the top of a hill if you've got one, a doorway, a bus shelter. **Then run with the windsock** and find out what number *running* counts as.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is around 18 months and will chase a ribbon anywhere. Tie one to his pushchair or his wrist and let him be the instrument.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **A scale with pictures lets her rate something she can't measure.**
> Wind has no units she can access — a rain gauge she can read, a thermometer she can look at, but wind speed is invisible. A picture scale solves that by turning the measurement into a matching task: which of these seven pictures looks like what's happening?
> That's a genuinely useful format and it's worth borrowing beyond the weather. Pain scales, tiredness scales, and how-cross-am-I scales all work the same way, and they let a child report something she otherwise can't put a size on. A child who can point at a picture and say *it's about a four* has a way to communicate an intensity, which is a real gain for a three-year-old who mostly has *fine* and *terrible*.

> 💛 **From Joseph:** Behind the shed was a one and the top of the field was a five, and she was outraged that it could be both at once.

## 🌟 Day 320: Frost & Ice 🌟
**📅 Tuesday, November 16, 2027**
**Theme:** Weather & Rainbows

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **7:45–8:15 AM** — **The Frost Hunt, early** — before it melts (30 min)
- **9:00–9:10 AM** — **The daily reading** (10 min)
- **9:10–10:30 AM** — Main Event: Ice Suncatchers & the Salt Test (80 min)
- **10:30–10:45 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:45–11:45 AM** — Get Outside: **Hanging the Suncatchers** (60 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (before lunch)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep
- **12:30–12:45 PM** — 📚 Book Time (before nap)

### 🎨 The Main Event: Ice Suncatchers & the Salt Test
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- Shallow dishes, cake tins, or plastic lids
- String, cut into loops
- Leaves, berries, petals, seed heads, and small twigs
- A jug of water
- A tray of ice cubes, made the night before
- Salt, and a small spoon
- A magnifying glass
- A dark plate, chilled in the freezer

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Do the frost hunt first thing** if there's been one — before breakfast, before it melts. **Look at frost on a leaf through the magnifying glass.** It has shapes and edges and she will not have known that.
2. **The suncatchers.** Lay leaves, berries and petals in the shallow dishes, arranged how she likes. **A loop of string with both ends in the water**, hanging over the edge.
3. **Water to about a centimetre deep.** Not more, or it takes all day and cracks when you turn it out.
4. **Outside overnight if it's cold enough, or the freezer if it isn't.**
5. **Then the salt test, with yesterday's ice cubes.** Two cubes on a plate. **Salt on one, nothing on the other.** Watch for ten minutes.
6. **The salted one melts faster and carves channels into itself.** Look at it with the magnifying glass. **That's why salt goes on the roads**, and she'll spot the gritter next time she sees one.
7. **Chilled dark plate:** breathe on it and watch the fog appear and vanish. **Then breathe on the cold window** — the same thing, made of her.

> ⚠️ **Ice straight from the freezer sticks to skin.** Handle with a cloth for the first minute, and keep the salt out of eyes and off cracked hands.

> 💡 **Tip:** Set the suncatchers up the evening before if you can, so they come out of the freezer or off the doorstep first thing and the day starts with the good bit.

### 🌳 Get Outside: Hanging the Suncatchers
Turn them out — **a splash of warm water on the underside of the dish releases them** — and hang them from a branch, a fence, or a washing line. **Then check them every hour.** They'll be gone by the afternoon if it's mild, and watching them go is part of it.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston gets one ice cube in a bowl on his tray, watched. Cold is a strong sensation at 18 months and he'll be fascinated and then annoyed by it, in about that order.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Cold is easier to investigate than hot, because she can hold it.**
> Heat is off-limits to a three-year-old — every hot thing in her life comes with a warning and a distance. Cold is the same physics from the other end, and she can pick it up, watch it change, and hold it until her fingers complain.
> That makes ice the best material available for meeting states of matter, melting, and the fact that temperature does things. She can run the whole experiment herself: put it there, watch it go, put salt on this one and not that one. Everything she learns about melting transfers straight to the hot version she isn't allowed to touch, and she'll have got it entirely from things she was allowed to handle.

> 💛 **From Joseph:** She hung one on the gate and checked it eleven times before lunch. It was a puddle by two and she took it personally.

## 🌟 Day 321: Where Does the Puddle Go? 🌟
**📅 Wednesday, November 17, 2027**
**Theme:** Weather & Rainbows

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **9:00–9:10 AM** — **The daily reading** (10 min)
- **9:10–9:20 AM** — Morning Meeting (10 min)
- **9:20–10:20 AM** — Main Event: The Water Cycle Bag (60 min)
- **10:20–10:35 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:35–11:45 AM** — Get Outside: **The Disappearing Puddle** (70 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (before lunch)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep
- **12:30–12:45 PM** — 📚 Book Time (before nap)

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Water Cycle Bag
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- A zip-lock freezer bag
- Strong tape
- Blue food colouring
- A permanent marker
- A jug of water
- A sunny window — or the warmest one you've got
- Two saucers, for the evaporation test
- Chalk, for outside

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **She draws on the bag first** — a sun in the corner, a cloud at the top, waves at the bottom, and an arrow going round.
2. **A few centimetres of blue water in the bottom**, zipped tight, and **taped flat to the sunniest window** you have.
3. **Mark the water line on the bag with the marker.** That's the measurement.
4. **Then the saucer test, to run alongside it:** two saucers with the same amount of water. **One somewhere warm, one somewhere cold.** Mark both.
5. **Ask where she thinks the water will go.** Write her answer down exactly. Most children say it goes *into* the plate, or that it's *gone*.
6. **Check the bag after a couple of hours.** There'll be droplets up the sides above the water line. **That's the water, out of the puddle and back on the wall of the bag.**
7. **Check both saucers before bed and again tomorrow.** The warm one will be further along, and the bag will have proper drips running back down — **water going up and coming back, in something she can hold.**

> 💡 **Tip:** Write her prediction down at step 5 and read it back to her on Thursday. Comparing what she thought to what happened is the whole scientific method in one sentence, and it needs a record to work.

### 🌳 Get Outside: The Disappearing Puddle
Find a puddle on a hard surface and **chalk right round its edge.** Come back in an hour and chalk round it again. And again after lunch. **The rings show it shrinking**, and it's the same question as the bag at full size. If it rains instead, the rings will grow, which is just as good.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston gets a paintbrush and a pot of water to paint the path with, like Day 268. He's 18 months; watching his own marks dry is a genuine activity.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **She thinks water that dries has gone, and it takes seeing it come back to fix that.**
> Ask a three-year-old where a puddle went and she'll say it disappeared, or sank in, or went away. Nothing in her experience suggests that a liquid can turn into something invisible and still exist — that's a genuinely counter-intuitive idea and telling her doesn't shift it.
> What shifts it is the bag, because the water leaves the bottom and reappears on the wall, inside a sealed thing that nothing could get in or out of. That's the whole argument, made physically. It's also her first encounter with the idea that stuff doesn't vanish, it moves and changes form — which is one of the biggest ideas in science and she's meeting it in a freezer bag taped to a window.

> 💛 **From Joseph:** "It climbed up." Not the word a scientist would use but not wrong either.

- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep
- **12:30–12:45 PM** — 📚 Book Time (before nap)

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Forecast
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The completed weather chart
- A large sheet of paper, for the map
- Card, scissors, and markers, for the symbols
- Sticky tack or tape
- A phone, to film it
- A pointer — a stick, a ruler, a wooden spoon
- Something smart to wear, if she wants

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Read the week first, together, off the chart.** Which day had the most rain? Which was the windiest? **How many days were stratus?** Point at the columns and count them.
2. **Ask the big one: what do you think tomorrow will be?** And then: **why?** Any reason counts as long as it's a reason.
3. **Draw the map** — a rough outline of your area with the house on it. It doesn't need to be accurate.
4. **Make the symbols:** sun, cloud, rain, wind arrow, snowflake, all on card with sticky tack on the back.
5. **She puts the symbols on the map** for her forecast, and rehearses once.
6. **Film it.** She presents — *tomorrow it will be…* — pointing at the map with the pointer, in the manner of every weather presenter she's ever seen.
7. **Then everyone else does one**, including Dad and including a deliberately silly one. **Write everybody's forecast on the back of the chart** so tomorrow can be checked.

> 💡 **Tip:** Actually check it tomorrow. A forecast nobody goes back to is just a performance — the checking is what makes it a prediction.

### 🌳 Get Outside: Checking the Forecast
Go out and look at the sky, the windsock, and the gauge one more time before she commits. **Then tomorrow, go out and see who was right.** Being wrong should be as loud and cheerful an event as being right.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston does a forecast too. Hand him a symbol, film him sticking it anywhere on the map, and treat it with complete seriousness.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Being wrong in a forecast is the most useful kind of wrong.**
> A prediction made out loud and then checked is a rare and valuable structure for a child, because the wrongness is impersonal. She didn't fail at anything; the weather did something else. Nobody marked it.
> That makes forecasting a very safe place to practise being wrong, which is a skill she'll need far more than she'll need to know about clouds. Children who learn that a wrong guess is interesting rather than shameful will keep guessing, and guessing out loud is how thinking gets visible enough to improve.

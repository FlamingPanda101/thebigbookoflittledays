# 🌧️ September Backup Days
## *For when the plan doesn't survive the morning*

**September's version of a bad day:** the weather turns without warning, the first proper rain arrives, the wind takes the garden apart, and it's suddenly dark at seven. And then, right on cue, the first cold of the season goes round the whole house. Two of these are for weather that's decided for you, and two are for the fortnight in late September when everybody is snuffly and nobody is sleeping well.

---

<a id="sep-weather-1"></a>

## 🌬️ Bad Weather Day 1: The Big Wind Day
**Use when:** It's blowing hard, the rain is horizontal, and outside is genuinely unpleasant rather than just wet.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting: Listen to the Wind (15 min)
- **9:15–10:20 AM** — Main Event: The Paper Aeroplane Factory (65 min)
- **10:20–10:35 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:35–11:20 AM** — Indoor Movement: The Wind Games (45 min)
- **11:20 AM–12:00 PM** — Quiet Play (40 min)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Paper Aeroplane Factory
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- A large stack of plain paper — more than you think, 30 sheets minimum
- Paperclips
- Masking tape, for the runway and the distance markers
- Markers and stickers
- A tape measure
- Paper for the results chart

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Tape a launch line** at one end of the corridor or the longest room, and tape markers along the floor at intervals. Number them 1 to 5.
2. Fold the first plane together. **One design, learned properly** — the simplest dart. Her folds will be uneven and it will still fly.
3. She decorates it and gives it a name. Named planes get flown far more often than unnamed ones.
4. **Then the testing.** Three throws each, and she calls out which marker it reached. Write it on the chart.
5. **Now change one thing.** A paperclip on the nose. Wings folded up at the tips. A heavier sheet of paper. **One change at a time**, then three more throws, then compare.
6. Keep going until you've a fleet of them and a chart with a clear winner.
7. **Ask her why the winner won.** Her theory will be inventive and possibly wrong. Write it down as it stands.

> 💡 **Tip:** Fold at least four planes yourself in advance. The folding is beyond her hands alone, and a supply of ready ones means the testing — which is the actual activity — never stalls.

### 🏃 Indoor Movement: The Wind Games
Everyone is the wind. Blow round the room, then be a gale, then a breeze, then stop dead. Blow a ping-pong ball or a scrunched paper ball across the floor with straws, racing on hands and knees. Blow a feather and keep it up.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston gets his own paper plane that he will immediately crush, and a stack of paper to scrunch. At 15–16 months, scrunching and throwing paper is a legitimate hour.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Testing the same thing twenty times is what a three-year-old means by an experiment.**
> Adults think of an experiment as a question with a designed answer. For her, it's repetition with small variations — throw it again, throw it harder, throw it with a paperclip on — and the understanding accumulates out of the pile of attempts rather than arriving as a conclusion.
> Which is why the useful move is to protect the repetition rather than hurry to the finding. Changing one thing at a time and saying so out loud gives the pile a shape, and that's all the method she needs at this age. The theory she offers at the end will be wrong. The habit of changing one thing and watching what happens is the part that isn't.

> 💛 **From Joseph:** Her explanation was that the winning one "wanted to go further." Hard to argue with, honestly.

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 2: The First Proper Rain
**Use when:** It's set in for the day — grey, steady, and cold enough that even a puddle walk isn't appealing.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–9:25 AM** — **The Doorstep Dash** — thirty seconds outside to grab leaves (10 min)
- **9:25–10:30 AM** — Main Event: The Leaf Print Studio (65 min)
- **10:30–10:45 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:45–11:30 AM** — Indoor Movement: The Puddle Course (45 min)
- **11:30 AM–12:00 PM** — Quiet Play (30 min)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Leaf Print Studio
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- A basket of leaves — grabbed from the doorstep, or gathered the day before
- Paint in autumn colours, and a roller or a fat brush
- Plain paper and a few sheets of cardstock
- Crayons with the paper peeled off
- Kitchen roll and old newspaper
- A heavy book, for pressing
- A rolling pin, or a smooth jar

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Dry the leaves first**, patted between kitchen roll. Wet leaves smear rather than print, and that's the difference between this working and not.
2. **Rubbings first**, as a warm-up: leaf under paper, peeled crayon rubbed on its side across the top. **Vein side up** — that's the one that shows.
3. **Then the printing.** Paint the underside of a leaf, lay it paint-side-down on the paper, cover with a second sheet, and roll over it. Peel it back slowly — the reveal is the best part and it should be hers to do.
4. Print a whole sheet of overlapping leaves in different colours for wrapping paper.
5. **Then pressing.** The flattest leaves go between kitchen roll inside a heavy book. **Write the date on a slip of paper and put it in with them.** Take them out in a fortnight.
6. Hang the prints along a string across the window.

> 💡 **Tip:** One leaf can be printed four or five times, and it gets better each time as the excess paint comes off. Don't repaint between prints — show her the second print is the good one.

### 🏃 Indoor Movement: The Puddle Course
Cushions and towels laid out as puddles across the floor. Jump from one to the next without touching the carpet. Then reverse it — only the carpet is safe. Then everyone crosses it backwards.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston gets one big leaf, one blob of paint on a tray, and paper taped to the floor. He prints with his palm, which is a perfectly good version, and it goes in the keepsake box.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Autumn is the first season she can watch changing week by week.**
> Time is genuinely hard at three — *yesterday*, *last week* and *last year* often mean much the same thing. Seasons are usually too slow to register as movement. Autumn is the exception: the tree at the end of the road looks visibly different every week for two months.
> That makes it unusually good material for building a sense of change over time. A pressed leaf with a date on it, taken out a fortnight later and compared to a fresh one, gives her something concrete to hang *before* and *after* on. It's the same reason the height chart works. Slow change becomes visible when you leave yourself evidence.

> 💛 **From Joseph:** She checks the pressed ones every single day to see if they're done yet. Two weeks is a very long time in this house.

## 🤒 Sick Day 1: The Sofa Nest
**Use when:** She's got the first cold of the season — streaming, croaky, tired, but not seriously unwell.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **Whenever she wakes** — Build the nest, no rush (20 min)
- **Mid-morning** — Main Event: The Story Recording (20–30 min, in bursts)
- **Snack** — Something warm, and a lot to drink
- **Late morning** — Wind-Down: The Story Played Back (as long as it lasts)
- **Nap early**, and let it run long

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Story Recording
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- A phone or anything that records
- Cushions, blankets, and her favourite things
- Two or three familiar books
- Story stones, toys, or picture cards as prompts
- A drink with a straw
- Tissues, within her reach

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Build the nest first and let her direct it.** Cushions where she wants them, blankets layered, everything she needs in arm's reach so she doesn't have to get up.
2. **Read to her first, for as long as she'll take it.** No participation required. This is the wind-in.
3. Then hold the phone out and ask her to tell you a story. **Prompts help enormously** — three objects, or a favourite book character, or "tell me about Kreston".
4. **Don't correct, don't prompt for detail, don't ask her to speak up.** Let it be rambling and half-inaudible.
5. **Then play it back.** This is the actual event of the day, and it usually produces delight, embarrassment, or both.
6. She'll want to record another one immediately. Let her, until she doesn't.
7. **Keep the files.** Label them with the date. A croaky three-year-old telling a story that goes nowhere is the single best recording you'll make this year.

> 💡 **Tip:** Record yourself telling one too, badly, first. It removes the self-consciousness entirely and she'll be much less guarded on hers.

### 😴 Wind-Down: The Story Played Back
Lie in the nest and play her stories back on a loop, with the lights low. If she drifts off during it, that's the ideal outcome and the whole reason it's the last activity.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** If Kreston's got it too, put them in the same nest at opposite ends and accept that today is a write-off for everyone. Record him babbling as well — you'll want it more than you expect.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Recording her and playing it back is a rare chance for her to hear herself.**
> Children of this age almost never encounter their own voice from the outside, and hearing it is genuinely striking to them. It also produces a small burst of self-awareness — *that's me, that's what I sound like, that's what I said* — that's hard to arrange any other way.
> There's a language benefit hiding in it as well. Listening back to her own sentences, at her own pace, with no one waiting for her to finish, is a form of self-monitoring she can't do while she's talking. But mostly it's a very cheap way to make a flat, miserable day contain one good thing, and to end up with something you'll be glad to still have in fifteen years.

> 💛 **From Joseph:** Her whole story was about a dog who had a cold. Not subtle, but committed.

## 🤧 Sick Day 2: The Quiet Table
**Use when:** She's past the worst but still flat — bored, whiny, and not up to anything that requires effort.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **Whenever she wakes** — Slow start, a warm drink, no demands (30 min)
- **Mid-morning** — Main Event: The Window Watch (20–40 min, in bursts)
- **Snack** — Warm, soft, and drinkable
- **Late morning** — Wind-Down: The Warm Water Table (as long as it holds)
- **Nap whenever she'll take it**

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Window Watch
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The best window in the house, and a chair or cushion at it
- A blanket
- A clipboard or a book to lean on
- Paper and a pencil
- Stickers, for tallying
- Binoculars, real or made from tubes
- A warm drink

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Set her up at the window, wrapped up, with the clipboard on her lap. **Nothing about this should require her to sit up straight or hold anything heavy.**
2. **Choose three things to count**, and she picks them: red cars, dogs, birds, people with umbrellas, leaves that fall past the window.
3. Draw three columns and label each with her picture. A sticker or a mark goes in the column each time she spots one.
4. **Let it be slow.** Long gaps where nothing happens are fine, and on a sick day the gaps are doing you a favour.
5. When she flags, take over the tallying and let her just watch and call them out.
6. **At the end, count the columns and find the winner.** Which was most? Which was least? Compare it to what she predicted at the start, if she made a prediction.
7. Leave the chart on the windowsill. She'll add to it tomorrow without being asked.

> 💡 **Tip:** Do the counting entirely out loud together. Touch-counting a row of stickers with her is real one-to-one correspondence practice, and it costs her nothing on a day when everything else does.

### 😴 Wind-Down: The Warm Water Table
A washing-up bowl of warm water on a towel on the table, a few cups and spoons, and nothing else. Sleeves up. Warm water is soothing when a child feels rough, requires nothing of her, and reliably runs for twenty minutes. **Stay in the room the whole time.**

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston does not get the water bowl unsupervised, at any point, for any length of time. If you're on your own with both of them, do the water later and give him a basket of soft things to unpack next to her chair instead.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Being ill is her first experience of her body not obeying her.**
> a three-year-old with a bad cold isn't just uncomfortable — she's confused. She wants to run and can't, wants to eat and doesn't want to, feels wrong in a way she has no words for. A lot of the whining is that confusion rather than the symptoms.
> What helps is naming it plainly and without alarm: *your body is busy fighting something, so it hasn't got much left over for playing today, and that's why you feel funny.* It gives her a story for the strangeness and a reason to expect it to end. Children handle discomfort far better when they've been told what it is and roughly how long it lasts.

> 💛 **From Joseph:** "I want to feel better yesterday." Same, love.

## ✨ September — Extra Ideas
### *When you just need one more thing*

---

### 🎲 Alternative Activities

**Leaf Pile** — Rake it up, jump in it, rake it up again. The best September hour there is, and it's free.

**Conker Collecting** — A bag, a walk, and a target number. Then sort them by size on the doorstep.

**The Puddle Depth Test** — Wellies and a stick. Find the deepest one on the street and mark it. Check it again after the next rain.

**Torch Tag in the Garden** — It's dark by seven now. Two torches, and you have to catch each other's beam.

**Post Office, Again** — The shoebox and the mailbag from Week 36. She'll run it for an hour with no input.

**Shape Sorter Races** — Week 38's cardboard machine, timed. Beat her own record, not yours.

**The Salt Tray** — Out on the table, five minutes at a time, whenever she drifts past it. More effective in scraps than in sessions.

**Wind Testing** — A ribbon on a stick, a plastic bag, a paper windmill. Find the windiest corner of the garden.

**Acorn Counting** — A pile, an egg box, and one in each hole. Simple counting she'll do repeatedly.

**Big Sister Reading** — Azlyn "reads" a board book to Kreston, entirely from the pictures. Both of them get something out of it.

**Kreston's Turn** — He's 15–16 months. A box with a hole cut in the lid and a pile of things to post, a low step to climb, and a basket of leaves to carry from one room to another. Three separate twenty-minute activities in one sentence.

---

### 🎨 Alternative Arts & Crafts

**Leaf Rubbings** — Peeled crayon on its side, vein side up. Endless and always works.

**Leaf Printing** — Paint the underside, press, roll, peel. Makes good wrapping paper.

**Pressed Leaf Cards** — A fortnight in a heavy book, then glued to folded card.

**Conker Threading** — Adult drills the holes, she threads them on a shoelace. Excellent fine motor work.

**Apple Stamping** — An apple halved across the middle has a star inside it. Paint it and print.

**The Colour Wheel, Again** — Week 38's mixing, with two colours she chooses. Twenty minutes, no set-up.

**Name Tracing in Anything** — Flour, sand, shaving foam, salt, condensation on the window.

**Sticky Window Collage** — Contact paper on the window, sticky side out, and a basket of tissue and leaves.

**Junk Model Vehicle** — Bin lorry, post van, tractor. Boxes, plate wheels, split pins.

**Autumn Colour Chart** — Paint chips and real leaves, added to every week as the tree turns.

**Handprint Tree** — Brown handprint trunk, fingerprint leaves in red, orange and yellow. Date it.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **September restarts everything, including her, and the first few weeks of that are hard.**
> Even with no school run in the picture, September changes the shape of the year — the light goes, the weather turns, routines around you shift, and everyone she knows is suddenly busier. Children are unusually sensitive to that kind of ambient change and it comes out sideways: worse sleep, shorter fuse, more clinginess, sudden fear of things that were fine in August.
> None of that needs solving. It needs a few weeks of slightly lower expectations, a slightly earlier bedtime, and a bit more of you than usual. By mid-October it settles on its own. Reading the wobble as a transition rather than a regression is most of what's required.

> 💛 **From Joseph:** She's been asking for you more this month than she has all year. That's not a step backwards, and you're not doing anything wrong.

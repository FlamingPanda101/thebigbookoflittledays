# 🌧️ September — Backup Days & Extra Ideas

September hands you both ends of the weather in one month, and this section is
for the days the plan does not survive. Two of these are written for cold rain
and the first hard gray mornings, as full days with the outdoor hour swapped
for something indoors. Two are for illness, and those run on a loose clock or
on none at all. The last page is a list to open at eight in the morning when
you have ten minutes and nothing in your head.

---

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 1 — The Day the Rain Sets In

Rain from before breakfast, the kind with no gap in it, and a cold floor. The
whole day runs on water indoors, which sounds like a bad idea and works better
than it sounds. She gets wet to the elbows twice and the mess stays in two
rooms.

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Stand a dark baking tray, a dinner plate, a heatproof mug and two saucers on the kitchen counter. Put the clothespins where you can reach them and clear a run of counter beside the sink.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: Wet Hands on the Dark Tray
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: Where the Water Comes Back (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Five Minutes at the Open Door (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: Washing Day at the Kitchen Sink (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🛋️ Indoor Hour: The Blanket Over the Kitchen Table (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: Bringing In the Rain Jar
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: The Clothespin Basket
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: Warm Bath and Dry Pajamas
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Wet Hands on the Dark Tray

**🧰 You need:** 1 dark baking tray · a shallow bowl of warm water · 1 wide paintbrush · 1 dish towel.

1. Put the tray flat on the table, the bowl beside it and the dish towel under the bowl. Let her press a wet hand down and lift it off.
2. Both of you watch the print until it goes. Ask her where it went and take whatever she says without fixing it.
3. Give her the brush and let her paint the tray full of shapes. Tell her you are going to catch the water at 9:15, and leave that sitting there.

### 🎨 The Main Event: Where the Water Comes Back

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 dark baking tray
- 1 heatproof mug
- 1 dinner plate wide enough to cover the mug
- 2 saucers
- 4–6 ice cubes
- Masking tape and a marker
- 1 wide paintbrush
- 1 dish towel

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Fill both saucers to the same depth from the cold faucet. Tape a label on each and let her make her mark on them. One goes on the warm spot near the radiator or the stove, one in the coldest corner of the room. You come back to these twice.
2. Fill the mug from the hot faucet, stand it on the dish towel in the middle of the counter, and hold the plate flat above it without touching the rim. Count to twenty out loud with her.
3. Turn the plate over and let her put her fingers in the drops. Tell her the water came out of the mug and landed there, and let her feel that it is warm.
4. Lay the ice cubes on top of the plate and hold it over the mug again. More drops, bigger ones, and some of them run. Tip the plate so they run back into the mug while she watches.
5. Paint one more handprint on the tray, count how long it lasts, then breathe on the cold window and let her draw in the fog with a finger.
6. Check the two saucers at 3:30 and again before dinner, and let her tell you which one has less in it.

> 💡 **Tip:** Her answer to *where did it go* will be *away*, and that is the right answer for now. The plate is the argument, so hand her the drops instead of the word.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Five Minutes at the Open Door

Coats and rain boots on, both of you, and open the back door as wide as it
goes. Stay under the cover and put an empty jar out at arm's length on the
step. Listen for three sounds: rain on the roof, rain on a leaf, rain on the
bare ground, and let her tell you which one is loudest. Bring the jar in every
few minutes to see the line creep up, mark it with tape, and put it back out.
Come inside, dry hands, and do it twice more before eleven. Five minutes at a
time is the whole trick, because the door shuts before anybody gets cold or
bored.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 15 months and he runs, so the doorway
> is a decision he will make for you. Hold his hand or keep the gate across
> while the door is open. Let him put a flat palm out into the rain and pull it
> back, over and over. At the counter he sits up at the table with his own
> spoon and a bowl holding a splash of warm water, doing the same job as his
> sister at her own level. The ice cubes stay on her plate, out of his reach;
> a whole cube is a choking size at 15 months. He points at the window; you
> name what he lands on.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: Washing Day at the Kitchen Sink

The morning was small drops on a plate. The afternoon is both arms in a sink
of warm suds, and it eats ninety minutes without any help from you.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- A sturdy chair or step stool at the sink
- Dish soap
- 8–10 small washable things: doll clothes, her socks, washcloths, dish towels
- 1 dry towel for the floor
- 1 dry towel for wringing
- A length of string, 6 feet
- 8 clothespins
- An apron or an old shirt

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lay the dry towel down at the foot of the stool before anything else. A wet kitchen floor and a running toddler is the one part of this that can go wrong.
2. Apron on her first, then fill one side of the sink with warm soapy water and the other with plain. Let her test both with a hand and tell you which is which.
3. Tip the pile out on the counter and have her sort it into what she thinks is dirtiest. She will be wrong about most of it and the order does not matter.
4. She washes and rinses one thing at a time. You wring the heavy things into the second dry towel, and hand her the ones where a poor squeeze costs nothing.
5. Tie the string between two chair backs at her height and let her peg the whole line up herself. The clothespins are stiff, so give her the ones that open easiest and let the fight with the rest run a while.
6. Tie it back to the morning: put a hand on the cold window and ask where the water in the socks is going. Check the coldest pane again after her snack.
7. Take down whatever is dry before dinner, let her fold the flat things, and leave the rest hanging overnight.

> 💡 **Tip:** The wringing is the part she wants and the part she is worst at. Give her the washcloths and keep her own clothes out of the pile.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- Skip the line and dry everything on the radiator, checking every twenty minutes.
- Wash the plastic play food and the toy cars instead, in a bowl on a towel.
- Bath toys in the sink, and she runs a car wash for the cars.
- Sort every sock in the house into pairs on the kitchen floor.
- Fill three cups to the same line and see which one is emptiest by bedtime.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** He wants the sink and he cannot have it. Put a
> shallow bowl with two inches of water on a folded towel on the floor beside
> her stool, give him a spoon and a plastic cup, and let him work down there
> where she can see him. ⚠️ Two inches is enough to drown a 15-month-old who
> falls face down in it. Stay in the room the whole time, and tip it out the
> moment you leave. Later, dry clothes and a stack of board books on your lap,
> and let him point while you name.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **She thinks water that dries has gone, and it takes seeing it come back to fix that.**
> Azlyn watches a wet handprint fade off a tray and files it under gone. She
> works from what she can see, and an invisible thing has stopped existing
> until something puts it back in front of her. The plate over the hot mug does
> that in about twenty seconds. Water leaves the mug, lands on the cold plate,
> and she can put a finger in it. She will not come away with the word
> evaporation and she has no use for it yet. She comes away with the memory of
> water turning up somewhere she did not expect, and that memory is what a
> science lesson at six hooks onto. Tell her it went into the air. Stop there.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Hot water from the faucet only, never a kettle or a pan, and
> the mug stays in the middle of the counter with its handle turned in.
> Kreston is 15 months and fast, so the standing water on the floor is yours to
> watch the entire time it is out. Ice cubes are a choking size at his age, so
> they stay on the plate in front of you and never in his bowl. Mop spills as
> they happen. A wet kitchen floor is what will hurt somebody today.

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 2 — The First Raw Morning of the Year

The first morning where the house feels cold before the heat comes on. Wind
hard enough to strip the maple, low cloud, rain that never commits, and
nothing out there worth walking in. Put the stove to work, because the warmth
is doing half the parenting today.

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Stand the biggest pot you own on the stove and put a chopping board and a butter knife on the table. Tear four strips of paper and find the tape. Empty the vegetable drawer into a bowl, and if it comes up thin, stand a can and a bag of frozen vegetables beside it.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: Four Guesses Taped to the Glass
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: Everything in the Drawer Into One Pot (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Wind From the Warm Side of the Glass (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: Eight Things and a Card Each (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🛋️ Indoor Hour: Reading the Four Guesses Back (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: The Bread Torn Into the Basket
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: The Cards She Wrote
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: The Warmest Room and Two Books
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Four Guesses Taped to the Glass

**🧰 You need:** 4 strips of paper · masking tape · 1 marker.

1. Stand at the window with her, both looking at the same sky, and ask four questions she can check by three o'clock. Will the rain stop. Will that branch still be moving. Will anybody go past with an umbrella. Will the puddle by the step be gone.
2. Write her answer word for word on a strip, in her words, and tape it to the glass at her height. Write your own guesses on strips and tape them alongside.
3. Do not steer her toward the likely answer. A confident wrong guess is worth more to this day than a careful right one.

### 🎨 The Main Event: Everything in the Drawer Into One Pot

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The biggest pot in the house
- Whatever the vegetable drawer holds: an onion, 2 carrots, 2 potatoes, a stick of celery, anything going soft. A can of mixed vegetables or a bag of frozen ones stands in for a thin drawer, and a cup of dry lentils or rice fills out a thin pot
- 1 can of beans or tomatoes
- 1 bouillon cube, a spoon of bouillon, or salted water
- 1 butter knife (hers) and 1 sharp knife (**adult only**)
- 2 chopping boards
- 1 wooden spoon
- A bowl for her cut pieces
- Half a loaf of bread, or a sleeve of crackers

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Empty the vegetable bowl onto the table and let her line everything up biggest to smallest. Do not correct the order she lands on. Cans and a frozen bag line up in the row with everything else.
2. You take the counter, the sharp knife and the hard vegetables. She takes the table, her own board, the butter knife and the soft things: mushrooms, a cooked potato, half a zucchini, and thick slices of bread when the drawer runs short.
3. Every piece she cuts goes into her bowl, and she carries the bowl to you at the stove and tips it in. Let her make the trip eight times rather than once. The can, the lentils and the bouillon cube go in behind her, then water to cover.
4. Lift her to see the pot before the heat goes on. Say what the top of it looks like out loud, then put her down and start it.
5. After fifteen minutes lift her again, hold the lid yourself, and let her tell you what is different. You stir with the wooden spoon and she counts the turns. Steam on her face is part of the answer.
6. Give her a bowl at lunch with a fork and let her mash her own, as thick or thin as she wants it. The pot holds for dinner, and the bread is her four o'clock job.

> 💡 **Tip:** The chopping is the activity and the soup is a by-product. If she turns one mushroom into thirty pieces and loses interest, you got the hour you needed.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Wind From the Warm Side of the Glass

Take a blanket to the window and sit in it together for the whole forty-five
minutes. Pick one branch and watch it. Count everything outside that moves and
everything that holds still, and keep a tally with two piles of clothespins on
the sill. Open the window three inches so she can put a hand out into the cold
and pull it back, then shut it and let her say what her hand felt. On the first
hard cold morning of the year that gap of three inches is the entire lesson.
Finish with a warm drink held in both hands while the glass fogs up in front of
her.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 15 months and points at everything
> through glass, so hold him up and name three moving things, then let him
> choose the fourth. While the chopping is on he sits strapped in his chair at
> the far end of the table with his own spoon and a bowl of dry cereal, doing
> the job beside her. Her board stays an arm's length past his reach. Raw
> vegetable pieces off it are a choking size for him, so his bowl holds his own
> food and the butter knife and the boards stay down her end.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: Eight Things and a Card Each

The morning was hot, loud and standing up. The afternoon is on the floor in
front of the coffee table, with the wind still working at the window.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 cloth or dish towel to cover the table
- 8 objects from around the house, hers to choose
- Paper torn into 8 rectangles
- 1 marker
- Masking tape
- 1 strip of paper for the sign
- A phone for photographs

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Give her a bag and one rule: eight things nobody else in the house would have picked, and none of them toys.
2. Cover the table with the cloth and set the eight out with space around each one. Space is what makes them look important, so resist crowding them.
3. Write a card for each. Ask what it is and where it came from, and put her answer down word for word without tidying it up.
4. Have her order them oldest to newest and say why. Her reasons will be wrong and worth writing on the back of the cards.
5. Tape the sign to the doorway and let her name the collection. Write the name in her spelling if she has any.
6. She takes you round, one object at a time. Ask a real question about each and let her answer run long past where you would stop it.
7. Photograph the table before she takes it apart. It comes down before dinner and the cards go in the drawer.

> 💡 **Tip:** Do not correct a wrong history. She told you the wooden spoon came out of the sea, and that goes on the card in the words she used.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- Do the eight objects by touch alone, in a pillowcase, and she guesses each.
- Give her the phone and let her photograph her eight instead of labeling them.
- Build the collection out of one color only.
- Set up a shop instead, and she prices everything with bottle caps.
- Turn every lamp in the house on and off and vote for the coziest room.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** He walks straight through an exhibition at 15
> months, so give him the far end of the room, a low box with three of his own
> things in it, and the job of bringing them to you one at a time. He will do
> that for longer than you expect. If there is another adult in the house, half
> an hour in the stroller under the rain cover in the wind buys her a clear
> stretch, and he comes back cold, fed up and ready to sleep.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Being wrong in a forecast is the most useful kind of wrong.**
> Four guesses went on the window this morning and half of them were wrong by
> three o'clock, yours included. A guess she has committed to changes how she
> looks at the sky, because now she has something riding on it. Your own wrong
> strip does more work than her right one. She watches you read it out, laugh,
> and say the sky went and did something else, and she is at the age where she
> is starting to notice that adults get things wrong at all. Your own reaction
> teaches her what to do with her own mistakes. Read every strip out, check it
> against the window, and leave the wrong ones taped up.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Pot handles turn in toward the wall and the back burner
> takes the pot whenever it fits. Kreston runs, so the gate goes across the
> kitchen door while the stove is on, or he sits strapped in his chair where
> you can see him. The sharp knife stays on the counter beside you and goes
> back in the drawer between cuts, never on the table with her board. Raw
> vegetable pieces off her board are a choking size for him. His bowl is dry
> cereal and nothing off her board. The clothespins are the only things that
> go on the sill while he is up at the glass with you, and they go up out of
> reach before he is down on the floor.

## 🤒 Sick Day 1 — The Day She Does Not Get Up

Fever, aching, the whole thing. She wants the sofa, one blanket and you within
arm's reach, and there is no plan today beyond keeping fluid in her and letting
her sleep when she can. Everything below is optional and can stop mid-sentence.
Nothing here needs cutting, baking or clearing up.

### 🕰️ The Shape of the Day

- **Whenever she wakes:** sofa made up before breakfast is mentioned
- **The slow first stretch:** she picks her tree, and that is the whole morning's project
- **When she asks for food:** small, cold, salty or plain, offered and not pushed
- **The rest block:** the middle of the day belongs to sleep, however long it runs
- **After the long sleep:** one thing from the one-hand list, then back to lying down
- **Before the afternoon dip:** fluids again, and the tree checked once more
- **The long evening:** same books, dim lamp, an early night that will not be early

### 🛋️ Whenever She Wakes: The Sofa Made Up Like a Bed

Do this before she has to ask. Her own pillow off her bed, the blanket she
sleeps under, a folded towel over the cushion in case, and a small cup of water
standing where she can reach it without sitting up. Put a wastebasket lined
with a bag on the floor beside her. Move the whole day to this one spot so she
never has to negotiate about where she is allowed to be. Sick children settle
faster in a nest that smells like their own bed than in a made-up guest
arrangement, and it takes four minutes to build.

### 🪟 Her Tree, Chosen From the Sofa

Sit her up enough to see out and let her pick one tree. Hers, out of whatever
is visible from where she is lying. She names it.
Write the name on a scrap of paper and prop it on the windowsill so everybody
in the house uses it. Then the tree does the work all day: how many leaves have
turned on it, is the top branch moving, has anything landed in it, is it the
same as it was before she slept. September gives you the first yellow, so
there is something to see. Three or four checks across the day is plenty, and
each one costs her nothing.

### 😴 The Rest Block

The middle of the day is sleep and nothing competes with it. A sick
three-year-old naps like a much younger child, and two hours is normal when she
is running a fever. Curtains half closed, the room cool, one blanket rather
than three. Lie down yourself if you can get away with it. Do not wake her for
food or medicine unless the medicine is due and the fever is high, and do not
worry about tonight's bedtime; a child who sleeps four hours this afternoon
still needs the sleep more than she needs the schedule.

### 🥄 When She Asks for Food

Offer small and often and let her refuse most of it. Water in a small cup she
can hold, a popsicle, ice chips in a bowl, dry crackers, plain toast, a
banana, cold applesauce out of the fridge. Fluids matter more than food, so
count wet diapers or trips to the bathroom rather than mouthfuls. Nothing hot
and nothing she has to sit up for. Put it on a plate within reach and walk
away; a plate she can ignore gets eaten more often than one held in front of
her face.

### ✋ Things You Can Do With One Hand

- Draw a shape on her back with one finger and let her guess it.
- Scroll the photos on your phone, oldest first, while she names who is in them.
- Tell a story where she gives you one word and you have to get it in.
- Put one of three objects into her closed hand under the blanket and let her name it.
- Read the same book four times without mentioning that it is the same book.
- Say what you are doing in the kitchen out loud so she can hear you from the sofa.

> 👶 **Where Kreston Goes Today:** He is 15 months, he runs, and he will climb
> straight onto her chest. Gate him at the other end of the room with his own
> pile of board books and let him point while you name from where you are
> sitting. If another adult can take him out for an hour, take that offer even
> if the weather is poor. Keep his cup, spoon and towel separate from hers all
> day. Assume he is next, and get his laundry and his bedding done tonight
> while you still have the hands to do it.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **A tree she's chosen is different from a tree she's shown.**
> You could point at the tree outside the window and tell her the leaves are
> turning. She would look for two seconds. Let her pick her own out of the four
> she can see from the sofa and it belongs to her for the day. Ownership does
> the work that attention has to be coaxed into. She checks it after her
> sleep without being asked, and reports a branch that moved. She holds on to
> what she has claimed, and a claimed tree gives a shapeless sick day one thread
> running from morning to dark. Ask her to name it. Write the name down and use
> it every time you mention it, tomorrow included.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Call the doctor, today, for any of these: she cannot keep
> fluids down for eight hours, no wet diaper or no trip to the bathroom in
> eight hours, breathing that is fast or looks hard, a rash that stays visible
> when you press a clear glass onto it, a fever that will not come down with
> medicine or has run past three days, or a child too drowsy to rouse. Dose
> fever medicine by her weight, off the bottle, and write down the time of
> every dose. If something looks wrong to you and it is not on this list, call
> anyway. Ice goes in only while she sits up, crushed small under the back of
> a spoon, and whole cubes stay in the freezer, because a cube slides back on
> a flat child and blocks her airway.

## 🤒 Sick Day 2 — Better Enough to Be Bored

The fever broke overnight and she is not better. She wakes early, wants
everything, has the energy for about nine minutes of it, and then falls apart.
This day is harder than yesterday. The job is to keep her horizontal while
giving her enough to chew on that she stops fighting you about it.

### 🕰️ The Shape of the Day

- **Whenever she wakes:** earlier than you want, and asking for things
- **The first long stretch:** four piles on the blanket, done lying down
- **When she asks for food:** real food today, small portions, and let her ask twice
- **The rest block:** the argument of the day, and worth having
- **After the rest:** one job she can do from the sofa, and Kreston for fifteen supervised minutes
- **Before the afternoon dip:** the window open a hand's width for four minutes of cold air, from under the blanket
- **The long evening:** an ordinary bedtime, held firm, because tomorrow is a real day

### 🧺 Four Piles on the Blanket

Start on your phone, because it costs nothing to set up. Swipe the photos and
let her put each one in hot, cold, growing or falling before you move on. Let
her name the four for herself and use her words for the rest of the day even
if they are odd. She will file a snowy picture under hot and argue for it. Let
the argument run and put it where she says. Paper is better once she has
warmed up, so bring the junk mail, a seed catalog and any old magazine nobody
will miss, spread the blanket over her legs, and let her tear pictures out
into the four corners of it. No scissors. When she is finished, fold the
blanket in on itself with the scraps inside and carry the whole thing to the
recycling in one trip.

### 😴 The Rest She Argues About

She does not want it and she needs it. Drop the word sleep and change the deal:
the blanket comes down onto the floor, the curtains go half closed, and the
rule is a quiet body rather than shut eyes. Lie down next to her and go quiet
first. Most children on the mend go under within fifteen minutes of an adult
who has stopped talking. If she stays awake, an hour of lying still is worth
having on its own, and it keeps the six o'clock collapse from arriving at four.

### 🧦 One Job She Can Do From the Sofa

Tip the clean sock basket onto her blanket and let her pair the lot. Everything
in the house, including the odd ones that have been odd since June. She sorts,
matches, folds them into rough squares, and stacks by whose feet they belong
on. Twenty minutes, no setup, no cleanup, and a useful thing gets done. The odd
ones go in a bag on her lap and she decides which of them get one more month.

### ✋ More That Works From Horizontal

- Read her the shopping list and let her add three things.
- Put the radio on and let her call out every time she hears a word she knows.
- Give her the stack of cards from the drawer and let her deal everyone a hand.
- Line the edge of the blanket with anything from the kitchen drawer and let her arrange it by size.
- Let her plan tomorrow out loud while you write it down as a list.

> 👶 **Where Kreston Goes Today:** She is well enough to want him and not well
> enough to cope with him, so the two of them get fifteen minutes on the floor
> with a book while you sit between them, and you end it before it sours rather
> than after. He is 15 months and running, so the sock basket is a game he will
> join uninvited; give him his own handful and let him carry them one at a time
> to the far end of the room. Keep his cup separate for another two days.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Sorting the year into four is easier than remembering it.**
> A year is too long for Azlyn to hold in her head. She has no working sense of
> last March and no way to place next February. Four corners of a blanket give
> her something her hands can manage: hot, cold, growing, falling, with a
> picture in each. Sorting is a skill she already owns, so the year arrives on
> the back of a skill instead of on its own. She will put a snowy picture in the
> hot corner and defend it. Let her. The categories firm up over a
> couple of years, and every pass adds a bit. On a day when she cannot get
> off the sofa, this costs her nothing and still moves her forward.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** A child who has been fine all day and then goes downhill in
> the evening is worth a phone call, and so is a second fever arriving after a
> clear day. Keep her home until she has been twenty-four hours clear of fever
> and vomiting without medicine, for the other children's sake as much as hers.
> Wash your hands after every diaper, every cup and every tissue today; you are
> the one carrying it around the house.

## 🎲 September — Extra Ideas

### 🎲 Alternative Activities
- **The Coat Argument, Settled at the Corner**, she picks her own coat, you walk two minutes, and the cold decides it.
- **Six Bottles Down the Hallway**, bowling with the fattest buckeye in the bowl and a rebuilt set every time.

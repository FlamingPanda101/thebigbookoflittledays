## Week 4: Puzzles & Problem Solving 🧩
**📅 January 22 – January 28, 2027**

---

### 🎯 What This Week Is About

Seven days of problems that have real answers in them, and your job for most of
the week is to sit on your hands. You cut a square into seven pieces and Azlyn
tries to rebuild it, gets six different lids off, works out what has gone from
a tray of eight things, sorts one pile twice under two different rules, and
rolls a ball into three dead ends before she finds the way out. The pause
before you help is the method. She solves more than you expect once nobody
reaches in.

No holiday lands this week, so the thread running through it is cardboard.
Friday's tangram, Saturday's jigsaw, Monday's pairs cards and Thursday's maze
walls all come off the same flattened cereal boxes, and one day's offcuts are
the next day's materials. Start the pile behind the sofa tonight. Tuesday is
swimming, the fourth of the year, and Saturday's aquarium is the big outing.

---

### 🧒 What Azlyn Will Learn

- **Turning a shape until it fits.** Tangram pieces go in flipped and backwards before they drop into the gap.
- **Six ways to open a thing.** Screw, clip, zip, tape, knot and elastic band, each one asking her hands for something different.
- **Holding a set in her head.** Eight objects on a tray, one taken away, and she names the gap.
- **Running two rules over one pile.** A red spoon is a red thing at nine and a two-hands thing at ten.
- **Backing out of a dead end.** She sees the route was wrong, goes back to the last corner and takes the other way.

---

### 👩 What You'll Learn

- Sharing and turn-taking are different skills, and one is much easier
- Being told what someone loves about you is different from being praised
- Puzzles build a skill that has nothing to do with puzzles
- Working memory is the thing she is building
- Classification is quiet, invisible math
- Describing before guessing is the whole skill
- Dead ends are the point of a maze

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Cutting the Seven Pieces

**🧰 You need:** 1 cereal-box card cut to an 8-inch square · a ruler or any straight edge · 1 pencil · scissors (adult only).

1. Rule a line corner to corner and cut along it. Two big triangles. She holds the card flat while you cut.
2. Cut one of those in half again, then work the other down into a medium triangle, two small ones, a square and a slanted four-sided piece.
3. Seven pieces on the table and no picture yet. Let her push them round, then leave them until 9:15.

### 🎨 The Main Event: Tangram Animals

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The seven cut pieces
- 4 sheets of paper for tracing outlines
- 1 pencil and 1 permanent marker
- 1 envelope or freezer bag to keep the set in
- A clear table or a tray to work on

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Hand her all seven and ask her to make the square they came from. Leave her with it a while. She does not have to get it.
2. Build a cat together on a sheet of paper: big triangle for the body, the square for the head, two small triangles for ears. She sets the ears.
3. Draw round the finished cat in pencil, lift the pieces off and go over the outline in marker.
4. Do a boat and a house the same way, tracing each one before you break it up.
5. Let her invent the fourth. Trace it and write her name for it along the bottom, spelling and all.
6. Everything goes in the envelope with **TANGRAM** on the front. She is back at it by 4:15.

> 💡 **Tip:** When she pushes a piece in upside down, leave it. Turning a
> shape over until it drops into the gap is the skill, and your hand on the
> piece ends it.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Puzzle Swap

A second set of seven comes off the other cereal box: two builders, two trays,
a timer.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**

- A second set cut from another cereal box
- 2 trays or baking sheets
- 2 sheets of paper
- 1 marker
- 1 dish towel
- An egg timer or the phone timer

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Cut the second set with her calling out which piece comes next. She watched at 8:00 and remembers more of the order than you expect.
2. One set on each tray, sitting opposite each other with the timer between you.
3. Two minutes on the clock. Build a picture on your tray without showing her while she builds one on hers.
4. Slide your tray alongside hers with your picture still standing on it. She clears her own tray and copies yours piece by piece. Set the timer again.

6. One round from memory, three pieces only. Build a small shape, give her a slow count of five, drop the dish towel over it and let her go. Three she can hold in her head at this age. Asking for seven ends the afternoon.
7. Let her set the timer for the last round, then tip both sets into one pile and build one enormous animal.

> 💡 **Tip:** Rebuilding someone else's picture is harder than making your own,

### 🌍 Around the World: China and the Tangram 🧩

The seven pieces on your table are Chinese and they are old. The puzzle is
called *qi qiao ban*, seven boards of skill, and Chinese printers were selling
books of tangram figures by the early 1800s. Sets traveled, and by 1818 Europe
and America were in the middle of a tangram craze, thousands of shapes, every
one using all seven pieces and nothing else.

> **✨ Try it:** Cut her lunchtime sandwich into the same seven shapes and
> let her rebuild the square on the plate before she eats it.

> *Did you know?* Two mathematicians proved in 1942 that only thirteen tangram
> shapes have no dents in the edges.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Sharing and turn-taking are different skills, and one is much easier.**
> Azlyn will hand you a tangram piece when you say *your turn* and refuse to
> hand over the same piece when you say *share it*. Turn-taking has a shape
> she can hold in her head. It comes back, and something outside her decides
> when: a timer, the end of a round, a count. Sharing asks her to give a thing
> up now with no visible return, and most three-year-olds manage the first a
> year or more before the second. You already put a timer or a count on it, so
> the return is something she can hear coming, and the piece leaves her hand.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Seven small card pieces and a 7-month-old who mouths
> everything do not go together. Build on the table, sweep before you put
> Kreston down, and count the pieces back into the envelope every time. The
> scissors are adult only and go back up out of reach between cuts, not onto
> the table where she is working.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Jigsaw with Words on the Back

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 2 sheets of cereal-box card
- Her drawing, or a printed photo
- 1 glue stick
- Scissors (**adult only**)
- 1 permanent marker
- 1 freezer bag or envelope for storage
- 1 tray to build on

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Glue the drawing onto the second sheet and press it under a heavy book for ten minutes.
2. While it presses, write ten things you love about her on the back of an envelope. Specific ones. *You hold the door open for Kreston* beats *you are kind*.
3. Turn the card face down and draw ten chunky pieces on it in marker. Fat lobes, nothing thinner than her finger. Write one of your ten inside each.
4. Cut along the lines with her holding the card steady. The scissors go back on the shelf as the last piece comes free.
5. Jumble the pieces picture side up on the tray and let her build it. Hand her the edges and wait to be asked about the middle.
6. Turn the puzzle over one piece at a time and read what is on the back. All ten, including the ones she talks over.

> 💡 **Tip:** Cut the pieces bigger than looks right. A puzzle she can finish gets built forty times. One that beats her goes in the drawer by Tuesday.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: One Piece Each

Five big pieces go out to five people, and the picture arrives when the last
comes back.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 sheet of card, cut into 5 large pieces
- Crayons
- 1 marker
- 5 envelopes or paper bags
- Masking tape

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Cut one sheet of card into five big pieces with curvy edges. Write a name on the back of each: you, Joseph, Azlyn, Kreston, and a grandparent or the teddy.
2. Let her hand them out. She reads the names off the backs by shape.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Being told what someone loves about you is different from being praised.**
> *Good girl* and *well done* are verdicts. They land on a performance, and
> the next performance has to earn them again. The ten things on the back of
> those pieces describe her instead, and she has to do nothing to keep them
> true. She holds the door for Kreston. She sings to herself in the bath.
> Nothing on that list can be failed. Children who hear a running verdict on
> their effort get more careful about trying things they might lose at, and
> children who hear themselves described build a steadier picture of who they
> are. The puzzle puts it in her hands.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Ten jigsaw pieces and a 7-month-old on the floor means the
> tray stays at table height and the count goes back in the bag every time.
> Glue sticks come apart in a mouth, so the lid goes on and the stick goes up
> whenever you leave the table. Crayons snap into mouth-sized pieces, so keep
> your hand round Kreston's for his ten seconds and put the crayon straight
> back in the box. The scissors are adult only.

### 🎨 The Main Event: Six Ways to Open a Thing

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 plastic screw-lid jar
- 1 clip-lid tub
- 1 ziplock bag
- 1 shoebox taped shut with masking tape
- 1 bag tied with string
- 1 tub held shut with an elastic band
- 6 small treasures inside: stickers, raisins, a small toy
- 1 tray

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Put the tray in front of her and sit on your hands. She picks the order and keeps what she gets out.
2. Count to twenty before you offer anything, then offer a word and not a hand: *turning*, or *find the corner*.
3. The screw lid is hardest, because two hands go opposite ways at once. If she is stuck, she holds the lid still and turns the jar.
4. The knotted string will beat her. Loosen it to one loop and hand it back so the last pull is hers.
5. When all six are open, count the treasures. Shut them again, and she picks which fastening goes on which.
6. Now she loads them and hands you the tray. Struggle out loud with the elastic band.

> 💡 **Tip:** Say what her hands are doing. *You are pushing and turning at the
> same time.* She gets words for a thing she worked out.

### 🌳 Get Outside: The Gate Latch Walk

the recycling bin, the brake on the stroller. Cold metal takes a firmer hand than

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Wall She Fits Through

A flattened box stands between two chairs with a hole in it, and the only way
across is through.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 large flattened cardboard box
- 1 permanent marker
- 1 craft knife or heavy scissors (**adult only**)
- 2 dining chairs
- Packing tape
- 1 blanket for the floor

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Spread the blanket, lay the box flat and draw round her while she lies on it, arms out. Leave that outline uncut, because it is the last hole of the afternoon.
2. Cut a dinner-plate circle high in the panel while she stands back, and put the knife away before the box goes upright.
3. Tape the box between the chair backs so it stands like a wall, blanket underneath.
4. She goes through the circle first. Starting easy is why she is still going at the fourth.
5. Take the box down, cut a new hole, stand it back up. A tall slot, a mail slot low down she crosses on her belly, one cut too small on purpose. Knife away before it goes up.
6. Let her name each shape and work out her own way through. She tries head-first twice before discovering feet.
7. Her own outline comes last, and cutting it takes the middle out of the wall. She walks through the shape of herself and the wall goes with her.

> 💡 **Tip:** Cut the next hole while she is still enjoying the last. The
> activity dies the moment she stands and watches you measure.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- One hole, one box, and she goes through it forty times.
- Crawl tunnel out of two chairs and a blanket instead.
- Post small toys through a slot in a shoebox lid and shake them out.

### 🌳 Out Again: **Pick one**

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** Lie him on the blanket on the far side of the

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Puzzles build a skill that has nothing to do with puzzles.**
> Six lids is a morning of Azlyn trying a thing, failing, and trying a
> different thing. That loop is the skill and it travels. Researchers who
> follow children into school find that staying with a hard task predicts
> school outcomes as strongly as anything they can measure at four. A closed
> container is a good place to practice, because the problem is honest: the
> lid is off or it is not, no adult decides whether she did well, and the
> treasure pays out the second she solves it. She sets her own difficulty,
> picking which one to attack next. You keep your hands still and she banks
> another minute.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Elastic bands, raisins and a ziplock bag are hazards for a
> 7-month-old. Work at the table, count the six treasures in and out, and pick
> the band up the moment it comes off. The craft knife is adult only and goes
> out of reach between every cut, not onto the arm of the sofa. Sit a bag of
> shopping on each chair seat to hold them down, stay beside her while she goes
> through, and tape the box so it pulls loose rather than dragging a chair over
> on her. Never leave Kreston beside the standing box wall on his own.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Maze in a Tray

## Week 5: Forest Friends 🌲
**📅 January 29 – February 4, 2027**

---

### 🎯 What This Week Is About

Azlyn prints four animal coats on Friday morning and builds the animals after
lunch, then spends the rest of the week finding places for them to live. A
tree up the living room wall on Saturday, a forest floor in a baking tray on
Sunday, tracks across the hall on Monday, feeders outside the window on
Tuesday, a cave in a cardboard box on Wednesday. Every day inherits the day
before it. By Thursday she has a forest in the house and enough of it in her
head to tell the whole week back to you through a lit sheet.

February arrives on Monday, and Groundhog Day lands on Tuesday the 2nd as a
bonus note inside the afternoon: people in America gather round a hole to
watch a woodchuck come up and check the weather. It sits an inch from
Wednesday's hibernation cave, so the two can talk to each other. The cold does
the rest of the work this week. Frost on the grass until the sun comes round
the houses, bare branches with every old nest showing, and shadows that run
long at eleven in the morning.

---

### 🧒 What Azlyn Will Learn

- **Patterns she can carry on.** Spot, spot, stripe down a paper strip, then finishing the row without you.
- **Where forest animals live.** Roots, trunk, branches, top, and which animal belongs at which height.
- **Reading evidence.** A print, a chewed leaf and a tuft of wool add up to an animal she never saw.
- **Waiting, and holding still on purpose.** She fills four feeders on Tuesday and the birds come on Thursday; she hibernates on Wednesday while you count to twenty.
- **Telling the week back.** Two tours, a puppet show, and a shadow fox that swells up the sheet as she walks him toward the flashlight.

---

### 👩 What You'll Learn

- Patterns are the beginning of algebra
- Explaining to someone else is how understanding gets locked in
- Loose parts beat finished toys
- Evidence and inference, a big idea learnable at three
- Delayed payoff is a muscle, and this is a gentle way to work it
- Pretending to sleep is real self-regulation practice
- Shadows are confusing at three, and the confusion is worth exploring

### 🌅 Opening Activity: The Stamp Hunt

**🧰 You need:** 1 grocery bag · the recycling · the odds drawer.

1. Hand her the bag and one job: find things that make a mark. A cork, a fork, a spool of thread, a tube end, a bottle cap.
2. Press each find into your palm and ask what shape it would leave. Take her guesses without fixing them.
3. Line the winners along the table edge in the order she wants to use them. That is the plan for 9:15.

### 🎨 The Main Event: Printing the Animal Coats

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- Long paper strips cut from a paper bag or the back of wrapping paper, 6
- Washable non-toxic poster paint in brown, black, white and orange
- 1 saucer or old plate for each color
- Cardboard tube ends, 1 cork, 1 fork, 1 crumpled piece of paper towel
- 1 wet washcloth in a shallow bowl
- Newspaper for the table

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Spread the newspaper, lay one strip in front of her the long way, and pour a thin puddle of each color into its own saucer.
2. Start the fawn. Two spots with the cork, a stripe dragged with the back of the fork, two spots again. Say it as you print: *spot, spot, stripe.* Hand her the cork on the third repeat.
3. Raccoon next. Wide dark and pale bands from a tube squashed flat, alternating down the strip like a ringed tail. She takes over after two.
4. Owl on the third strip: rings from the open end of a tube, rows of them, brown on brown.
5. Fox on the fourth: orange the whole length with the crumpled paper towel, then a white tip.
6. Hand her the last two strips with no pattern to follow. Those two are hers, printed however she likes. Lay all six flat to dry, wash hands, then walk the row and ask what comes next on the four you started.

> 💡 **Tip:** Print the first repeat and stop. She finishes the strip. A pattern she completed is one she can read back at you an hour later.

### 🌳 Get Outside: The Bark Rubbing Walk

Take paper and a fat crayon with the wrapper peeled off. Rub the crayon on its
side over paper held flat against four different trunks. Oak comes out deep
and ridged, birch close to smooth, and she feels the difference through the
paper before she sees it. January bark holds the wet, so work the sheltered
side of each trunk.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 7 months and passes things from hand to
> hand now. Give him a wooden spoon and a folded washcloth, the second set out
> to his side so he has to reach across his body for it. He keeps up a running
> commentary at both of them while Azlyn prints. On the walk, hold his open
> palm flat against a trunk for a second.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: Building the Forest Family

The strips are dry by one o'clock. Wrapped round a cardboard tube and taped,
each one stands up on the table by itself.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The printed strips
- Cardboard tubes, 4–6
- 1 roll masking tape
- Scissors (**adult**)
- Card scraps for ears, tails and beaks
- 1 permanent marker

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lay the dry strips out and ask which animal each one is. Take her answer even when the raccoon has turned into a zebra.
2. Wrap the fawn strip round a tube with the pattern facing out and tape the seam on the inside. She holds, you tape.
3. Cut ears, a tail and a beak from the card scraps. She draws the shape, you cut on her line.
4. Tape the pieces where she points, including the ears you would have put somewhere else.
5. Stand each animal on the table as you go so the row grows in front of her.
6. Write every name on the base in marker. Fox is a name and so is Bibby.
7. Give them an address: a shelf, a windowsill, the corner of the table. They stay there all week.

> 💡 **Tip:** Tape the seam inside the tube where her fingers cannot find it. A seam she can pick at comes apart by Sunday, and these four have a week of work ahead.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives

- Draw faces onto plain tubes and skip the printing.
- Sort the dried strips by color, then by pattern.
- Line the animals up by height and photograph them.

### 🌍 Around the World: Japan, *Shinrin-yoku* 🌲

In Japan, *shinrin-yoku* means forest bathing, a walk with nothing to achieve.
No route, no distance, no finish. You go in among the trees and use your
senses: what you can hear, what you can smell, how bark feels under a hand.
Japanese doctors send patients out to do it, and the walk counts as treatment.

> **✨ Try it:** Green tea and a rice ball, eaten under a tree with your coats
> on. Ten minutes of sitting and listening counts as the whole practice.

> *Did you know?* Japan's forestry ministry invented the word *shinrin-yoku* in
> 1982, and the name came before any of the science did.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Patterns are the beginning of algebra.**
> Azlyn printed spot, spot, stripe, spot, spot, stripe down a paper strip this
> morning, and the work underneath it is what a nine-year-old does with x. A
> pattern is a rule that holds while the pieces change. Once she can say what
> comes next on the raccoon strip, she can find the rule in a row of cups, a
> clapped rhythm or a line of numbers, because one skill sits under all three.
> Researchers who track early math find pattern work predicts where a child
> lands later, alongside counting, and it adds something counting does not.
> You never have to name it. Print the first repeat, hand her the cork, and
> let her carry the rule to the end.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Kreston is 7 months and everything he reaches goes in his
> mouth. Corks, tube ends and card scraps go up out of reach the moment you
> stop watching, on a shelf and not the table edge. Washable non-toxic paint
> only. The scissors stay with you.

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Tearing the Trunk

**🧰 You need:** brown paper bags or a flattened box · masking tape · the cleared wall.

1. Tear the paper into long ragged strips instead of cutting it. A torn edge looks like bark, and her hands can do it all.
2. Lay the strips end to end along the floor and stand her beside the line.
3. Ask where the branches should start. Tape a tab on the wall at that height and leave the pile until 9:15.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Tree That Fills the Wall

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- Brown paper bags, a flattened cardboard box, or the plain side of wrapping paper
- 1 roll masking tape
- Scissors (**adult**)
- Green paper, newspaper or magazine pages for leaves
- 1 permanent marker
- The Forest Family animals, or any toy animals

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Tape the torn strips to the wall as a trunk, floor to shoulder height, wider at the bottom. Overlap them until no wall shows.
2. Add branches from the shorter strips. She decides how many and where, and crooked ones stay crooked.
3. Tear leaves from the green paper or magazine pages and tape them along the branches in handfuls.
4. Draw a line along the baseboard and run roots below it. That line is the ground.
5. Sort the animals. Ask where each one lives: roots, trunk, branches, top. Tape a card shelf at that height and stand it there.
6. Write the four layers up the wall in marker: **roots, trunk, branches, top.** Read them out with her, bottom up.

> 💡 **Tip:** Build it at her height. A tree she can reach the top of is one she keeps adding to all week, and the ceiling end is yours.

### 🌳 Get Outside: Choosing Her Tree

Walk the route and let her pick one tree to be hers for the year. She has to
give a reason, and any reason counts. Put both arms round the trunk to see
whether they meet, then count the big branches. Photograph her against it
today, the last Saturday in January, and again in April, July and October.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 7 months and tracks Azlyn across a room
> now. Lay him on his back under the wall tree to look up at the leaves. Tape
> one green paper leaf at the height of his hand, off to one side so he has to
> reach across his body for it. He crumples it and passes it over.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Forest Tour

She knows where every animal on that wall lives. This afternoon she says it
out loud to an audience that cannot get up and leave.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The wall tree
- 1 flashlight
- 1 cardboard tube, for a megaphone
- Paper scraps and 1 crayon, for tickets
- Your phone, to record one tour for Joseph
- A chair each for the teddies, and a cushion on the floor for Kreston

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Set the chairs in a row facing the tree with a teddy in every one. Kreston sits at the end, propped against the cushion.
2. Make tickets. She scribbles on the paper scraps and hands one to every seat, Kreston included.
3. Give her the tube and the flashlight. The tube is her voice and the flashlight is her pointer.
4. Take a seat and be the audience. Who lives at the top. Why does nobody live in the roots. What happens up there when it rains.
5. Let her be wrong. Owls in the roots is a claim, and a claim beats a correct answer copied off you.
6. Record one tour start to finish with no retakes. Joseph gets it tonight.
7. Swap over. You give the tour, and she corrects you before you finish the first sentence.

> 💡 **Tip:** Ask for the tour twice with an hour in between. The second runs longer and adds something she worked out in the gap.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Explaining to someone else is how understanding gets locked in.**
> Azlyn giving the tour is doing harder work than Azlyn being told. To explain
> the tree she has to put the parts in an order, find words for the joins, and
> run into the bits she does not have. Researchers call it self-explanation,
> and children who talk their way through a problem hold on to it longer than
> children who watched a demonstration. Her audience does not need to follow a
> word. Kreston and three teddies do the job as well as an adult, because the
> value sits in the telling. Ask one real question at the end and let the
> silence run while she hunts for the answer.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Kreston sits on the floor against a cushion for the tour and
> never on a dining chair. At 7 months he goes over sideways with no warning.
> The leaf taped at his height and every loose end of masking tape come away
> the moment you stop watching, because both go straight to his mouth. The
> scissors stay with you, up on a shelf between cuts.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Forest Floor Tray

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Spread the ground first: soil, sand, or brown paper torn small. Her hands do it. Sweep at the end.

7. Lift the tray onto the table when she has finished. It comes down again tomorrow.

> 💡 **Tip:** Rain is the best thing here and she will ask for it eleven times. Set the number before you start, three rains, and hold the line, or the tray is soup by two.

### 🌅 Opening Activity: The Four Feet

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Track Trail

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- All-purpose flour, 2 cups, in a sieve or a small bowl
- 2 paw shapes cut from a sponge or thick card
- Dark paper or a dark towel for the prints to show up on
- 3 clues: a chewed leaf, a tuft of wool, a few crumbs
- 1 hidden animal from the Forest Family, or any toy animal
- 1 dustpan and brush

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Open the door and stand back. Let her look before anybody says a word.
2. Follow it from the start on hands and knees. Count the prints out loud and let her take over.
3. Stop at every clue and ask the same question: what does this tell us. A chewed leaf says it eats plants, a tuft of wool says it is furry, crumbs say it went past the trash can.
4. Find the animal at the end. She lifts whatever it is hiding under and says the name.
5. Now walk it backwards, animal to door, and ask which way it was going. This is the hard part.
6. Sweep up together. Keep half a cup of flour back for after lunch.

> 💡 **Tip:** Do not tell her who it was at the start. Guessing wrong for ten minutes and finding out at the end is the whole activity.

- 1 flashlight
- 1 dustpan and brush

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**

4. Make three arrow cards. She draws them, you tape them at Joseph's eye height rather than hers.

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Evidence and inference, a big idea learnable at three.**
> A chewed leaf on the floor and a fox at the end of the hallway are different
> kinds of thing, and Azlyn spent the morning getting from one to the other.
> Scientists call the move inference: the leaf is what you can see, the fox is

> within a week.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: Waking the Bears

The cave is dark and lined by lunchtime. Azlyn runs the afternoon on a wooden
spoon and a pan lid, and winter ends whenever she says it does.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**

- 1 wooden spoon and 1 pan lid
- 1 blanket each
- 1 cup of warm milk and a snack, for eating inside
- Books about bears or winter, 2–3
- 1 flashlight

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Everybody hibernates. Into the cave or under a blanket, eyes shut, and hold it while you count to twenty out loud.

3. Go round again with her holding the spoon. She decides how long winter lasts, three seconds the first time and two minutes the fourth.
4. Change one rule each round. Sleep standing up. Sleep in a pile. Sleep with the flashlight on. Sleep and snore.
5. Creep past a sleeping bear without waking it, then swap and let her creep past you. Getting caught is the fun of it, so get caught.
6. Take the milk and the snack into the cave and eat in there with the flashlight on.
7. Read the books in the cave and finish with everybody asleep and nobody banging anything.

> 💡 **Tip:** Let her hold the pan lid for most of the rounds. Deciding when winter ends is a lot of power for a three-year-old.

> climbing the walls.

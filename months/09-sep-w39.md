## Week 39: Letters & Sounds 🔤

---

### 🎯 What This Week Is About

Not the alphabet from A to Z. Five letters, chosen because they're hers — the ones in her name — plus the sounds that start the words she uses most. Everything this week is physical: letters cut out and rebuilt, drawn in salt, painted with water on a fence, stuck on the fridge and the door and the bin.

The most important thing in this week is that none of it is a test. She's four. If she can hear the first sound in *sock* by Thursday, that's a genuinely good week's work.

---

### 🧒 What Azlyn Will Learn

- **The letters in her own name**, in order, by rebuilding them.
- **How a letter feels to make** — the movement, in salt, water, and chalk.
- **Rhyme detection** — noticing when two words end the same.
- **First sounds** — that *sock* and *sun* begin the same way.
- **That the marks on signs, packets, and doors say something**, and someone put them there.

- Twenty-six is too many, and five is plenty
- Print in the real world teaches more than print in a book
- Repetition of one sound all day does more than a lesson on it

## 🌟 Day 267: The Name Puzzle 🌟
**📅 Friday, September 24, 2027**
**Theme:** Letters & Sounds

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting: Whose Name Is Longest? (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — Main Event: The Name Puzzle (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:30 AM** — Get Outside: **The Giant Chalk Name** (60 min)
- **11:30 AM–12:00 PM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Name Puzzle
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- Thick card or cut-up cereal boxes
- A thick marker
- Scissors, adult and child
- An envelope or small box, labelled with her name
- Crayons and stickers
- Optional: a photo of her, to glue to the box

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Write A Z L Y N in big capitals**, one letter per card, each card about the size of your hand. Capitals first — they're easier to tell apart and easier to make.
2. She decorates each letter however she likes. **Every letter should end up looking different from every other one** — that's what makes them memorable, and it's why decorating isn't a filler step.
3. Lay them in order and say the name slowly, touching each card as you go. **Then mix them up and let her rebuild it.** Leave the correct version visible to copy from the first few times.
4. When she can do it with the model, turn the model over and let her try from memory. If that's too hard, turn it back. No fuss either way.
5. **Then make Kreston's**, and yours, and Dad's. Compare lengths — whose name has the most letters? Line the names up one above another.
6. **Point out the shared letters.** N is in both Kreston and Azlyn. That single observation is worth more than any amount of alphabet recitation.
7. All the cards live in the envelope and come out every day this week.

> 💡 **Tip:** Don't correct a backwards or upside-down letter this week. Reversals are completely standard until six or seven and correcting them now mostly teaches her that letters are a thing you get told off about.

### 🌳 Get Outside: The Giant Chalk Name
Chalk her name across the whole drive or path, one letter per paving slab, letters as tall as she is. **Then she walks the letters** — heel to toe along the shape of each one, in order. Making a letter with her whole body is a genuinely effective way of learning its shape.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Make Kreston's letter cards too and give him the K. He'll carry it round, chew the corner, and possibly bring it to you. Him knowing that this object is *his* — that it means him — is the first step of the same process.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Her own name is the doorway and it doesn't generalise for a while.**
> Almost every child learns the letters in their own name first, well before the rest — because it's the one word that's genuinely about her, appears everywhere in her life, and gets pointed out constantly. Her name is doing more literacy teaching than any programme will.
> The part that surprises parents is how narrow it stays at first. She may recognise the A in *Azlyn* and not recognise it in *apple*, because for a while the letter belongs to the name rather than existing on its own. That isn't a failure to transfer; it's the normal sequence. Pointing out her letters when they turn up elsewhere — on a sign, a cereal box, her brother's name — is exactly what widens it.

## 🌟 Day 268: The Salt Tray 🌟
**📅 Saturday, September 25, 2027**
**Theme:** Letters & Sounds

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — Main Event: The Salt Tray (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:30 AM** — Get Outside: **Water Writing on the Fence** (60 min)
- **11:30 AM–12:00 PM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Salt Tray
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- A shallow tray or baking dish with a rim
- A bag of table salt, or fine dry sand
- Dark paper or card to line the base — the contrast is what makes it work
- Yesterday's letter cards
- A thick-handled paintbrush, a chopstick, and a finger
- A small jug, for pouring the salt in and out

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Line the tray with dark card and **let her pour the salt in** — a thin, even layer, about the depth of a grain of rice. Pouring it herself is half the fun and good for her hands.
2. **Shake it flat.** This is the reset button and she should be the one who does it, every time.
3. Start with shapes, not letters. A line, a circle, a zigzag, a spiral. Five minutes of this makes the letters easier.
4. **Then one letter card at a time**, propped up next to the tray to copy. A first — she draws it with a finger, then shakes it flat, then does it again.
5. Work through A, Z, L, Y, N over the session. **Two or three goes at each, then move on.** Stop the moment interest drops rather than pushing for the whole name.
6. Swap the finger for a paintbrush handle, then a chopstick. Each one demands a slightly different grip.
7. **Let her write on your back with a finger** and you guess the letter. Then swap.

> ⚠️ **Kreston will eat the salt if he reaches it.** A large amount of salt is genuinely dangerous for a toddler, so this is a table activity, not a floor one, and the bag gets put away.

> 💡 **Tip:** Straight-line letters are much easier than curved ones. L, Y, and N will come before A and Z, and that ordering is about hands rather than intelligence.

### 🌳 Get Outside: Water Writing on the Fence
A bucket of water and a fat decorator's brush. She paints letters, shapes, and whole scribbled paragraphs on the fence, the wall, the path — dark while wet, gone in five minutes. **The vanishing is the point:** nothing to keep, nothing to get wrong, and enormous arm movements.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston gets his own small brush and a shallow bowl of water — a couple of centimetres, and never left with him. Painting water onto a warm path is a complete activity at 16 months.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Tracing in salt removes the fear of getting it wrong.**
> A pencil on paper makes every attempt permanent, and children who are aware of that — which is most of them by four — start refusing to try. Salt, water on a fence, and chalk in the rain all share one property: the evidence disappears, so there's nothing to be judged on.
> That's why these mediums produce ten times the practice of a worksheet. She'll write the same letter twenty times in salt and once on paper, and the twenty is what builds the motor memory. Anything erasable is doing more for her handwriting than anything permanent, and it stays true for years.

> 💛 **From Joseph:** She wrote her whole name on the fence in water and then stood watching it dry, which I think she found more interesting than the writing.

## 🌟 Day 269: Rhyming Soup 🌟
**📅 Sunday, September 26, 2027**
**Theme:** Letters & Sounds

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:10 AM** — Main Event: Rhyming Soup (55 min)
- **10:10–10:30 AM** — Snack (20 min)
- **10:30–11:30 AM** — Get Outside: **The Silly Rhyme Walk** (60 min)
- **11:30 AM–12:00 PM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep

### 🎨 The Main Event: Rhyming Soup
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- A large pot or mixing bowl
- A ladle or big wooden spoon
- Objects in rhyming pairs, gathered in advance: a cat and a hat, a sock and a rock, a mug and a toy bug, a star and a toy car, a spoon and a picture of a moon
- A tea towel to hide them under
- A tray

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lay all the objects out on the tray and name each one together. **No rhyming yet.** Just make sure she has the word for each.
2. Put the pot in the middle. **Sing the same line every time** — *"I'm making lots of silly soup, I'm making soup that's silly, I'm going to put it in the fridge to make it nice and chilly. In goes… a CAT!"* The repetition is what makes this work.
3. Drop the cat in, then ask: **what else rhymes with cat?** She finds the hat and drops it in.
4. Ladle it round, tip it out, start again with a different pair.
5. **Then the harder version:** you say a word with no object at all — *sock* — and she has to find something in the room that rhymes. Prompt freely; this is much harder.
6. **Then the hardest version, and only if she's flying:** you say a word and she makes up a rhyme herself, including nonsense words. *Sock, bock, zock.* Nonsense counts fully.
7. Finish with the silliest soup she can make and read it back to her as a list.

> 💡 **Tip:** If she can't hear rhymes at all today, don't push it and don't worry. Rhyme detection typically arrives somewhere between four and five, and reading nursery rhymes is a better route to it than drilling pairs.

### 🌳 Get Outside: The Silly Rhyme Walk
Rhyme everything you pass, badly. *A tree — a bee — a knee.* *A car — a star.* **Wrong rhymes are funnier and work just as well.** She'll take over within five minutes and start correcting you, which is the sign it's landing.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston gets the pot and the ladle once the small objects are cleared away, plus a few large safe things to put in it. Filling and emptying a pot at 16 months is exactly what she was doing at his age.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Hearing a rhyme and making one are two different skills, months apart.**
> Recognising that *cat* and *hat* go together comes first. Producing a new rhyme for *cat* on demand is substantially harder — it requires holding the ending fixed while searching for a new beginning, which is real working memory plus a search through everything she knows.
> So a child who says "hat!" confidently and then goes blank when asked for another one hasn't lost the skill; she's hit the edge of it. The useful response is to supply one and let her judge it — *does "bat" work? does "dog" work?* Judging is easier than generating, and it keeps her in the game while the harder half develops.

## 🌟 Day 270: The Sound Post Boxes 🌟
**📅 Monday, September 27, 2027**
**Theme:** Letters & Sounds

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — Main Event: The Sound Post Boxes (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:30 AM** — Get Outside: **The S Scavenge** (60 min)
- **11:30 AM–12:00 PM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Sound Post Boxes
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- Three shoeboxes or tubs
- Paper and a thick marker for the labels
- Tape
- A basket of small objects: sock, spoon, stone, star, mug, mouse, milk carton, magnet, ball, book, brush, banana
- A tray

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Three boxes, three letters: S, M, B.** Write each letter huge on a label and tape it to the front. Stick a picture next to it — a sun, a mouse, a ball.
2. **Say the sound, not the name.** *Sss*, not "ess". This matters more than almost anything else this week — *sss-o-ck* blends into a word and *ess-oh-see-kay* doesn't.
3. Tip the basket out. Pick up the sock, say it slowly, stretching the first sound: **ssssssock.** Which box?
4. She posts it. Then the next. **Say every word slowly for her** — the stretching is doing the teaching, not the sorting.
5. When a box is full, tip it out and say all the words in a row. Hearing four *sss* words together is when it clicks for a lot of children.
6. **Then the hard round:** she closes her eyes, you say a word with no object, and she points at the box.
7. Leave the boxes out for a few days and let her add things she finds.

> 💡 **Tip:** Choose sounds you can stretch — s, m, f, n, l, r. Sounds like b, t and k can't be held, so they're much harder to hear in isolation and they're the wrong place to start.

### 🌳 Get Outside: The S Scavenge
One sound, one walk. **Find ten things that start with *sss*.** Sticks, stones, signs, steps, a snail, somebody's shed. Count them off on fingers. Then pick a new sound on the way home and find three more.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston gets a fourth box with no letter on it and a handful of large objects, and posts them in while you narrate what each one is. Hearing the words is his entire job at 16 months, and it's a real one.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **The first sound in a word is the easiest one to hear, and that's where reading starts.**
> Breaking a spoken word into its separate sounds is the single strongest predictor of how easily a child learns to read — and it's an *ear* skill, developed entirely without print. The first sound is the easiest to isolate, the last sound comes next, and the middle is hardest by a long way.
> Which is why this is worth doing in a box-and-basket game rather than on paper. Stretching *ssssock* out loud, in the car, in the bath, at dinner, does the work. And it's why the sound matters more than the letter name at this stage — *sss* is what she'll eventually need to blend, and "ess" is a fact she'll have to set aside later.

## 🌟 Day 271: The Alphabet Book 🌟
**📅 Tuesday, September 28, 2027**
**Theme:** Letters & Sounds

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:20 AM** — Main Event: Make the Alphabet Book (65 min)
- **10:20–10:35 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:35–11:35 AM** — Get Outside: **The Five-Letter Collection** (60 min)
- **11:35 AM–12:00 PM** — Free Play (25 min)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep

### 🎨 The Main Event: Make the Alphabet Book
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 6–8 sheets of plain paper, folded in half
- A stapler, or string and a hole punch
- Card for the cover
- Crayons and markers
- Old magazines, catalogues, and junk mail to cut from
- Glue sticks and scissors
- Glue for anything collected outside

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **This is not an A-to-Z book.** It's a five-page book: **A, Z, L, Y, N** — her letters, one page each. Say that out loud to her; a book she can actually finish is the point.
2. Fold, staple, and let her title it. *Azlyn's Book of Letters.*
3. **One page at a time.** Write the letter huge at the top, capital and lowercase side by side. She traces over both with a crayon.
4. Then fill the page with things starting with that sound — drawn, cut from a magazine, or stuck on from outside. **Four or five per page is plenty.**
5. Say each one slowly as it goes on, stretching the first sound.
6. **Some pages are hard, and that's useful.** Z will nearly defeat you. Zip, zebra, zoo, and then nothing — let her see you struggle with it and invent a solution.
7. Read the finished book back to her cover to cover, then put it on the shelf with the real books.

> 💡 **Tip:** Let her include things that are wrong, and don't fix them silently. If she puts a cat on the L page, ask her to say *cat* and *L* out loud and see what she thinks. If she keeps it, keep it.

### 🌳 Get Outside: The Five-Letter Collection
Out with the book and a bag. **Find one real thing outside for each of her five letters** — an acorn for A, a leaf for L, a nut, a yellow leaf for Y. They get glued straight onto the pages when you get home, which makes the book hers in a way drawings don't.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Make Kreston a one-page book: **K**, with four photos of things he knows — his cup, the dog, his shoes, Azlyn. At 16 months a book about his own life will beat any picture book you own.

> 🌍 **Around the World — Korea: *Hangul***
> Most alphabets grew up by accident over centuries. **Korean *hangul* was invented on purpose**, in the 1440s, by King Sejong — who wanted a writing system ordinary people could learn in a morning rather than the thousands of Chinese characters only scholars had time for. The letters are shaped like the mouth and tongue making the sound: ㅁ is a closed mouth, ㄱ is the back of the tongue lifting.
> **✨ Try it:** Make letter biscuits. Any plain dough, rolled into ropes and bent into her five letters, baked and eaten in order. Eating the letters of your own name is a surprisingly big deal at four.
> *Did you know?* Korea has a public holiday every October to celebrate its alphabet. Very few countries have a day off for their writing system.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Twenty-six is too many, and five is plenty.**
> The instinct is to teach the alphabet as a set, in order, A to Z. But letter *names* in sequence are just a song, and a child who can sing it often can't pick out a single letter on a page. Meanwhile, twenty-six symbols is a lot of undifferentiated new information to hold.
> Depth beats coverage at this stage. Five letters she can find, make, trace, and hear the sound of are worth more than twenty-six she can chant. And because they're the letters in her name, she has a reason to care about them — which is doing more than any teaching method. The rest arrive over the next two years, and they arrive faster once the first few are solid.

## 🌟 Day 272: The Sign Maker 🌟
**📅 Wednesday, September 29, 2027**
**Theme:** Letters & Sounds

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:20 AM** — Main Event: The Sign Maker (65 min)
- **10:20–10:35 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:35–11:35 AM** — Get Outside: **The Sign-Spotting Round** (60 min)
- **11:35 AM–12:00 PM** — Free Play (25 min)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Sign Maker
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- Sticky labels, or card and masking tape
- Thick markers
- Card for the bigger signs
- Crayons for decorating
- Scissors
- A basket to carry it all round the house

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Walk the house together first** and pick what gets labelled. Keep it to things she uses: DOOR, BED, CUP, CHAIR, BIN, MILK, TOYS, KRESTON.
2. **You write, she copies.** Write each word clearly on one label; she copies it underneath on a second one, however it comes out. Both go up.
3. **Capitals throughout.** Easier to form and easier to tell apart.
4. She sticks each one on. **She decides where** — the label goes where she thinks the word belongs, and putting MILK on the fridge rather than the carton is a perfectly good decision.
5. **Then the bigger signs**, which are the fun part: HER ROOM, KNOCK FIRST, SHOP OPEN, QUIET PLEASE. Let her invent the rules and enforce them.
6. Read the labels aloud whenever you pass one, for the rest of the week and beyond. **Point at the word as you say it** — the pointing is what teaches that print goes left to right.
7. Leave them up until they fall down.

> 💡 **Tip:** Resist adding more than about a dozen. A house covered in words becomes wallpaper within a day; twelve labels she chose herself stay visible.

### 🌳 Get Outside: The Sign-Spotting Round
Out to look at real print. Street names, shop signs, STOP, the bus number, the opening hours on a door, the price on a chalkboard. **Ask what each one is telling us**, not what it says. Signs have jobs, and that idea is the one worth landing.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Read Kreston's labels to him as you pass — his cup, his chair, his name on his door. He can't read a word of it, and hearing the word attached to the object every day is exactly how his vocabulary is expanding right now.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Print in the real world teaches more than print in a book.**
> Environmental print — the labels, signs, packets and logos she moves through every day — is where most children first work out that marks carry meaning. It's the reason a four-year-old can "read" a supermarket logo years before she can read a sentence.
> Labelling the house takes that instinct and points it at ordinary words. She isn't decoding CUP; she's learning that the word sits with the object, stays the same every day, and was put there deliberately by a person. That last part matters — print isn't weather, it's something people make. Pointing at the words as you read them, left to right, delivers the rest of the lesson without a single sentence of instruction.

- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — Free Play (15 min)
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch & Nap Prep

### 🎨 The Main Event: The A Party
**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- Card, markers, and stickers for invitations
- Apples, apricots, avocado, animal crackers
- Paper and crayons for the A banner
- The week's letter cards, alphabet book, and sound boxes
- Balloons or bunting if you have them
- A basket for the A collection

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Announce the rule at breakfast: today, everything starts with A.** For Azlyn. She'll test it immediately and that's the game.
2. **Invitations first** — to Dad, to Kreston, to a grandparent on the phone, to a cuddly toy. She writes A on each one.
3. Make a banner. A's the whole length of it, decorated as differently from each other as she can manage.
4. **The A collection:** ten minutes going through the house filling a basket with things beginning with the *a* sound. Apple, arm (she'll try), ambulance, animal, aeroplane, alligator, art.
5. **A games:** Amazing Animals, where everyone moves like an animal beginning with A; and Applause, where she gets clapped for each thing she found.
6. **Snack is the party.** Apples, apricots, avocado on toast, animal crackers. Name each one out loud, stretching the *a*.
7. Finish by reading her alphabet book to the guests, starting with the A page.

> 💡 **Tip:** She'll bring you things that don't start with A all day. Say the word out loud with her rather than saying no — *"orange… or-ange… does that start like Azlyn?"* Testing the rule is how she learns it.

### 🌳 Get Outside: The A Hunt
Out with the basket. **Find A's in the world, both kinds** — things that start with the sound, and the actual letter on signs, plates, and shopfronts. Tally them. Ten is a good haul; twenty is a very good walk.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is at the party and he gets the animal crackers. Every time she names an A thing, hand it to him and say the word. **He learns words by hearing them attached to objects he's holding**, which is precisely what's happening all morning.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Repetition of one sound all day does more than a lesson on it.**
> Twenty minutes of focused teaching about the letter A is worth less than an ordinary day in which the sound turns up eighty times in eighty different contexts — food, games, signs, a banner, her own name, her brother being handed an animal cracker.
> This is how young children learn nearly everything: not through instruction but through saturation, across contexts, with no test at the end. It's also why theme days work so well at this age and why they don't need to be elaborate. Pick one thing, put it everywhere, don't check whether she's got it. She will have, by teatime, without either of you doing anything that felt like work.

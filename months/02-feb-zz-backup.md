# 🌧️ February — Backup Days & Extra Ideas

February is the month the plan breaks. Ice sets in for three days. One of the
children comes down with something, and the day on the page wants an oven and a
walk you have no intention of taking. These five pages cover that. Two of them
are full days for weather that keeps you in, two are for a child who is ill,
and the last one is a list you can open with ten minutes of warning and no plan
at all. Everything on these pages runs on what is already in the house, because
the weather that cancels the walk cancels the store run with it. Use them in
place of any day this month and put the printed one back in the week whenever
you feel like it.

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 1 — The Course Through the House

> *For the morning everything outside is glazed. Ice down the front steps, ice
> across the sidewalk, and no stroller is crossing that. She has energy for a
> two-mile walk and nowhere to spend it, so the hall becomes the walk.*

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Stack the sofa cushions at one end of the hall, and put the masking tape, a wooden spoon and a saucepan lid on top of them.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: Clearing the Runway
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: Six Stations Down the Hall (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Ten Laps (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: The Judge's Chair (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🛋️ Down Time: Cushions, a Blanket and Something on the Screen (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: The Forks
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: Rebuilding Station Three
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: The Long Warm Bath
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Clearing the Runway

**🧰 You need:** the hall, or the longest run of floor in the house · masking tape · 1 envelope and a pen.

1. Walk the length of it together and take out everything that would hurt to land on. The plant, the basket of coats. She carries what she can into another room and you take the rest.
2. Tape a line across the floor at each end. She decides which one is the start and stands on it to make it official.
3. Ask her what should happen in the middle. Write every answer on the back of the envelope, the ones you can build and the ones you cannot. The stations at 9:15 come off that list.

### 🎨 The Main Event: Six Stations Down the Hall

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The cleared hall, taped at both ends
- Sofa cushions, 3–4
- 2 dining chairs
- 1 blanket or a bath towel
- 4 books, to weight the blanket corners
- 1 roll masking tape
- 1 cardboard box with the flaps cut off, or the laundry basket
- 1 saucepan lid and 1 wooden spoon
- The envelope of her ideas

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Station one is a straight line of tape down the middle of the floor, three steps long. Heel to toe, arms out, and the floor either side counts as water.
2. Station two is the cushions, spaced a small stride apart. She works out the gap that suits her legs, so lay the first two and hand her the rest.
3. Station three is the two chairs a body's length apart with the blanket over the top, weighted with a book at each corner. She crawls through it.
4. Station four is the box on the floor. In with both feet, out with both feet, and the box has to still be where it started.
5. Station five is hers. Take one thing off the envelope and build it however she describes it, down to the parts you would fix.
6. Station six is the finish. Hang the pan lid on a door handle at her shoulder height and give her the spoon. One tap and the lap is over. Run the whole course yourself first, badly, while she watches.

> 💡 **Tip:** Build it too easy. A course she clears on the first go gets run
> twenty times, and she raises the difficulty herself by lunchtime.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Ten Laps

The sidewalk is a sheet of ice and the walk is off, so the forty-five minutes
go into laps. Tape a strip of paper to the door frame at the finish and let her
mark it after each one. Change one thing every couple of laps and say what it
is: backwards through station two, teddy under one arm, a cup of water carried
the whole way, feet only on the tape. Ten is the number to aim at, and she will
pass it. Sit down at the finish and be the person who is there every time she
comes around.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 8 months and crawling with a
> destination in mind, which is his version of the same work. Put him at one
> end of the cleared floor with something he wants at the other, well clear of
> the run while anybody is on it. He travels the length of the hall on his
> hands and knees and arrives pleased with himself. Let Azlyn be the thing at
> the far end and he goes twice as fast.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Judge's Chair

The course is built and she has run it thirty times. This afternoon she sits
down and everybody else runs it, under rules she makes up as she goes.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The course, still up from this morning
- 1 dining chair set at the finish line
- Paper and a marker for the scorecard
- 1 dish towel as the flag
- Everybody in the house
- The pan lid and the spoon

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Set her chair where she can see all six stations at once. That chair is the job and nobody else sits in it.
2. Make the scorecard together. One row per person, names written big while she watches, and she draws a face beside each one so she can find them again.
3. She says the rules out loud before anybody runs. Take all of them, including the rule that you have to hop at station four.
4. You go first. She drops the dish towel to start you and marks your row when you tap the lid. Get station three wrong on purpose and let her rule on whether it counts.
5. Everybody in the house takes a turn. Carry Kreston through on your hip, station by station, and let her mark his row too.
6. Swap over. She runs, you take the chair, and you read your marks back the way she read hers.
7. At the end she says who won. Leave the result standing, whoever it favors.

> 💡 **Tip:** Play it straight when she changes the rules mid-race. A judge who
> gets argued with stops judging, and the whole ninety minutes runs on her
> holding a job nobody is taking off her.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- One station only, the cushions, jumped until she is done with it.
- Move the whole course into her bedroom and leave the hall alone.
- Everybody runs it in slow motion, and last one to the lid wins.
- Take the course down together, station by station, and stop there.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** He does his lap on your hip with a hand on each
> station as you pass it, and Azlyn marks his row. After that, lay one cushion
> flat on the floor with something he wants on the far side. At 8 months a
> climb with a reason on the other side of it holds him for a long stretch, and
> he is working on getting a knee up onto things. Stay beside him for the
> tipping-over part.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Specific recognition beats general praise, every time.**
> Azlyn hears *good girl* several times an hour and it stops landing by
> Wednesday. Say the thing you saw instead. *You got both feet on that cushion
> and held your arms out to stay up* hands her a fact about her own body that
> she can use on the next lap. Children who get described stay with a hard task
> longer and take bigger risks inside it, because the words carry information
> and no verdict to live up to. Description is also cheaper on a morning when
> your patience has gone, since you are reporting what happened. She starts
> reporting back within a week, and her reports are worth hearing.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Check what is under and beside every jumping station before
> she starts. Radiators, door frames and the corner of a bookshelf are what
> hurt, so move the cushions rather than trusting her aim. The two chairs need
> a hand test for wobble before she crawls under them, and the blanket goes
> over the backs and not across her head. Keep Kreston out of the run while
> anybody is on it. A crawling 8-month-old cannot get out of the way of a
> three-year-old at full speed, and she cannot stop.

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 2 — The Lamps On at Eleven

> *For the wild one. Wind against the glass, snow going sideways and banking up
> on the sill, the lamps on all morning and dark again by five. Nobody is going
> out, and the weather is the thing she keeps looking at.*

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Put both flashlights on the kitchen counter and check they work. One flashlight and a charged phone covers the whole day if the other is dead, so nobody needs batteries. Leave a pen and an old envelope on the windowsill.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: Counting the Gusts
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: The Bangs and What Makes Them (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Parade That Beats the Wind (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: The Pretend Power Cut (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🔦 Free Play: Still No Lights (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: Collecting the Flashlights Into One Place
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: The Blanket Over Two Chairs
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: Every Lamp in the House, Switched On by Her
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Counting the Gusts

**🧰 You need:** the window the weather hits hardest · 1 old envelope · 1 pen.

1. Two chairs at that window and the lamp off behind you, so the glass shows the street instead of the room.
2. Every time the wind moves something out there, she makes a mark on the envelope. A branch, a trash can lid, the snow going flat across the road. Count the marks at the end of ten minutes.
3. Ask her which one was the biggest and what it hit. Tell her the noises in the house get their names at 9:15, and leave that sitting.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Bangs and What Makes Them

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The whole house, one room at a time
- Paper and a marker for the list
- 1 old towel, rolled
- Thick card or a folded cereal box, for wedging
- 1 roll masking tape
- 1 heavy book

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Start in the room with the most noise in it. Stand still, both of you, and wait for a bang without talking over it.
2. Track it down together. Put her hand flat on the thing while it is moving, so she meets the vent flap or the loose pane herself.
3. Name it and write it on the list in her words. *The clacky one. The one that goes whoosh.* Draw a small picture beside each so she can read the list back.
4. Fix what can be fixed from indoors. Card folded and wedged under the door that slams, the rolled towel along the bottom of the drafty one, a strip of tape across the rattling vent, a book against the blind that keeps knocking.
5. Walk the house again and listen. She reports which noises stopped and which ones beat you, and the ones that beat you get a circle.
6. Read the list back at the end, both columns. Six noises with names and causes hold better than one house full of banging.

> 💡 **Tip:** Answer the question she asks and stop there. *That is the fence
> gate, it swings when the wind pushes it* is the whole answer, and adding
> reassurance she did not ask for tells her there was something to be reassured
> about.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Parade That Beats the Wind

Nobody is walking anywhere in this, so put the noise indoors. Saucepan, wooden
spoon, an upturned plastic tub, a set of keys, feet. Parade the loop of the
house, kitchen to hall to living room and around again, loud on the straight
bits and silent through one doorway you nominate. Three laps loud, one lap on
tiptoe, then back to the window to see how much deeper the snow on the sill has
got and to work out who is winning, the parade or the weather. Kreston rides on
your hip through all of it.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 8 months and checks your face before he
> decides what a bang means. A gust that rattles a window will send his eyes
> straight to you, so let him see the same face you would wear on a still day
> and carry on with what you were saying. Sit him on your knee for the parade
> and let him hit the upturned tub with a flat hand while you hold it steady.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Pretend Power Cut

The lamps have been on since breakfast and the snow has not let up. At one
o'clock every switch in the house goes off on purpose, and the afternoon
happens in one room with two flashlights.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 2 flashlights, or one and a phone
- Blankets, 2, and every cushion in the room
- 1 pitcher of water and 2 cups
- The afternoon snack, whatever is in the cupboard that needs no stove

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Announce it at one and let her be the one who walks the house turning switches off. Lamps, kettle, the hall light. The heating stays on and she does not need to know that.
2. Work out together what a real one would need and go and collect it: flashlights, blankets, water, food that needs nothing done to it. She carries what she can and names each thing as it goes in the pile.
3. Pick one room, shut the door and build the camp on the floor. Cushions, blankets, both flashlights standing on end so the light bounces off the ceiling.
4. Hands in the beam. Show her one shape, a bird or a dog, then hand the flashlight over and let her invent the rest against the wall.
5. Eat the snack down there. Food on the floor in a half-dark room is most of the appeal, and it buys you twenty minutes.
6. Turn one flashlight off, then the other, and sit in the dark for as long as she wants. Four seconds counts. Talk the whole time so she can hear where you are.
7. At five, when the real dark arrives outside, walk the house together and let her put every lamp back on. Say the room name as each one comes up.

> 💡 **Tip:** Give her a flashlight of her own for the whole ninety minutes and
> do not ask for it back. Holding the light is what makes the dark hers instead
> of something happening to her.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- One flashlight, one blanket over two chairs, and read in there.
- Shadow shapes on the ceiling from flat on your backs.
- Keep the lights on and eat the snack under the kitchen table instead.
- Call somebody and let her tell them how deep the snow is getting.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** He comes into the camp in the carrier on your
> front for the dark stretch, where your voice arrives through your chest
> before it arrives through the air. At 8 months a room he knows with the light
> gone is a new room, and your heartbeat is the thing that makes it an old one
> again. Once the flashlights are on, sit him in the cushions facing Azlyn and
> let him watch the shapes move. He gets no flashlight of his own today.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Children take the reading off your face before they take it off the weather.**
> A gust hits the window and Azlyn looks at you before she decides how to feel
> about it. Psychologists call that social referencing, and it runs hard from
> the end of the first year: children read a trusted adult's face and voice and
> take the reading as information about the world. Kreston is doing the same
> thing at 8 months in its purest form. Your eyebrows carry more weight than
> your explanation does. Naming the noise and giving it a cause in a flat voice
> does more than any amount of *there is nothing to be scared of*, which
> arrives as a tone she can hear straight through. Handle the snow in front of
> her the way you would handle it alone.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Flashlights today, never candles, with a crawling 8-month-old
> and a three-year-old holding the light. Check the flashlight cases are shut
> tight and the battery compartments screwed down before either child touches
> one. Loose batteries are a medical emergency if swallowed, and a button cell
> burns through a throat in hours, so count them in and out and keep the spares
> in your pocket. Stay back from the windows while the wind is throwing
> anything around outside, and leave the trash cans, the porch chairs and the
> steps until it drops. Nobody goes out to clear snow with both children
> indoors on their own, and the front steps stay off limits to her until you
> have salted them.

## 🤒 Sick Day 1 — The Day That Asks Nothing

> *For the worst of it. Fever, no appetite, and a three-year-old who wants to
> be held and does not want to be moved. Nothing on this page needs setting up,
> cutting or cleaning, and every part of it can be abandoned halfway.*

### 🛏️ Before Anything Else

Make one nest and keep her in it. A pillow and her own blanket on the sofa,
the curtains half open for gray daylight, a cup with a lid, a bowl within
reach, a towel folded under her head that you can swap without moving her. The
day has one job, and the nest is where it happens. Cancel whatever was on for
today out loud in front of her, because she will hear you do it and stop
worrying about it.

### 🕰️ The Shape of the Day

There are no clock times today. Work off her instead.

- **Whenever she wakes.** Get fluids in before food. A cup with a lid and a straw, or a teaspoon if the cup is too much. Take her temperature once and write the number and the time on the back of an envelope you keep on the counter.
- **The first long stretch.** She lies down and you sit with her. This is the rest block and it is the day's main event.
- **When she asks for food.** Whatever she asks for, in a small amount, on a small plate. Toast, yogurt, a banana, plain crackers. Appetite goes first and comes back last.
- **Before the afternoon dip.** One low-demand thing off the list below, on a lap or lying down, for as long as it holds and no longer.
- **The dip itself, somewhere around mid-afternoon.** Fever climbs in the late afternoon most days. Expect the worst hour to land here and plan nothing across it.
- **The end of the day.** Warm bath if she wants one, skipped without argument if she does not. Early bed, in your bed or hers, whichever gets everybody the most sleep.

### 😴 The Rest Block

Give the middle of the day to lying still, and give it hours rather than
minutes. Take the tablet and the television out of the decision by leaving
them off from the start; a screen keeps a sick three-year-old awake and wanting
more of it. Lie down with her if she will not settle alone. She may sleep, and
sleep is what her body is asking you for. Read the same thing over her head in
a low voice while she drifts, and stop when she stops answering.

### 🛋️ Things She Can Do Without Sitting Up

**One Book, Four Times.** She picks it, you read it, she picks it again. The
repetition is the comfort, and a familiar book asks nothing of a tired brain.
Read it flat, at half your usual speed, and let her turn the pages when she
has a hand free.

**Her Whole Year on the Phone.** Open the photos and scroll backwards from
today with her on your chest. Name who is in each one and where it was.
Christmas, the museum, the box house in January. She narrates more than you
expect and it costs her nothing.

**Counting the Sips.** Fluids are the whole medical plan on a day like this
and she will refuse a cup. Count instead: ten sips, one at a time, out loud,
with a rest between each. Ice chips, watered juice, a popsicle from the
freezer, a spoon of yogurt. Little and often beats a full cup at any point.

**The Ceiling Story.** Both of you flat on your backs looking up. Start a
story about somebody her age and let it wander nowhere in particular. She joins
in when she has it in her and lets it run over her when she does not.

### 👶 Where Kreston Goes

Kreston is 8 months, crawling, and he will head for her the moment he is on the
floor, which is the one place he cannot be today. Ask somebody whose face he
already knows to have him for two hours if there is anybody to ask, and a
morning out is worth more to you today than any amount of tidying. A face he
has not met costs more than the two hours buy back at 8 months, so a familiar
one or nobody. Otherwise he goes where he can see you and not reach her: a
travel crib in the same room, the high chair pulled up beside you, or the
kitchen floor behind a gate with his own things. Keep his cup, his spoon and
her cup on different counters. He will catch it anyway, and washing your hands
between the two of them still shortens the odds. His naps hold their usual
times, and those two stretches are the only two-handed hours you get, so do
the things that need two hands then.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **The third day of a cold is the worst one for both of you.**
> Most of the ordinary winter viruses run to a shape: a day of it coming on,
> a hard middle, then a turn. Day three is where the fever tends to sit
> highest, where her sleep has been broken enough to show, and where your own
> reserves have gone, since you have been up for two nights and had no daytime
> to recover in. Knowing the shape is worth something. She is where she is
> meant to be at this stage, the clinging and the crying are the illness rather
> than a slide backwards, and the turn tends to arrive by the fourth or fifth
> day. Plan nothing across it and let the day cost what it costs.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Call the doctor rather than waiting it out for any of these:
> a fever over 104°F, a fever in a child who is hard to rouse or floppy, a
> rash that does not fade when you press a glass on it, trouble breathing or
> fast breathing at rest, a stiff neck, dry pants and no trip to the toilet in
> eight hours, or a fever running past three days. Trust yourself over the
> list if she looks wrong to you. Never give aspirin to a child, check the
> dose on the bottle against her weight rather than her age, and write down
> the time of every dose on the envelope, because you will not remember it at
> three in the morning. Kreston is 8 months and takes nothing at all without
> the doctor saying so, including anything left over from Azlyn. Ice goes in
> only while she sits up, crushed small under the back of a spoon, and whole
> cubes stay in the freezer, because a cube slides back on a flat child and
> blocks her airway.

## 🤒 Sick Day 2 — Bored, Not Better

> *For the mend. The fever has broken, she is complaining that there is nothing
> to do, and she is still one bad hour away from the sofa. The bar stays low
> and everything on this page can be done from a lying start.*

### 🛏️ Before Anything Else

Move the nest. Yesterday's pillow and blanket go from the bedroom to the sofa,
or from the sofa to a floor pile in whichever room you are working in. The
change of scene is most of the medicine today. Put a tray on the coffee table
within her reach with her cup, a plate of small food and whatever she is
carrying around this week, so she can start something without calling you in
from the kitchen.

### 🕰️ The Shape of the Day

Still no clock times. She will tell you where the edges are.

- **Whenever she wakes.** Bring the drink to her and watch how she takes it. Her hands and her voice tell you both more about how she is doing than any question you could ask.
- **The good stretch, which comes early.** The best hours today land in the morning, so spend them. One job with an end to it, then a book, then stop while she still has some left.
- **When she asks for food.** Appetite comes back sideways. Offer what she asked for yesterday and was refused, and let her leave most of it.
- **The rest block after lunch.** Shorter today and it still happens, whatever she says about it.
- **Before the afternoon dip.** The bath, warm and long, and back into pajamas afterwards even though it is the middle of the day.
- **The end of the day.** Same bedtime as a well day, and hold it. A child who slept all afternoon yesterday needs the shape back.

### 😴 The Rest Block

She will fight this one, because she feels better than she is. Take the
argument out by lying down with her yourself and putting a timer on for twenty
minutes, out loud, where she can see it. She can look at books, and her body is
horizontal for the whole twenty either way. Most children on the mend fall
asleep inside the first five minutes of an argument they were winning.

### 🛋️ Things She Can Do From the Sofa

**The Jobs She Can Do Lying Down.** Bring the work to her. A laundry basket of
socks matched into pairs on her stomach. The crayon box tipped out on a
cushion and put back in some order she invents. The silverware basket and a
tray, sorted into forks, spoons and the blunt knives, while Kreston is asleep
or behind the gate. One job at a time, and take the tray away when it is
finished so the finish is visible.

**The Spoon on the Mug.** Give her a teaspoon and a mug on the tray and one
rule: two taps means she needs you, and you will come every time. She uses it
eleven times in the first hour and about twice after that. A child stuck on a
sofa with no way to reach the adult in the next room shouts, and this stops the
shouting.

**The Tally on the Envelope.** Prop her where she can see out and give her a
pen and an envelope. One mark per car, or per bird, or per person with an
umbrella. Come back later and count them together, then start a second row and
see which row wins.

**The Sofa Menu.** She dictates what the house eats tonight and you write it
down as a menu, her words as they come. Read it back, let her change it twice,
and then cook whatever version she landed on. Being in charge of one real
decision does more for a bored convalescent than any activity.

### 👶 Where Kreston Goes

Kreston is 8 months and today he goes back on the floor near her, which is what
they both want. Set him up at the end of the sofa with something he can work at
on his own: a wipes box stuffed with a burp cloth to haul out, or a shallow box
of things too big to swallow. Azlyn can hand him things from where she is
lying, and passing something down to him is a job she will take on for twenty
minutes. Sweep the floor before he goes down, twice if the crayons came out.
Watch his own nose and temperature today, because he is four or five days
behind her on whatever she had, and stranger wariness makes him harder to hand
to anybody else the week he comes down with it himself.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Coming back to normal needs a smaller version of normal first.**
> Azlyn is bored, which is the clean signal her body has finished the work. The
> pull is to hand her the whole day back at once, and a full day today puts her
> flat again by four. Give her the shape without the load: the same meals at
> the same points, one job, one bath, the same bedtime, and nothing that runs
> longer than twenty minutes. Routine is what tells a small child which
> category today belongs in, and a recognizable day is how she works out that
> the ill part is over. Children on the mend hold their gains better when the
> markers come back before the demands do. Add the demands tomorrow.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Illness that turns back around is worth a phone call. A fever
> that clears and then returns, a cough that gets worse after several days of
> getting better, or ear pain arriving late all point at something on top of
> the first virus, and none of them is a wait-and-see. Playgroup, the pool and
> anybody else's kitchen wait until she has gone a full day without a fever and
> has eaten a proper meal. The silverware basket comes to her while Kreston is
> asleep or behind the gate, and the socks or the crayons take its place the
> moment he is back on the floor beside the sofa. Keep her cup and her spoon
> separate from Kreston's for the rest of the week and wash your hands between
> the two of them.

## 🎲 February — Extra Ideas

> *Ten minutes of warning and no plan. Everything here runs on what is already
> in the house, and none of it needs the weather to cooperate.*


> ⚠️ These run under the same rules as the rest of the month: an adult within

### 🎲 Alternative Activities

- **The Cupboard Nobody Sorts.** Everything out of one low cupboard, sorted her way, and back in before lunch.
- **The Weight Line.** Five cans from the pantry, lifted one at a time and lined up lightest to heaviest.
- **Statues Until the Song Stops.** Music on, everybody dancing, and she runs the pause button.
- **Hide the Spoon.** One wooden spoon hidden in one room, and warmer or colder called out from the doorway.
- **The Cushion Island Game.** Every cushion on the floor, and the floor between them counts as water.
- **The Big Pile of Shoes.** Every shoe in the house tipped out and matched into pairs, biggest to smallest.
- **Washing the Windows From the Inside.** A spray bottle of water, a cloth each, and her half of the glass is hers.
- **The Cold Doorstep Minute.** Coats on, sixty seconds on a salted step in the falling snow, back in for something warm.
- **The Phone Call to Somebody Far Away.** She dials, she talks, and you stay out of the conversation.
- **The Grocery List She Dictates.** She says what the house needs and you write every item down, including the four cakes.
- **Everything That Fits in a Shoebox.** A hunt through the house, and a long argument about the things that almost fit.

### 🎨 Alternative Arts & Crafts

- **Foil Animals on a Plate.** Kitchen foil squeezed into creatures that have to stand up on their own.
- **The Junk Mail Chain.** Strips looped and taped end to end until the chain crosses the room.
- **Two Crayons, Two Hands.** One in each fist on a big sheet, both arms moving at once.
- **The Sticky Wall.** Plastic wrap taped across a doorway sticky side out, and torn tissue pressed onto it.
- **The Drawing That Comes Through the Paint.** Heavy crayon first, then a watery wash over the top of it.
- **Tape Under the Paint.** Masking tape shapes stuck down, painted over, peeled off when dry.
- **Printing With What Is in the Fridge.** A halved potato, the end of a celery bunch and a fork, pressed into paint.
- **Soap Drawings on the Mirror.** A dry bar of soap on the bathroom mirror, wiped off with a warm cloth after.
- **Drawing With Her Feet.** Paper taped to the floor and a fat crayon held between her toes.
- **The Torn Cloud Sky.** White paper torn into lumps and glued across a page of gray.
- **The Family Drawn From Behind.** Everybody in the house drawn from the back of the head, and guessed at afterwards.

### 👶 Kreston's Turn

*He is 8 months this month: crawling or shuffling, banging two things together,
and wary of any face he does not know.*

- **The Muffin Tin and Four Cloths.** A washcloth pushed into each cup for him to haul back out.
- **Two Lids and a Loud Floor.** A pan lid in each hand on the kitchen floor, banged together for as long as you can stand it.
- **The Face That Comes Back.** A dish towel over your head, then off, ten times, and he pulls it the last few.
- **The Cold Spoon From the Fridge.** A metal teaspoon chilled for ten minutes, his to hold and chew while his gums work.

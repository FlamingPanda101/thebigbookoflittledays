# 🌧️ October — Backup Days & Extra Ideas

October will take a week off you. Driving rain sets in before eight and stays,
the wind strips the last of the leaves onto the path, and the dark arrives by
six, so a day built around a walk has nowhere to go. Then somebody wakes up
hot and the whole page stops applying. Four complete days sit here for those
mornings, two for weather and two for illness, and you swap one in for the day
you were meant to be on. The extras page at the end is for the ten minutes
when you have nothing planned and need something now.

---

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 1 — Wet Windows

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Push the sofa around to face the biggest window and leave a roll of masking tape, a dry cloth and the crayons on the sill.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: Every Sound the Rain Makes
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: The Race Down the Glass (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Twelve Laps of the House (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: The Chain That Gets Shorter (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🎬 The One Movie of the Day (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: The Forks
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: Racing the Drops Alone
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: Bath With the Rain Still Going
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Every Sound the Rain Makes

**🧰 You need:** the run of the house · 1 sheet of paper · 1 crayon.

1. Walk her around the downstairs and stop at four windows. Twenty seconds of listening at each one with nobody talking, then she gives you a word for it and you write her word down.
2. Add the places that are not windows: the roof over the back door, the gutter, the drain by the step, the mail slot with the wind coming through it.
3. Read the four words back and let her pick the loudest window. That is where the two of you sit at 9:15.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Race Down the Glass

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The biggest window in the house
- 1 dry cloth
- 1 roll masking tape
- Paper and 1 crayon for the tally
- 2 cushions on the floor under the sill

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Wipe the inside of the pane clear with the cloth so she can see out through it. Run a strip of tape across the top as the start line and another along the bottom as the finish, then put both cushions on the floor under the window.
2. Pick a drop each at the top of the pane. Hers first, and she says which one is hers out loud before either of you moves.
3. Race them. A drop sits still for a long moment and then goes in one rush, and a drop that swallows the one above it gets heavy and takes off, which she will spot before you mention it.
4. One mark on the paper per race, her column and yours. Ten races, then read the tally back.
5. Change one thing. Press a strip of tape flat across the middle of the pane on the inside, then race a fat drop against a small one and have her call the winner out loud before either of them moves. The tape line gives her a halfway mark, and a gust throws a fresh sheet of water down the glass every minute or two and starts the whole race over.
6. Leave the tape up. She runs it on whoever comes through the door for the rest of the day.

> 💡 **Tip:** Let her choose first every round. She picks the fattest drop, the fattest drop wins most of the time, and a three-year-old who is winning stays at that window for the full hour.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: Twelve Laps of the House

The wind is driving the rain flat against the house, and a three-year-old who
has not moved her whole body by eleven takes it out on the afternoon. Set a
lap and walk it once together: kitchen, hall, living room, back to the kitchen
door. Load a basket of clean laundry at the start and let her deliver one item
per lap to wherever it belongs, counting the laps out loud as she goes. Twelve
of them, with the last three run backwards or on tiptoe. Open the back door at
the end, keep your own hand on it against the wind, and let her put a flat
palm out into the rain for as long as she wants it. Then shut the door on the
weather.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 16 months and copies whatever your
> hands are doing, so give him the second cloth and let him wipe the glass at
> his own height while Azlyn works at hers. He has ten words or more and *wet*
> arrives fast at a window like this one. He walks the laps with the pair of
> you and carries one sock the whole way around.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Chain That Gets Shorter

The morning ran on something that lasted eight seconds a go. This afternoon
she makes a thing that runs until March.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- Junk mail, catalogs or wrapping paper, enough for 22 strips
- 1 pair of adult scissors (**adult only**)
- 1 glue stick, or masking tape
- Crayons and 1 permanent marker
- A hook or a door frame to hang it from
- Your phone, for the photo

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Cut 22 strips, a finger wide and about as long as her hand and a half. You cut, she counts them into piles of five as they come off the pile.
2. She colors them. Say what the chain is for while she works: one link a week, torn off on a Sunday, and the last one comes off in the spring.
3. Count forward and mark the links that have something in them. A star on Christmas, and a big four on the week she turns four.
4. Loop the first strip and glue it shut. Thread the next through and glue that. Her hands do the gluing, your thumb holds each joint while it takes.
5. Stop at every fifth link and count the whole thing back to the beginning with her.
6. Hang it from the door frame with the bottom link at her reach and the rest of it above Kreston's.
7. Photograph her holding all twenty-two, then tear the first one off with her, because she will not last the evening otherwise.

> 💡 **Tip:** Glue one spare link into a ring on its own and give it to her to tear whenever the wait gets too much. The chain on the door frame keeps its count and she gets her hands on something.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- Ten links instead of twenty-two, and the chain runs out at Christmas.
- Draw twenty-two boxes on one sheet and color one in a week.
- Tape the strips into a chain with no glue and hang it in her room.
- Skip the chain and let her cut junk mail into scraps for an hour.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** Stick a length of tape to the table sticky side
> up and give him three strips of his own to press onto it. At 16 months he
> works with both hands at a job like that one and comes back to it twice. He
> will haul the whole chain down off anything he can reach, so his goes on the
> table and hers goes high.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Five months is longer than she can picture, and that's exactly why it's worth doing.**
> Azlyn holds yesterday, today and a vague after-this. Five months sits a long
> way outside that, so the chain does the holding for her. She cannot feel
> March coming, and she can see twenty-two rings and watch the row get shorter
> under her own hands, one Sunday at a time. Children given a countdown they
> can touch wait better than children told to be patient, because a wait she
> can hold in her hands is a wait she can measure. Expect her to forget the
> chain for three weeks and then find it. Tear the missed links off together,
> count what is left, and carry on.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** No chair, stool or box goes under that window. The two floor
> cushions are the only thing there, and they stay flat. Move the chairs well
> back before the racing starts and check the window catch is shut, because
> Kreston pulls himself up on whatever is nearest at 16 months. The scissors
> stay in your hand and go back in the drawer between cuts.

## 🌧️ Bad Weather Day 2 — Damp Gets Into Everything

### 🌙 Prep Tonight
> Put an apple, half a lemon, four saucers and the masking tape on the table, and find six washcloths or six socks that match each other.

### ⏰ At-a-Glance Schedule
- **8:00–9:00 AM** — 🌅 Opening Activity: The Oldest Thing in the Kitchen
- **9:00–9:15 AM** — Morning Meeting (15 min)
- **9:15–10:15 AM** — 🎨 The Main Event: One Apple, Four Saucers (60 min)
- **10:15–10:30 AM** — Snack (15 min)
- **10:30–11:15 AM** — 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Bucket and the Kitchen Floor (45 min)
- **11:15–11:45 AM** — Free Play (30 min)
- **11:45 AM–12:00 PM** — 🤸 Wiggle Time (15 min)
- **12:00–12:30 PM** — Lunch
- **12:30–1:00 PM** — 📚 Book Time
- **1:00–2:30 PM** — 🎨 Second Main Event: Where the Laundry Dries First (90 min)
- **2:30–3:30 PM** — 🛁 The Long Bath, Early (60 min)
- **3:30–4:00 PM** — Snack & Free Play
- **4:00–4:15 PM** — 🧹 Her Job: The Round of the Washcloths
- **4:15–5:00 PM** — Quiet Play: The Four Saucers Again
- **5:00–5:45 PM** — 🛁 Wind-Down: Two Books Under One Blanket
- **6:00 PM** — **Dinner**

### 🌅 Opening Activity: The Oldest Thing in the Kitchen

**🧰 You need:** the fruit bowl · the vegetable drawer · 1 tray.

1. Hand her the tray and hunt the kitchen for the oldest thing in it. The soft apple, the bendy carrot, the potato with white shoots on it, the heel of the bread.
2. Lay them in a row and let her put them in order, worst first. Ask what she thinks happened to each one and write nothing down yet.
3. Tell her the apple in the middle of the table gets cut into four at 9:15 and that the four pieces are going to different places.

### 🎨 The Main Event: One Apple, Four Saucers

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 apple, or a potato, a pear or a banana, whichever the kitchen has
- 1 sharp knife and 1 cutting board (**adult only**)
- 4 saucers
- Masking tape and 1 marker for the labels
- Half a lemon, or a spoonful of bottled lemon juice or vinegar
- 1 drinking glass
- Plastic wrap
- Paper and crayons

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. She picks the apple. Cut it into four on the board while she stands back on the far side of the table, and lay one piece cut side up on each saucer.
2. Label the four with tape and marker: WINDOW, LEMON, GLASS, FRIDGE. She sticks the labels on and draws the four cut faces while all of them still look the same.
3. Squeeze lemon over the second, or spoon the bottled juice or the vinegar over it, then stand the glass upside down over the third, wrap the fourth and put it in the fridge. The first sits bare on the windowsill.
4. Go back to them after the snack, then after lunch, then at four. Each visit she says which one has changed the most, and you write the time beside her answer.
5. Line all four up beside her morning drawing at four o'clock. The bare one has gone brown, the sour one has held its color, and she can see it without any help from you.
6. Leave them out on a high shelf for the week. The change from here on is slow and it is the interesting part, so put a look at them into the day for the next few mornings.

> 💡 **Tip:** The browning starts inside twenty minutes. Say nothing about the sour saucer, and let her be the one who works out that one of the four is holding out.

### 🏠 Instead of Getting Outside: The Bucket and the Kitchen Floor

The rain is coming at the windows sideways and nobody is walking anywhere
today, so give her the heaviest indoor job in the house. Half a bucket of
warm water, a squirt of dish soap, one cloth each, both of you on your knees
on the kitchen floor. Push the chairs into the hall first and let her work a
square at a time. Wringing a cloth out is hard work through the hands and
shoulders, and forty minutes of it settles her better than the walk would
have. Dry each square behind you with a towel as you go. Finish by standing
back at the door together to look at the whole floor.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 16 months and copies a chore before he
> understands it, which makes this his morning. Give him his own damp cloth and
> a chair leg to work on. The bucket goes up on the counter the second you stop
> using it, because a 16-month-old can drown in the water left in the bottom of
> it.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: Where the Laundry Dries First

Six wet cloths go up in six places around the house and one of them wins. The
last one is still damp at bedtime, which is the whole of October in one
afternoon.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 6 washcloths or 6 socks, all the same
- 1 bowl of warm water
- Masking tape and 1 marker for the numbers
- Paper and crayons for the chart
- The clock on the stove
- 1 towel for the floor under each one

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Soak all six in the bowl and let her wring each one out as hard as she can. Wring them again yourself, because hers come out heavy.
2. Number them 1 to 6 on tape labels and let her stick one on each.
3. Walk the house and pick six places together: on the radiator, over the back of a chair, along the banister, flat on the windowsill, over the shower rail, and one in the middle of the living room floor. She chooses, you hang, and a towel goes on the floor under each one to catch the drips.
4. Six boxes on the paper, one per place, and she draws the spot in each box so she can read her own chart.
5. Check on the hour, every hour. Her hand goes flat on each cloth and she calls it wet, damp or dry, and a dry one gets its box colored in.
6. First dry wins. Ask her why that one and take her answer as it comes, then put her hand on the radiator and on the living room floor and let her feel the two together.
7. Last thing before dinner, walk the round once more and find the one that has not moved at all.

> 💡 **Tip:** Put one cloth somewhere with no chance at all. The place that fails teaches her as much as the radiator does, and she picked it herself.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- Three cloths instead of six, checked twice.
- Hang the real laundry and let her decide where every item goes.
- Paint two sheets of paper and dry them in two different rooms.
- Sort the whole laundry basket into piles by who it belongs to.

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** He stacks four blocks at 16 months, so give him
> four dry washcloths of his own to pile up and knock over at the far end of
> the room. He copies chores, so hand him one to carry to the radiator with
> you. Check the radiator with the back of your own hand before either of them
> goes near it.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Rotting is the part of nature children are usually protected from, and it's the interesting half.**
> A brown apple goes in the trash before Azlyn gets a proper look at it, and
> the furry one at the back of the fridge gets thrown out while she is in
> another room. She sees food arrive and she sees it disappear, with the middle
> edited out. Leaving four saucers on a shelf for a week hands the middle back
> to her. Rot is the same process as the compost pile and the windfalls going
> soft in the grass, and she meets it here at a size she can watch on a
> windowsill. Give her the disgust as well. Being revolted by something and
> going back for another look is a good afternoon at three.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** The knife is adult work and it goes back out of reach the
> moment the apple is quartered. Once a saucer goes furry it leaves the house
> whole, apple and all, with nobody opening it up for a sniff, because mold
> spores hurt small lungs. Keep all four saucers on a high shelf: Kreston will
> eat what is on them. A wet kitchen floor is slippery, so dry behind you as
> you work and keep both children out of shoes and socks on it.

## 🤒 Sick Day 1 — The Day She Stays Put

### 🌙 Before You Come Downstairs
> Fill a lidded cup and stand it where she can reach it without sitting up. Put a bowl, a towel and a change of clothes by the sofa, and clear the low table down to bare wood.

### 🕗 The Shape of the Day

> *No clock times today. She sets the order and you follow her.*

- **Whenever she wakes** — the nest goes up around her before anything else happens
- **Before she asks for it** — water within reach, the bowl on the floor, the big light off
- **The first stretch** — Yesterday, Told Back
- **When she goes quiet** — the rest block, and you stop too
- **After the first long sleep** — The Warm Cloth and the Cold One
- **When she asks for food** — dry toast, and half of it comes back
- **Before the afternoon dip** — What Today Was Going to Be
- **The afternoon dip** — the rest block again, however long it runs
- **When the light goes** — bed early, and skip the bath

### 🛋️ Whenever She Wakes: The Nest You Build Around Her

**🧰 You need:** the sofa · 2 pillows · the blanket she asks for · 1 lidded cup · 1 bowl · 1 towel.

1. Build it around her where she has landed. Moving a sick three-year-old to a better spot costs more than the better spot is worth.
2. Everything she needs goes inside one arm's length: the cup, the bowl, the towel folded under it, one soft thing, one book she knows by heart.
3. Turn her so she faces the window and the door. She wants to see who comes in and she has nothing left for turning her head.

### 🗣️ The First Stretch: Yesterday, Told Back

Tell her yesterday, in order, out loud, with her eyes shut. Start at waking up
and go through the whole thing, and put three things in wrong on purpose. She
corrects you with one word from under the blanket, which is all the talking
she has in her. Stop the second she stops answering. A story she already
knows, told by a voice she knows, asks nothing of her at all, and it beats a
screen for a child whose head hurts.

### 😴 The Rest Block

This is the main event of the day. Curtains half shut, lights off, nothing
playing, and you within reach of her hand. Sit down and put your own phone
face down, because a sick child settles faster with a still adult in the room
than with a busy one going in and out. She may sleep for two hours. Let the
whole plan for the day go if she does. Sleep is the work, and everything else
on this page exists to fill the gaps around it.

### 🧼 After the First Long Sleep: The Warm Cloth and the Cold One

Two washcloths in a bowl, one wrung out warm and one wrung out cool. She
chooses which one she wants and where it goes: her forehead, the back of her
neck, her wrists, the soles of her feet. She swaps them as often as she likes
and it costs her one hand and no sitting up. Say the two words as you hand
them over and let her run the whole thing from where she is lying.

### 🕯️ Before the Afternoon Dip: What Today Was Going to Be

Walk her through the day she is missing. Say what would be happening now, and
now, and at half past two, and give her the whole list: no getting dressed, no
walk, no swimming, Kreston in the other room. She says yes or no to each one
and some of them she will mind about. Finish on when it all comes back, which
is a day or two, and say that part in the same voice as the rest.

> 👶 **Where Kreston Goes:** He is 16 months and he copies everything, which
> today means climbing on her, drinking out of her cup and putting her
> washcloth in his mouth. A sick three-year-old cannot hold him off. Get him
> out of the house for the morning if anybody can take him, and if nobody can,
> put a gate across the doorway and set him up on the near side of it with his
> own basket, his own cup with a mark on it, and a job he already knows. Wash
> your hands between the two of them, every time, and keep their cups apart.

### 🎯 If She Wants Something Else
- The same book read four times, with her choosing where you stop.
- Photographs on your phone of her own last month, named one at a time.
- One hand in a bowl of warm water on a towel on her lap.
- Nothing at all, for a whole hour, with you sitting there.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Absence is harder to notice than presence, and it has to be pointed out.**
> Azlyn knows today is wrong and cannot tell you what is wrong with it. A thing
> that is there announces itself, and a thing that has gone gives off nothing
> at all, so the whole strangeness of the day reaches her as a mood she cannot
> name. Say the gaps out loud and the mood gets a shape she can hold: no
> clothes, no walk, no swimming, Kreston behind the gate. Three-year-olds
> settle once an odd day has a description, even a description they hate. Name
> what is missing, say when it comes back, and stop there. She will raise it
> again at dinner, and you give her the same answer in the same words.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Fluids matter more than food today, so small sips often, and a
> three-year-old who has kept nothing down for hours or has not passed urine
> since morning needs a call to the doctor rather than another hour of
> watching. Get help now, whatever the hour, if she is hard to wake, floppy,
> breathing fast or working at every breath, if her neck is stiff or the light
> hurts her eyes, or if a rash stays visible when you press the side of a glass
> against it. Check the dose on the bottle against her age and weight, write
> the time of every dose on a scrap by the sink, and never give aspirin to a
> child.

## 🤒 Sick Day 2 — Mending and Restless

### 🌙 Before Anything Else
> Move the nest into a different room from yesterday's, open the curtains the whole way, and put a shoebox with a lid where she can see it.

### 🕗 The Shape of the Day

> *Still no clock times. She has more in her than yesterday, and less than
> she thinks.*

- **Whenever she wakes** — A Different Room to Be Ill In
- **Before she asks for it** — the cup filled, the blanket where she can kick it off
- **The first stretch** — The Box for Next Time
- **When she flags** — the rest block, and she will argue about it
- **After the first long sleep** — Telling Somebody on the Phone
- **When she asks for food** — whatever she names, in a smaller amount than she asks for
- **The afternoon dip** — the rest block again, shorter, eyes open allowed
- **The long stretch before dinner** — the tray on her knees and no plan for it
- **When the light goes** — bed at the usual time, however much she slept

### 🛋️ Whenever She Wakes: A Different Room to Be Ill In

**🧰 You need:** her blanket · 2 pillows · 1 lidded cup · one thing she has not had out in a week.

1. Move the nest to a room she was not in yesterday. Day two in the same corner is the longest day this house has.
2. Curtains all the way open. Yesterday wanted the light down and today wants her to see the weather.
3. Put the thing she has not seen in a week within reach and say nothing about it.

### 📦 The First Stretch: The Box for Next Time

**🧰 You need:** 1 shoebox with a lid · 1 marker · paper and crayons · whatever is already inside the nest.

1. Give her the job in one sentence: the box is for the next person in this house who gets ill, and she is the one who knows what helps.
2. She names things and the two of you start with what is already on the blanket around her. Three trips out of the room at most, and you take everything she says, including the one that makes no sense at all.
3. Stop wherever she stops. Lay what she has on the blanket and let her put it in the order it gets handed over. A box with three things in it and the lid on works as well as a box with seven.
4. She draws a card for whoever opens it, and you write her words on it while she talks.
5. Lid on, her name and the date across the top in marker, and it goes on a shelf where the next sick person can find it. Say out loud who it might be.

### 😴 The Rest Block

She is on the mend and she will fight this, so give it a shape she can accept.
Curtains half, lights low, one long story or one audiobook, and no getting up.
An hour flat on her back does the work even with her eyes open the whole time,
and a child who skips it on day two is the child who goes backwards on day
three. Lie down yourself if you can. Say when it ends before it starts.

### ☎️ After the First Long Sleep: Telling Somebody on the Phone

Being ill is a story and she wants to tell it. Call a grandparent, or Joseph
at work if he can pick up, and hand her the phone with the speaker on so she
does not have to hold it. She tells them what happened, how much she brought
up and where the bowl is, and she will get most of it wrong and enjoy every
part of it. Two minutes is plenty. Take the phone back before it turns into
work.

> 👶 **Where Kreston Goes:** He has either had this already or he is three days
> from it. At 16 months he copies whatever she does, so keep their cups, their
> cloths and their spoons apart and wash your hands between them. He wants back
> in the room today and she has no patience left, so give him the floor at the
> far end with a job of his own: a basket to fill and tip out, or a cloth while
> you wipe. Nothing out of her box goes on the floor, because half of it is
> mouth-sized for him.

### 🎯 If She Wants Something Else
- Every crayon in the house tipped onto a tray and sorted into a jar of stubs and a box of good ones.
- The photographs on your phone from her birthday, named one at a time.
- A card for whoever she is missing, drawn on her knees and mailed this week.
- Her own bed made up on the sofa by her, with you handing her the pillows.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **A collection with a purpose behaves differently from a collection.**
> Azlyn gathers for the pleasure of gathering, and a heap of gathered things
> holds her for about four minutes. Give the heap a job and the whole activity
> changes shape. She has to picture a person who is not in the room, work out
> what that person will want, and hold both of those in her head while she
> chooses. Purpose is what turns picking things up into deciding, and the
> deciding is the harder half. Say who the box is for before she starts, keep
> saying it, and let her put in the things you would leave out.
> She is imagining somebody else's bad morning, which is a large thing to ask
> of a three-year-old and worth the afternoon.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Keep her home until a fever has been gone for a full day
> without medicine in her, whatever she says about how well she feels. A cough
> that gets worse once she has started to mend, or a fever that comes back
> after two clear days, gets a call to the doctor rather than another day on
> the sofa. Everything in the box stays bigger than Kreston's mouth or stays up
> on a shelf, because that box ends up on the floor by Friday.

## 🎲 October — Extra Ideas

> *For the morning you open this book with ten minutes and no plan. One line
> each, nothing to prepare, everything already in the house.*


> ⚠️ These run under the same rules as the rest of the month: an adult within
> arm's reach of any water, small parts up off the floor while Kreston is on
> it, and anything taped across a doorway down and in the trash before he
> crawls through.
### 🎲 Alternative Activities
- **The Draft Hunt** — a strip of paper towel held along every door and window to show where the wind is getting in.
- **Hands on Everything Cold** — flat palms on the window, the fridge door, the floor and the radiator, ranked coldest to warmest.
- **Lamps On Before Dinner** — she walks the house switching every light on as the gray comes down and picks the room she likes lit best.
- **One Branch, Counted** — count what is still hanging on one branch from the window, then count the same branch tomorrow.
- **Wettest to Driest** — every pair of shoes in the house lined up along the hall in order.
- **The Mail on the Mat** — the day's junk mail split into a pile to keep, a pile for the recycling and a pile she may tear up.
- **The Same Three Puddles** — the same short walk two days running, checking whether each puddle grew or went.
- **Everybody's Coat Pockets** — turned out onto the table, and she works out whose coat is whose from what falls out.
- **Three Cups, One Toy** — a toy hidden under one of three cups, shuffled on the table, and she keeps her eye on it.

### 🎨 Alternative Arts & Crafts
- **The Wet Sky** — clean water brushed over the whole sheet first, then color dropped in to run where it wants.
- **Pale Crayon on Dark Paper** — the street after dinner, lit windows drawn in white and yellow on a dark sheet.
- **Junk Mail, One Color Only** — every scrap of one color torn out of the pile and glued into a single block.
- **The Potato Stamp** — half a potato cut into a shape by you, dipped in paint and pressed until the page is full.
- **Drawing the Rain** — a whole page of nothing but the lines it makes coming down the glass.
- **The Paper Bag Face** — a face drawn on a lunch bag that fits over her hand and gets a voice.
- **Foil Over Something Rough** — aluminum foil pressed down onto a doormat, a brick or a coin until the pattern comes through.
- **The Torn Tree** — a brown paper trunk, torn scraps for the few leaves left up, and the rest of the scraps on the ground.
- **Painting With What Isn't a Brush** — a fork, a cork, a crumpled paper towel, a leaf off the step.
- **Salt on the Wet Sky** — a pinch of table salt dropped onto the wet wash from two lines up and left to dry into blotches.
- **A Card for Whoever Is Ill** — folded, drawn, and walked around or dropped in the mailbox this week.

Every day this week hands Azlyn a job that somebody in your town does for a living. She carries a mail route in a bag on her shoulder, draws the street from above and walks the map she drew, works a half-hour night shift by flashlight, and runs a lending library off the bottom shelf. Each job turns up with its own kit (a bag, a sign, a badge, a stamp), and the kit is what makes her believe in the work. Friday's bag comes back off the hook on Thursday, loaded with six cards for the six helpers she marked on Saturday's map.

Labor Day lands on Monday and gets a light touch. The morning washes the summer off the buckets and shuts them in a crate for the year, then Azlyn makes a picnic for the people who work in this house, serves it, and no adult gets up for an hour. Tuesday comes in hot, so that day bends around the heat: cold experiments in the morning, swimming at half past two, the middle of the day indoors.

🧒 What Azlyn will learn

The Bag That Holds the Whole Round

⏱️ 60 min🧰 7 items

You need: 1 sturdy paper grocery bag with handles, or a cloth tote, 1 old belt, a bathrobe cord or 3 feet of thick string, Packing tape, 1 permanent marker, The six wrapped parcels from 8:00, Newspaper, 4 or 5 sheets, Kitchen scales, if you have them

The Depot in the Hallway

Six houses go down the hallway after lunch, and every parcel carries a number that has to match one of them.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 8 items

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Carrying her own pack changes the walk.

Azlyn will ask to be carried on an empty walk and cover half a mile with a loaded bag on her shoulder. A load hands her a job, and a job keeps a three-year-old moving, because the walk now has a point that belongs to her. Weight through her shoulders and back sends steady information about where her body is, and that settles her more than talk does. Keep the bag under a tenth of what she weighs, about three pounds, and let her load it herself. Parcels going to somebody else beat a bag of her own toys, every time.

⚠️

Safety: The bag strap. Keep it short enough that it cannot go over Kreston's head at 14 months, and take it off her before she climbs anything. The six numbered parcels are choke-size for him and they look like food, so they stay in her bag or in your hand and get counted back in before he is down on the floor. What he carries is a rolled dish towel, wider than his fist, and it comes back to your hand every time. Untie the string from the basket the moment the round finishes. September sun still burns on a walk with no shade: hat on, cream at eight, cream again at eleven.

The Crossing She Runs Herself

The crossing goes on the driveway after lunch and she works it. Everybody else does as they are told.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 7 items

You need: Thick chalk, 1 large cardboard circle, cut last night, 1 wrapping paper tube, or a wooden spoon, Red, yellow and green paper, or paint, Packing tape, Toy cars, The stroller or a ride-on

  1. Chalk the crossing across the top of the driveway, well in from the street: six fat bars, her width apart, with a waiting box at each end. She draws, you keep the line straight.
  2. Tape red paper to one face of the circle, green to the other, then tape the circle to the tube, right round the join. Finger paint it instead if you would rather.
  3. Tape a small yellow circle to the tube below the sign. Yellow means get ready, and she decides what counts as ready.
  4. Push the toy cars along a chalked road towards the crossing and let her stop them with the red face.
  5. Then real traffic: you with the stroller, Kreston on a hand, anybody else in the house. She holds it up and everybody obeys.
  6. Swap. You take the sign, she takes the stroller, and now she watches for the color instead of showing it.
  7. Sign indoors at the end, and the crossing goes onto the big map. She crayons six fat bars across the road beside the label she made this morning, so it is on the map as well as the driveway.

💡Obey her the first ten times with no commentary. A sign held up by a small person still stops a grown-up.

The Jar Lantern for After Dinner

The lantern has to be dry and lit by seven, so it gets built at one and left in a warm window while you are out.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 9 items

You need: 1 clean glass jar, no lid, Tissue paper or parchment paper, Flour-and-water paste, 3 tbsp of each, 1 small bowl for the paste, 1 brush, 20 inches of string, 1 battery tealight (a small flashlight does the same job), 1 small bag for the late walk, 1 lidded cup and a drink for the walk

  1. Mix the flour and water in the small bowl until it looks like thin custard. She stirs, and it wants to be smooth.
  2. She tears the tissue into palm-sized pieces. A torn edge lies flatter on the curve than a cut one.
  3. Paste the jar with the brush, press the pieces on, then paste over the top. Two overlapping layers, and leave the rim bare.
  4. Tie the string round the neck under the rim, both ends knotted into a short handle. Test the knot over the table.
  5. Stand it on a warm windowsill and leave it there while you are out.
  6. After dinner, drop the battery tealight in and turn the kitchen light off.
  7. Pack the small bag for the walk: the second flashlight and something to drink. She carries the bag, an adult carries the lantern.

💡Pajamas, shoes and out of the front door at seven is the part she tells people about. The whole thing takes fifteen minutes.

Four Tiles That Turn

The four card squares match the ones on the panel and each gets the same design. Turning them makes the pattern.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 5 items

Matching the Blue Exactly

The winning blue thing comes off the windowsill onto the table, and she makes its color on a saucer.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 6 items

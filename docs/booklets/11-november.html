On the Farm 🚜

November 5 – November 11, 2027 · Days 309–315

On Friday you tape a farm onto the living room floor and it stays there until Thursday. She gets fields, a track and a muddy yard, and every toy animal in the house moves in. The days after that hang something new on the same farm: a cardboard cow with a glove for an udder, a pig in a wig, three houses for the wolf to blow down, five animals wearing her name on card tags. Print runs under all of it. The five letters of her name, the sound at the front of a word and the big word on a milk carton each arrive attached to something she built herself.

Thursday is Veterans Day. Shearing day runs as written, the farm gets packed into a box that afternoon, and the walk out to the shed passes whatever flags are up on your street. Stop at one and tell her whose day it is. A minute covers it, and she will ask again next year.

🧒 What Azlyn will learn
  • The five letters of her name. A, Z, L, Y, N on a gate sign, on five ear tags, drawn in salt and called out at the gate.
  • The sound at the front of a word. She sorts animals and kitchen objects onto three towels by how each word starts.
  • Rhyming pairs. Pig and wig, hen and pen, goat and coat, matched by ear and then built out of the toys she owns.
  • Print does a job. She reads the big word off the milk, the oats and the flour, then stands the real food under each one.
  • Cutting, gluing and threading through five ear tags, five woolly sheep and a bag of fleece she shears herself.

Milk, Everywhere 🥛

Cows are one option out of many. Mongolian families milk mares and ferment it into airag, poured for guests all summer. Across the Himalayas the milk comes from yaks and goes into salty butter tea. India produces more milk than any other country, and much of it comes from water buffalo, thicker and sweeter than a cow's.

Naming the Farm

The farm has fields, a track and a muddy yard and no name. She fixes that this afternoon.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 8 items

Milking the Glove

⏱️ 60 min🧰 8 items

You need: 2–4 dishwashing or disposable gloves, 1 straight pin or a needle (adult only), 1 large cardboard box for the cow, Packing tape and a permanent marker, 1 bucket or a deep pitcher, Warm water, 1 low stool, 2 old towels

  1. Towels down first, one under the working spot and one folded by the door.
  2. Build the cow. Box on its side, flaps taped shut with packing tape, a face on one end in marker. She picks the head end.
  3. Fill a glove with warm water, tested on the inside of your wrist. Twist the wrist end over and tape it shut hard.
  4. Prick four fingertips once each with the pin over the sink, because a cow has four teats. Leave the thumb. Tape the glove under the box so all four fingers hang at her sitting height.
  5. Stool under the cow, bucket under the glove, her on the stool. Show her the grip once: thumb and finger closed at the top, then squeeze down. Pulling gets nothing.
  6. Refill and run it again. Three gloves is a morning's milking, and the last one can have eight holes.

💡She will try to hold the bucket and milk at the same time. Let her, and let it go over once.

The Tray You Can Rub Out

The salt tray is the calmest thing in this book. Clear the table and set it up while she watches.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 6 items

You need: 1 baking sheet or roasting pan with a lip, Fine salt, 2 cups (fine semolina or all-purpose flour works the same), Dark card or foil to line the tray, 1 chopstick, or the wrong end of a paintbrush, Her name written big on a card, 4–5 animal picture cards or drawings

  1. Line the tray with the dark card, or foil shiny side up, so her lines show black.
  2. Pour the salt in and let her level it with the flat of her hand. As deep as a fingernail. Any deeper and the lines close behind her finger.
  3. Stand the name card at the top of the tray. She copies the first letter with one finger, straight into the salt.
  4. Shake the tray flat. Do it again. She can run it thirty times.
  5. Hand her the chopstick when her finger tires. It draws thinner and slower.
  6. Lay the animal cards along the edge and let her draw those too.
  7. Leave the tray set up. She comes back to it at 4:15 on her own.

💡Say nothing about backwards letters. A backwards Z at three is standard and mileage fixes it. Correct it now and you teach her the tray is a test.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Tracing in salt removes the fear of getting it wrong.

Hand a three-year-old a pencil and paper and the mistake stays on the page in front of her. Azlyn knows that, and a fair share of the wobble in early writing comes from a child who has decided she cannot do it. Salt has no memory. She draws an A, it comes out backwards, she shakes the tray and the A is gone. She has nothing to rub out and nobody to show it to. Her finger in salt feeds her more than a pencil does, the drag and the grain arriving through her skin. Fifteen attempts in salt cost her nothing.

⚠️

Safety: The pin is yours. Make every hole yourself and count it back into your hand at the end. Warm water, never hot, tested on your wrist. A bucket with water in it is a drowning risk for a 17-month-old, so it never sits on the floor unattended and it gets emptied when the milking stops. Keep the second towel down; the wet floor is a slip risk for everyone. Two cups of loose salt is a poisoning risk swallowed, so the tray works at the far end of the table with Kreston in the high chair, and the salt goes back in its box and up out of reach when the 4:15 quiet play ends, the tray staying at the far end of the table until then.

The Pig in a Wig

⏱️ 60 min🧰 7 items

You need: Toy animals, or animals drawn on card and cut out, Wool scraps or cotton balls for the wig, 1 sock or a fabric offcut for the coat, 1 small box for the pen, 1 toy truck, or a box on a string, 1 roll masking tape, 1 permanent marker

  2. The goat gets the coat. Sock over the body, head out of the top, and she does the pulling.
  3. The hen gets a pen. Box on its side, hen in, and write PEN on the front in marker while she watches each letter arrive.
  4. The duck gets the truck. She loads it and drives it the length of the room, saying both words every trip.

The Barn Dance

She has the moves, the music and the caller's job. Push the sofa back and take the rug up before anybody puts boots on.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 7 items

You need: Card, 4 pieces, 1 marker, Music on a speaker or a phone, 2 wooden spoons, 1 scarf or a dish towel each, 1 broom, Rain boots or boots, indoors, on purpose

  1. Ask her for four dance moves. Stamp, spin, clap, jump, whatever comes. Draw each one on a card in marker while she names it.
  2. Boots on indoors. She will not believe you at first.
  3. Music on, four cards in her hand. She is the caller. She holds one up, shouts it, and the room does it until she holds up the next.
  4. Give two people the wooden spoons to bang the beat on the floor, and swap them halfway through.
  5. A scarf or a dish towel each. Swing them overhead on the spins, and let go on purpose once.
  6. Lay the broom flat on the floor. Dance up one side and down the other without touching it, then hop over it on the beat.
  7. Last dance slow, big light off, everybody swaying. She calls that one too.

💡Take her calls even when they come three seconds apart and nobody can keep up. Being obeyed by a room full of adults is the whole appeal.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Hearing a rhyme and making one are two different skills, months apart.

Azlyn will tell you that pig and wig sound the same long before she can hand you a word that rhymes with hat. Spotting a rhyme is a matching job and she is good at it now. Making one asks her to hold an ending still in her head, run through everything she knows and test each word against it, and that arrives later on its own schedule. Give her the pairs ready-made and let her hear a few hundred of them. The night she offers you pig, wig, big, zig out of nowhere in the bath, she has crossed over on the strength of the hundreds.

Seven days in the kitchen, and the same question sits under all of them: what does a tool do that her own two hands cannot? 🧒 What Azlyn will learn
  • A tool does one job faster than her hands. She works thirty counts by hand, thirty counts with the tool, and calls the winner herself.

Whisking Until It Stands Up

⏱️ 60 min🧰 8 items

You need: 4 eggs, 2 large bowls, one metal or glass, 1 balloon whisk, 1 fork, A pinch of salt, 1 tablespoon sugar, 1 jam jar with a lid, the substitute if there is no whisk, 1 clean dish towel

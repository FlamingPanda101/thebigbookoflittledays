Colors of Summer ☀️

June 4 – June 10, 2027 · Days 155–161

Color is the one thing Azlyn has all the words for, so this week takes it apart. She mixes red into five pinks a dot of white at a time, freezes color and drags it across paper, drops it onto wet paper and gets a circle every time, leaves it out in the sun until it fades, and pours it bowl to bowl until all three go brown. Every day she guesses first and looks second, and you write down both.

Kreston turns 1 on Saturday. Day 156 gives him a banana cake he is allowed to wreck, one candle for ten seconds, a walk down the block on one finger, a pencil line on the door frame, and the museum after lunch. Azlyn chooses his icing color at breakfast and then has to keep her hands off the cake, which is the hardest thing anyone does all week.

🧒 What Azlyn will learn
  • Color is something you make. Red walks to pink in five steps, one dot of white at a time.
  • Guess first, look second. She calls what the cabbage water will do before the lemon goes in, and both answers go on the paper in the same pen.
  • Do it twice. The same red ice cube down a second sheet, to find out whether the world repeats itself when nobody changes anything.
  • Counting with a reason. Five blue and two red, dropped in with a teaspoon and spooned back out, gives her a color she can build again from her own card.
  • Things move without being touched. The sun pulling color out of paper over four hours, a balloon lifting paper it never reaches.

Colors Mean Different Things 🎨

Color carries a different message depending on where you stand. Across much of East Asia white belongs to mourning and funerals, while red belongs to weddings, luck and money. Languages divide color up in their own ways too: Russian keeps separate basic words for light and dark blue, the way English keeps red and pink apart, and the Japanese word ao covers a stretch English splits between blue and green.

Five Steps from Red to Pink

⏱️ 60 min🧰 8 items

You need: 6 strips of thick card cut from a cereal box, Washable poster paint in red, blue and yellow, White poster paint, 1 old plate as a palette, 1 brush, 1 jar of water, 1 cloth, 1 marker

  1. Rule three of the strips into five boxes each with the marker. Put the other three strips to one side, blank.
  2. Box one on the first strip gets red straight from the pot, no white at all.
  3. Scrape a little red onto the plate, stir in a dot of white, and fill box two. More white for box three, more again for four and five, until the last box has almost no red left.
  4. Rinse the brush in the jar, dry it on the cloth, and run the whole ladder again with blue, then with yellow.
  5. Stand all three strips along the windowsill. Ask which two boxes are hardest to tell apart and let her hold the strip to the light.
  6. Check the red strip with a finger at 10:15. It dries first, and it goes out with her.

💡Add the white in doses smaller than feel worth doing. A big spoonful jumps her from red to pink and loses the three steps in between.

The Summer Color Board

The strips are dry by lunch and the bag has not been opened since the walk. This afternoon they meet on one big sheet.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 6 items

You need: The three dried paint strips, The paper bag from the walk, 1 glue stick, or masking tape, 1 large sheet of card, 1 marker, 1 pair of adult scissors (adult only)

  1. Tip the bag out at one end of the table and let her spread everything flat.

💡She will put something in a box you would swear is wrong. Leave it. Move it and the board becomes yours and she stops sorting.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Appreciation is different from praise, and children need more of the first.

Good job lands on Azlyn as a verdict. She hears that you approved, has no idea which part, and the next strip is aimed at collecting the verdict again. Appreciation hands her the detail back: you mixed white in five times before you stopped, or there are three reds here and this morning I could see one. Children given descriptive feedback stay with hard work longer and hold up better when it goes wrong, because their read on it sits in the work rather than your face. Thank her for the board on the wall too, which appreciates her rather than the paint.

The Smash Cake

⏱️ 60 min🧰 8 items

You need: 1 ripe banana, 1 egg, 4 tbsp self-rising flour, 3 tbsp plain yogurt, 1 mixing bowl, 1 small pan, ramekin or oven-safe mug, 1 spatula, 1 fork, 1 tray

  1. She mashes the banana in the bowl with the fork until she cannot find a lump.
  2. Egg in, yogurt in, and she beats the lot with the same fork. Lumps are fine.
  3. Four tablespoons of flour, counted in by her and folded through with the spatula.
  4. Scrape it into the pan and bake it at 350°F for twenty-five minutes. You open the door and you carry the pan, both times.
  5. Turn it out onto the tray and stand it somewhere cold. Cream cheese slides off a warm cake, so it has until one o'clock. Washing the bowl is her job while it cools.

💡Build it for wrecking. Anything you tidy up turns it into a cake somebody wants kept, and this one has about eleven minutes to live.

One Candle, One Year

A sheet on the floor, a cake on the sheet, and a one-year-old who has never been told he is allowed to destroy something.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 10 items

👶 Kreston's afternoon 1:00 onward

A museum is loud, so give it forty minutes and leave before he tells you to. At twelve months he would rather walk than look, so find an empty stretch of floor and let him do laps holding one of your fingers. Azlyn picks the room.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Things that break the rules are the best teachers.

Kreston has spent a year learning that food stays in a bowl, hands get wiped and cake belongs on a plate. This afternoon all three come off at once and his face does the work. Babies look longer at events that break what they expected, and afterwards they investigate the object that misbehaved rather than the one that behaved. A ball that hangs in the air gets shaken. A cake he may sit in gets taken apart with both hands. Azlyn gets the same gift from the other side, watching a rule suspended on purpose and learning that rules have edges.

⚠️

Safety: The oven is on for half an hour this morning, so say it out loud when Azlyn comes near, and Kreston is in the high chair or out of the kitchen while the pan goes in and comes out. Balloons are the other one. Uninflated and burst latex is among the worst choking hazards there is under three, so you inflate them, they stay above head height, and a burst balloon is picked up whole and thrown out before Kreston is back down. The candle is lit for ten seconds, blown out by an adult, then out of the cake and up high. No honey for Kreston, no whole grapes, no whole nuts.

Ice Painting on the Path

⏱️ 60 min🧰 6 items

Five Blue, Two Red

Everything in this one gets counted, and a color she invents gets built twice.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 7 items

You need: 6 clear cups or jam jars, Water, Food coloring in 3 colors, 1 teaspoon, 6 cards and 1 pen for the recipes, 1 tray, Paper towels

  1. Paper towels down first, tray on top, six cups on the tray, each half full of water from the tap.
  2. She invents the first color. Drops go in one at a time and she counts them out loud: five blue, two red.
  3. Write the numbers on a card while she watches. Whatever she calls the color goes on the card too.
  4. Second cup, and she builds it again from her own card. You read the numbers back and she counts the drops in.
  5. Hold the two cups to the window side by side. If they do not match, count what went in and hunt for the missing drop.
  6. If one comes out too strong, she spoons some off with the teaspoon and tops the cup back up with water.
  7. Three recipes is plenty, and that is your six cups. Lay the cards along the table with their cups behind them and photograph the row.

💡Let her name them. Dragon and sad blue and Kreston beat dark purple, because a name she invented is one she can find again a week later.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Repeating an experiment is what makes it an experiment.

Running the red cube twice looks like an afternoon of the same thing. Azlyn is doing what every scientist does before a result counts, checking that the world behaves the same way when nobody changed anything. A three-year-old has no reason to assume it will. Her days are full of one-offs, and the idea that a cause repeats gets built out of cases she watched happen twice. The second run is also the only thing she can compare the first against, so it is where small differences show up. Say the numbers out loud both times and let her call the match.

Every Drop Makes a Circle

⏱️ 60 min🧰 6 items

You need: Paper towels, half a roll, 6 coffee filters, Food coloring in 3 cups, each half filled with water, 1 teaspoon, or a dropper, 1 baking tray with a lip, 1 small pitcher of water

About a quarter of the Netherlands sits below sea level and the country is threaded with canals, so Dutch children swim before they read. Most earn Zwemdiploma A at five or six, and part of that test happens in clothes and shoes, because nobody falls into a canal in a swimsuit.

Teddy's Life Jacket

⏱️ 60 min🧰 6 items

You need: 6 empty plastic bottles with caps, 500 ml, 1 dishpan or deep storage box, filled, 1 roll of tape, 1 teddy or plastic doll that can get wet, 2 old towels, 1 marker

  1. Spread one towel under the bowl and keep the other within reach. Sit teddy in the water on his own and let her watch him get heavy and go over.
  2. Number the bottles 1 to 6 with the marker so she can count what she has used.
  3. Tape bottle 1 across teddy's back and put him in. Ask whether more of him is out of the water than last time, and let her answer first.
  4. Add one bottle at a time, testing after each. Have her call it first: does this one hold his chin clear, or does he need another?
  5. Stop when his chin stays clear on its own, and count the bottles out loud together.
  6. Unscrew one cap under the water, keeping it closed in your other hand, and let her watch the air leave in bubbles. Teddy drops by that much. Dry him and stand him in the sun.

💡Check every cap is tight before you start. One loose cap fills and teddy goes under.

The Starfish Outline

The float has a shape, and she learns it on dry ground, where nothing goes near her ears.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 6 items

You need: Chalk, A flat patch of patio or path, The kiddie pool, filled that morning, or a deep storage box, 1 kitchen timer or a phone timer, 2 towels, 1 washcloth

  1. Draw a chalk starfish on the patio, five fat arms, wide enough for her to lie inside with room to spare.
  2. She lies in it face up, arms and legs along the arms. Chalk round her own body instead if she would rather.
  3. Teach the head position on the ground: ears down in the chalk, chin up at the sky, tummy pushed up. Count to ten out loud while she holds it.
  4. Move to the pool. Kreston goes in the high chair beside you for the whole ninety minutes; your hands are both under her back. She sits down first, then lies back with your hand flat between her shoulder blades.
  5. Set the timer for one minute and say the seconds out loud. She will sit up four times. Start again from the count she reached.
  6. Take your hand away for two seconds, telling her before you move it every time, and put it straight back.
  7. Wipe her face with the washcloth when she comes up, dry her with one towel, and keep the second for the puddle indoors.

💡Say I am taking my hand away now before you move it, even on the fifth go. Surprise turns a float into a fright.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Stillness has to be taught, and one minute is the right dose.

Floating asks Azlyn to hold still while everything in her body says move, and stillness is a skill she is starting to own rather than one she has. Psychologists call it inhibitory control, the same muscle she uses in musical statues and in waiting her turn, and at three it runs in seconds rather than minutes. Counting a minute out loud hands her a shape to hold to and an ending she can hear coming. She will break it. Start again from the count she reached. Short goes across a week build the hold; one long go teaches her that water is where she fails.

⚠️

Pour them in from the far side of the pool and stir, then hold a whole hand in the water before Azlyn gets near it. Sunscreen on both of them before you go out, and again after the water. The Bubble Breath Test

⏱️ 60 min🧰 6 items

You need: 1 dishpan of warm water, 2 straws, 1 ping-pong ball, or a plastic bottle cap instead, 1 scrap of tissue, 1 hand towel, 1 small mirror propped up (optional)

  1. Bowl of warm water on the table, hand towel beside it, tissue scrap in front of her. Prop the mirror where she can see her own face.
  2. Blow the tissue across the table, first with a huge puff, then with the smallest breath that still shifts it. Her breath is the power on her list.
  3. Float the ball in the bowl and blow it from one side to the other. Then race, one of you at each end, one breath each.
  4. Straws out, one each. Blow at the ball through a straw so she feels the thinner, faster stream. Then straw under the water, bubbles, and count how long she keeps one string going.
  5. Last game: chin resting on the water, mouth under, hum out. Do it yourself first and let her watch your face.
  6. Stop the moment she coughs. Hand her the towel and let her say whether there is another go in it.

💡Let her choose when her mouth goes in. She might do it in the first minute or take three days. Push her and the three days become two weeks.

The Sponge Pump

Same water, four tools, one job: get the full bowl into the empty one.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 7 items

You need: 2 dishpans or buckets, 1 large sponge, 1 turkey baster, or a clean squeezy sauce bottle instead, 1 pitcher, 3 plastic cups, one with holes poked in the bottom, 1 tray, Towels

  1. Set the two bowls at opposite ends of the tray on the patio. Fill one from the pitcher and leave the other empty.
  2. Sponge first. Soak it, lift it dripping, squeeze it out over the empty bowl. Let her run that five or six times without commentary.
  3. Hand her the baster and show the order once: squeeze, then in, then let go. She will get it backwards twice before it sticks.
  4. Fill all three cups from the pitcher. Two hold and one leaks, so the leaking one has to be carried faster than it empties.
  5. Ask which tool moved the most water. Take her answer, then test it. Empty everything back and give each tool one minute.
  6. Pour the last of the pitcher from up high, then from low down, and let her hold a hand in the stream both times.
  7. Tip both bowls out onto the grass, wipe the tray, and dry her hands and feet on the towels.

💡Do this in swimwear or in nothing at all. Every version ends with her sitting down in the full bowl.

The Lifeguard Chair

⏱️ 60 min🧰 7 items

You need: 1 dining chair, the sturdiest in the house, 1 red towel or red t-shirt, 1 roll of tape, 1 marker, Paper and a bulldog clip as a clipboard, 1 whistle on a loop of wool sized for her wrist, or a chosen shout word instead, A sun hat and sunscreen

The Teddy in the Deep End

⏱️ 60 min🧰 6 items

You need: The kiddie pool or a deep storage box, filled, 1 teddy that can get wet, or a capped bottle with a face drawn on, 1 broom or a garden stake, 1 large towel to throw and pull, 2 m of clothesline or string, 1 empty capped 2 L bottle

Today runs longer than any other day this year, and she has no way to feel that. Laid out flat, it fills the floor.

Shoes On and Out the Door

You walk the same route out of the house until her feet know it. Run it as a race and she will do it four times without being asked twice.

💡Stop the moment it stops being a game. A drill she enjoyed gets run again next month. One that frightened her gets refused.

Red, White & Blue Countdown 🎇

June 25 – July 1, 2027 · Days 176–182

On Friday Azlyn makes a paper chain with nine links in it, one for every sleep before the Fourth of July, and she tears one off every morning after that. The chain runs the week. Independence Day lands on Sunday the fourth, three days after this week ends, so nothing here is the celebration. Everything is the run-up, which is the part a three-year-old is best at. The other thread is the heat. Late June sets the temperature of every afternoon, so the ice pops, the tub under the sheet and the shaded chalk course are all built around it, and Wednesday moves the whole day out the back door at eight in the morning and leaves it there until dinner.

Fireworks Made With a Fork

⏱️ 60 min🧰 8 items

You need: Dark blue or black paper, 4 sheets (substitute: a cereal box painted dark), Washable non-toxic poster paint in red, white and blue, 1 fork, 1 whisk, 2 cardboard tubes, one end cut into a fringe, 2 shallow plates for the paint, Newspaper for the table, 1 wet washcloth in a bowl

  1. Newspaper back down. Red and white onto one shallow plate, blue onto the other, wet washcloth in its bowl at her elbow.
  2. Press the back of the fork into the paint, then straight down onto the dark paper and straight up again. Turn a quarter, press again round the same middle. Four presses make one burst.
  3. Whisk next. Dip the round underside, press, hold for a count of two. It prints fatter than the fork.

💡Load the fork light. A fork thick with paint prints a blob, and a fork with almost nothing on it prints thin lines that read as a firework.

The Bang She Controls

A firework goes off whether or not she is ready. Every loud noise this afternoon starts with her hands, and she counts it down first.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 6 items

You need: 6 paper lunch bags, 1 saucepan and lid, Bubble wrap, a sheet, 1 wooden spoon, 2 cushions, 1 folded towel to muffle the floor

  1. Set up at the far end of the room from Kreston, or hand him over and take this hour with her alone. Everything happens on the folded towel.
  2. Bags first. She blows one up, twists the neck, and you both count backwards from three before she claps it flat. Six bags, six goes, all hers.
  3. Pop the second bag under a cushion so she hears how much it takes away. The other cushion is hers, for her ears.
  4. Saucepan on the towel, lid in her hands. Lid on pan, lid on towel, wooden spoon on pan. Three sounds, and she names each one.
  5. Bubble wrap flat on the towel. Stamp along it, then roll it up and squeeze with both arms.
  6. Second round is hers to order. She says what is coming, you both count, and she does it.
  7. Finish on the floor with the towel over the bubble wrap. Ask her which was loudest.

💡Say the noise out loud before it arrives, every time, in the same words. Ready. Three, two, one, bang. She will start doing the countdown herself.

Six Pages and a Cover

⏱️ 90 min🧰 5 items

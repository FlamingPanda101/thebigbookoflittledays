A bedsheet goes over four dining chairs on Friday morning and the big top stays up until Thursday. Every day after that adds one act underneath it. She tumbles on a comforter and names her own six tricks, builds a sound desk out of the low cupboards, lifts a hundred-kilo barbell made of newspaper, draws four clown faces off her own reflection, walks a rope taped down the hall with a rice sock on her head, and catches scarves that fall slow enough to give her time. 🧒 What Azlyn will learn
  • Balance she can feel changing. Heel to toe along a rope, arms out sideways, a rice sock on her head that falls the moment she looks down.

  • What heavy does to her body. Sorting the counter into two piles, hauling the barbell overhead, loading a basket until it stops moving.

The Big Top

⏱️ 60 min🧰 7 items

You need: 1 large bedsheet, or 2 single sheets, 4 dining chairs, or a drying rack and 2 chairs, 8 clothespins or bulldog clips, 1 roll masking tape, Red paper, 6 sheets, cut into long strips, String, 2 meters, 4 heavy books to weight the edges

  1. Stand the four chairs on the tape circle, backs facing out, as far apart as the sheet will reach.
  2. Drape the sheet over the top and clip it to the chair backs. Clips hold where a tucked edge slides off the moment she leans on it.
  3. Ask her where the door goes. Clip that edge so she can lift it and drop it, and put it on the side you can see from the kitchen.
  4. Tape the red strips down the outside, roof to floor, all the way round. You hold each strip, she sticks the tape.
  5. Weight all four outside edges with the books. Nothing goes on the roof.
  6. Tape a paper flag to the string, run it from the top of the tent to the back of one chair, and get in with her.

💡It stays up until Thursday, so build it to survive her crawling in and out forty times. More clips and heavier chairs beat a neat sheet.

The Ringmaster

The tent has a door and nobody has been announced through it yet. The hat takes the longest, so it comes first.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 8 items

You need: 1 cereal box or 2 sheets thick card, 1 pair of scissors (adult only), Black paper, or a black crayon (paint works), Packing tape and masking tape, 1 sheet of paper rolled into a cone, 1 wooden spoon or cardboard tube for the baton, 1 ribbon, scarf or old belt, Every teddy in the house, for the audience

  1. Cut a strip of card long enough to go round her head, tape the ends into a ring, and check it sits above her ears.
  2. Cut a card circle wider than the ring for the brim, snip tabs round the inner hole, and tape the tabs up inside the ring. She holds, you cut.
  3. Cover the outside with black paper, or hand her the crayon and let her do it. The patches she misses stay missed.
  4. Roll the sheet of paper into a cone, tape the seam, and let her test her voice through the narrow end.
  5. Tie the ribbon over one shoulder and under the other arm. She chooses the shoulder.
  6. Line every teddy along the hallway. She walks the parade with the baton, announcing each one by name, and the parade ends inside the big top.
  7. Let her announce you, and then be whatever she introduced you as.

💡Some children never get past the cone. If the parade never happens, the afternoon still worked.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Interoception, the sense nobody lists.

Sight, hearing, touch, taste and smell get all the attention. Azlyn is also building interoception, the sense that reads her own insides: hunger, thirst, a full bladder, a heart going fast after she has run round a tent. That wiring is half built at three, so she can be starving and tell you she is fine, then come apart over a sock. You are already doing the thing that helps. Each label hands her a word for something she can feel and cannot yet place.

Six Tricks and a Bow

⏱️ 60 min🧰 6 items

You need: 1 comforter, or 3 folded blankets, 4 sofa cushions, 6 index cards, or 6 pieces cut from a cereal box, 1 marker, 1 roll masking tape for the start line, A phone, for filming one trick

  1. Show her a move, let her try it, then ask what it is called. Write her name for it on a card in marker, big enough to read from the other side of the room.
  2. Six moves: a forward roll, a log roll down the length of the comforter, a bridge on hands and knees, a candle on her back with her hips on the mat and both legs straight up, a jump with a half turn, and a stork balance on one foot.
  3. Every move ends on the tape line with a bow. The bow is how she knows a trick has finished.
  4. Lay the six cards face up. She picks the running order and performs the set from start to end.
  5. Film the one she is proudest of and watch it back with her once.
  6. Leave the cards on the mat. She runs the set again at 4:15 with nobody watching.

💡Spot her with your hands ready and off her. A forward roll goes wrong at the moment an adult lifts her halfway through it.

The Beanbag on Her Head

Three rice socks take ten minutes to make and get used every day for the rest of the week.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 6 items

You need: 3 clean socks, 2 cups rice or dried lentils (flour in a double freezer bag if you have neither), 1 roll masking tape, 1 tray, 1 paperback book, A phone timer

  1. Fill each sock a third full, knot it twice, and turn the cuff back over the knot. She pours, you knot.
  2. Run a line of masking tape from one end of the hall to the other with a bend in the middle of it.
  3. She walks the line with one sock on her head. It falls. She starts again from the beginning, and she will insist on that rule herself.
  4. Stack the second sock on top of the first and let her find out how much less her head can move.
  5. Add the tray held flat in both hands, with the paperback on it and one sock on her head.
  6. Time a run with the phone and say the number out loud. Then ask for a slower one and time that.
  7. Put a sock on your own head and let her walk you down the line.

💡Arms out sideways do more for her balance than anything you can say about it. Put your own arms out and she copies without being asked.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Adjectives are the scaffolding for thinking.

Azlyn named six moves this morning and you wrote her words down. Describing words are the tools she sorts the world with. Nouns hand her the objects, and adjectives let her hold a quality on its own, so heavy stops belonging to one can and becomes something a box, a basket and a brother can each be. Slower, higher and softer are the first measuring she does without a ruler. Feed her the ones she is missing while her body is still doing the thing: that roll was tighter than the last one. An adjective attached to something she can feel sticks better than one off a page.

The Sound Effects Desk

⏱️ 60 min🧰 9 items

You need: 1 upturned box or tray as the desk, 1 saucepan lid and 1 wooden spoon, 1 comb and a sheet of baking paper, 1 empty plastic bottle with its lid, 1 shoebox and 4 thick rubber bands, Dried pasta in a screw-top jar, 2 sheets of paper to rip, A bunch of keys or a bell, Masking tape and a marker for labels

The Clown Shoes

A clown walks wrong on purpose, and she has to work out how first. The boxes take an hour to build and the walking takes the rest.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 9 items

You need: 2 shoeboxes or cereal boxes, String or wide elastic, 1 meter, Packing tape, Paint, or colored paper and glue, 1 pair of scissors (adult only), A chair back to hold for the stand-up, 1 sturdy hardcover book, for the step up, One of your cardigans and a belt, A full-length mirror

  1. Cut two slits across the top of each box, a foot's width apart. You cut, she holds the box still.
  2. Thread the elastic through and knot it underneath so it makes a strap over the top of her foot.
  3. Line the inside with packing tape where her heel lands, because that corner goes first.
  4. She paints or papers them, and the colors go on big, bright and uneven.
  5. Put them on sitting down on the carpet, with her hand on a chair back for the stand-up.
  6. She walks a length of carpet on her own first. Then she takes a hand in yours for one step up onto a book, a turn on the spot, and the walk back.
  7. Put your cardigan on her with the belt round the middle and stand her in front of the mirror. Give her ten minutes in front of it with nothing to do.

💡Boxes with the sides left tall stay on her feet. The moment you cut them down to look neat, they fall off at every step.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Auditory discrimination is the reading skill nobody mentions.

Azlyn shut her eyes this morning and picked one noise out of seven. Telling two close sounds apart is a skill of its own, and reading sits on top of it. A child who cannot hear the difference between the front of bat and the front of pat has nothing to hang a letter on, and the gap turns up in school as a reading problem when it started in the ear. Household objects beat an app for this, because the sounds are messy and she has to hold two of them side by side to choose. The two she keeps confusing are the ones her ear is still working on.

Farm kitchens baked it in a covered pot over the fire, and the cross cut across the top let heat reach the middle.

The Egg That Learns to Float

⏱️ 60 min🧰 9 items

You need: 2 tall clear jars or glasses, 1 pitcher of warm water, Table salt, about 8 tablespoons, 2 eggs, 1 long spoon, 1 deep tray, 1 towel, 1 scrap of paper, 1 marker and masking tape, The sink pile and float pile from the morning

💡Warm water takes salt fast and cold water sulks. If the egg will not lift, add two more spoons and stir for longer.

Raw Egg, Cooked Egg

Four eggs, two of them cooked, no way to tell from the outside. She works it out three ways without cracking one open.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 8 items

You need: 2 raw eggs, 2 hard-boiled eggs, 1 marker to label them, A smooth tray or counter, 1 large mixing bowl, 1 freezer bag, 1 towel, Soap at the sink

  1. Mark the four eggs A, B, C and D. Tell her two are cooked and let her guess which before she touches anything.
  2. Spin each one flat on the tray. The cooked ones go like a top. The raw ones wobble and stall, because the liquid inside keeps moving after the shell stops.
  3. Spin one, stop it with a finger for half a second, then let go. A raw egg starts turning again on its own. A cooked one stays dead.
  4. Hold each one against a flashlight or a bright window. The raw ones glow at the edges.
  5. She sorts them into two groups and you write her decision on the tray before anyone checks.
  6. Crack them into the bowl one at a time. Do the two she called cooked first.
  7. Shells into the freezer bag, knot it, and everyone washes with soap before anything else.

💡Say nothing about why the raw one wobbles until she has spun all four twice. She spots the pattern before she can explain it.

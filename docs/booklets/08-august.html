Sports & Team Play ⚽

August 6 – August 12, 2027 · Days 218–224

This week Azlyn builds a sport out of its parts. A ball wound from grocery bags on Friday, a numbered shirt on Saturday, a whistle and three signals on Sunday, five events chalked onto the path on Monday, a long jump measured in string on Tuesday, half-time drinks she mixes to her own recipe on Wednesday. The question sitting under all of it: what does a game need before anybody can play it? On Thursday she answers it by making up a game of her own and running the whole thing end to end.

Nothing lands in this week but the heat, and it shapes every day. The outdoor hour goes before eleven, and the afternoon event sits indoors at a table, on the floor or on a tray. Four things get built to be used later: Friday's wound ball, Saturday's numbered shirts, Sunday's peg scoreboard and Monday's five chalked stations all come back out on Thursday. Keep the first three somewhere she can see them, because the week ends with a trophy and she needs to know what it is for.

🧒 What Azlyn will learn
  • Sorting by what a thing does. Balls go in the HIGH basket or the LOW one, and only dropping them settles which.
  • Stopping on a signal. One whistle blast freezes her mid-stride, two turn her round, three put everybody on the floor.
  • Measuring with string. A cut length holds a jump, so three jumps lie side by side on the wall and she sorts them longest to shortest.
  • Keeping a score. One point, one peg, one space along the line, counted out loud.
  • Writing rules and mending them. She invents a game, plays it until something breaks, and says what the new rule should be.

High Bouncer, Low Bouncer

⏱️ 60 min🧰 6 items

You need: Every ball in the house, 8–12 (soccer ball, tennis ball, ping-pong ball, the sock ball, the orange, this morning's foil ball), 2 laundry baskets or empty boxes, 1 roll masking tape, 1 permanent marker, 4 sheets of paper for her predictions, A clear stretch of hard floor and a wall

  1. Tape a strip to the wall at your shoulder height and write DROP HERE on it. Every ball starts against that tape and gets let go, never thrown.
  2. Stand the two baskets a stride back on either side. Tape a label on each, HIGH and LOW.
  3. Drop the first ball. Watch where the top of the bounce reaches on her, knee or waist or shoulder. She calls it.
  4. Before each new ball she squeezes it and says which basket it lands in. Write the prediction down and tick it after the drop.
  5. Work through the lot on hard floor. The orange divides opinion and the foil ball disappoints her.
  6. Count each basket and read the prediction sheet back, ticks and all. Ask what the high ones have in common and write her answer word for word.

💡The squeeze is doing the work. Hard and springy comes back, soft and dead does not.

The Ball Made of Bags and String

Children in half the world make their own soccer balls, and this is how.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 7 items

You need: 4–6 plastic grocery bags, 1 ball of string or thick wool, Packing tape and masking tape, 1 old sock, Newspaper, 4 sheets, 1 pair of adult scissors (adult only), The taped mark on the wall from this morning

  1. Scrunch two sheets of newspaper into the tightest ball she can manage. Squeeze, do not fold. That lump sets the size of everything after it.
  2. Push the core into the old sock, knot it tight above, and cut the tail off with your scissors.
  3. Wrap a grocery bag round the sock and twist the handles down flat. Then the next over the twist from a different side, four to six in all.
  4. Hold the bundle while she winds the string. Round, then across, then round again. Twenty turns leaves an egg. Fifty looks like a ball.
  5. Tie the string off, cover the knot with packing tape, and run masking tape over any bag still showing.
  6. Take it to the taped mark on the wall. She calls high or low, then drops it, and finds she was right about a sock ball.
  7. Out on the grass, kick it. It flies heavy and dead and will not roll far, which is what makes it good on a dry lawn.

💡Wind the string tighter than feels reasonable. A loose ball goes egg-shaped on the second kick, and the fix is another fifty turns.

The Number the Paint Runs Off

⏱️ 60 min🧰 7 items

You need: 1 white wax crayon or a white candle stub, Thick paper, 6 sheets, Washable poster paint in two colors, watered down in jam jars, 1 wide soft brush, 1 jar of clean water, 1 old towel, 1 permanent marker

  1. She picks the team's two colors out of the jars and names the team. Write the name on the back of one sheet in marker.
  2. Give her the white crayon and a sheet of thick paper. She draws a big number, pressing hard. It comes out close to invisible.
  3. Load the wide brush with the first watery color and wash it edge to edge in one direction. The number rises up through it.
  4. Rinse the brush in the jar of clean water and dry it on the towel before the second color, or you get mud.
  5. Do a second sheet in the other color, and a third with a stripe of each meeting in the middle.
  6. Hand her the last three sheets to do on her own. Write a squad number on the back of each and lay them out of the sun to dry.

💡Press hard with the crayon and water the paint down further than looks sensible. Thick paint covers the wax instead of sliding off it.

Numbers on Real Shirts

The wax trick works on cloth too, and this one comes off the line in time for tomorrow.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 11 items

You need: 3–4 old white t-shirts (pillowcases work if you have no spare shirts), 1 roll masking tape, Washable poster paint or fabric paint, 1 sponge or 1 stiff brush, Flattened cardboard, one piece per shirt, 6 clothespins and the clothesline, 1 sheet of thick card for the August handprints, 1 wide soft brush, from this morning, 1 wet washcloth, 1 permanent marker, 1 pencil, if you are marking the door frame this month

  1. Slide a piece of cardboard inside each shirt, up to the shoulders. Paint goes through cotton in seconds and the card is the only thing stopping it.
  2. Tear the masking tape into strips and build a number on the back of the first shirt. Straight lines only. A 1 is one strip, a 7 is two, a 4 is three. Press every edge down hard.
  3. Let her build the next number herself. Crooked is correct. If she wants a 12, both digits go on.
  4. Load the sponge and press it straight down over the tape rather than brushing across. Brushing lifts the edges and the paint creeps underneath.
  5. Cover the whole back panel, tape and all, then peel the tape off while the paint is still wet, corner first, pulling it back on itself. The number underneath stays white.
  6. Clip the shirts on the line in the sun. August dries a t-shirt inside an hour.
  7. August's handprint sheet while the shirts dry. Kreston goes first, while he is calm, with the wet washcloth already in your other hand. Then Azlyn goes on the same sheet beside him. Paint her palm with the brush rather than dipping her hand. Press flat, count to three, lift straight up. Write August, her 3 and his 14 months beside the pair in marker. If August is a month you want on the door frame, take both marks in pencil while they are standing still, with the name and the date beside each.

💡Do a spare pillowcase first if you have one. The first number teaches you how much paint a sponge holds.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Oil and water is the same lesson as the wax crayon and the sticker resist.

Azlyn met one idea three times today in three materials. Oil sitting back up on top of the water, wax turning the paint away, tape holding a white number under a loaded sponge. The rule underneath all three is that some things refuse to mix, and the one doing the refusing wins. A three-year-old does not carry a rule from a single demonstration over to a new material on her own. She needs it in enough different clothes to strip out whatever was incidental about the first one. That is why the jar at breakfast earns its five minutes, and why the shirts matter.

⚠️

Safety: Use a plastic bottle for the shake rather than a glass jar, or keep your hand round the jar while she shakes it. Statues on the Whistle

⏱️ 60 min🧰 5 items

You need: 1 whistle (a saucepan lid and a wooden spoon, or your own voice, does the same job), Music on your phone, 1 roll masking tape for the start and finish lines, 2 cushions as the turning markers, A clear hallway, or the coolest room in the house

  1. Tape a start line and a finish line at each end of the hallway and stand a cushion past each. Those are the turns.
  2. Music on, and send her line to line however she wants to travel. Running, crawling, sideways, hopping.
  3. One blast. She holds whatever shape she is in, mid-stride, until the next blast. Count three out loud while she wobbles, then let her go.
  4. Add the second signal. Two blasts, she turns at the nearest cushion and comes back. Give her four or five before the third arrives.
  5. Three blasts, everybody on the floor including you. Getting down there yourself is what makes her drop fast.
  6. Hand her the whistle for the last ten minutes and run when she says run. She holds the stop for an unreasonable length of time.

💡Blow the stop when she is going flat out, not when she is slowing down anyway.

The Peg Scoreboard

The rest of the week needs somewhere to put its points. It gets built once, sitting down, and used on three more days.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 6 items

You need: 1 sheet of thick card, or a flattened box, String or thick wool, 2 arm-lengths, cut last night, 10–12 clothespins, 1 permanent marker, 1 roll masking tape, Stickers or bottle caps for the point markers

  1. Lay the card flat and rule a line down the middle. Two teams. She names both and writes what she can of the names at the top.
  2. Tape one string across the upper half, the other across the lower half. Pull each tight before the second tape goes down, or the clothespins sag instead of sliding.
  3. Mark ten spaces under each string. Nought at the left, then a line for every point up to ten. She does the marks under the second string.
  4. Clip a peg to the left end of each string. That is nought all, and a point moves a peg one space right.
  5. Stick a sticker at the five and the ten on both strings, or tape a bottle cap there, so she finds halfway without counting.
  6. Test it. Three points to you, four to her, one space at a time and counting out loud, then slide both back.
  7. Stand it against the wall where it lives all week, spare pegs clipped along the top edge.

💡Let her move both pegs, yours as well as hers, counting out loud.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Freezing on cue is one of the best self-control exercises there is.

Stopping a body that is already moving is one of the hardest things a three-year-old brain does. Azlyn has to hold a rule, catch a signal, and shut down a movement her legs have committed to. Researchers who study executive function use games close to this one as a measure, and how children handle them at four tracks with how they settle into a classroom later. The two-blast turn is a step up again, because it swaps one rule for another mid-run. She will fail the first few, and those are the ones doing the work. Every failed stop is practice, and the laughing is what keeps her doing thirty of them.

Passports come in four colors and no more. Burgundy across most of Europe, green across much of the Muslim world and West Africa, blue across the Americas, black in a few countries including Botswana and New Zealand. The choice tends to say which neighbors a country stands with.

The Passport That Gets Stamped

⏱️ 60 min🧰 10 items

You need: 4 sheets of printer paper, 1 cereal box, or a sheet of thick card, for the cover, 1 pair of adult scissors (adult only), 1 stapler (adult only), 1 glue stick, Her photo, or her drawing of her face from 8:00, 1 permanent marker, 1 potato and 1 table knife (adult only), Washable poster paint, or an ink pad, Paper towels

  1. Fold the four sheets in half and stack them inside each other. She folds and presses every crease with her thumb.
  2. Cut the front panel off the cereal box, fold it round the stack and staple twice through the fold. Both tools stay in your hands.
  3. Glue her drawing or photo onto the first inside page. Write PASSPORT, her name and her age on the cover.

💡Let her stamp page one four or five times. A passport covered in the same star beats one tidy print.

The Bottle for the Long Journey

Every traveler packs a bag. Hers gets a bottle with the sea in it, and the case shuts before dinner.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 9 items

You need: 1 clear plastic bottle, 16 oz, lid on, label peeled off, Warm water from the tap, 4 tbsp cooking oil (or clear glitter glue if you have it), Blue food coloring, 1 tbsp rice, sequins or small beads, Packing tape for the lid, 1 small bag or case, 1 toy and 1 book to pack, The passport from this morning

  1. Let her peel the last of the label off.
  2. Fill the bottle two-thirds with warm water at the sink. The oil beads rounder in warm water.
  3. Two drops of blue coloring, lid on, one hard shake. Stop at two or you lose the light through it.
  4. Pinch the rice, sequins or beads in a few at a time. Sweep the misses up before you put Kreston down.
  5. Pour the oil in until a finger's depth sits on top, then screw the lid down hard.
  6. Wrap packing tape round the lid three full turns, over the threads, so it cannot come off in anyone's hands.
  7. Turn it over together and watch. Then pack the case: passport, bottle, one toy, one book, and stand it by the front door.

💡Name it tonight and keep it somewhere she can reach on her own.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Sensory bottles work as a calming tool, and it's worth keeping one.

Azlyn will turn that bottle over a hundred times this week. The oil and water separate at a pace her eyes can follow, slower than anything else in the room, and watching something move at that speed pulls her breathing down with it. Occupational therapists hand these out for a living. Keep this one somewhere she can reach on her own, because a tool she fetches herself does more than one you produce at the point of a meltdown. Offer it before the storm rather than during, on the drive home or before dinner, and she starts to notice what it does for her.

The Rangoli at the Front Door

⏱️ 60 min🧰 12 items

You need: All-purpose flour, 4 cups, as the white and the base, Ground turmeric, Paprika, Cocoa or ground coffee, 4 small bowls, 1 spoon, 1 stick of chalk, 1 pitcher of water, A stiff brush or broom, 1 old towel, Your phone, for the photo before it goes, Her passport, for the stamp on page two

  1. Sweep the step and test one hidden corner with a pinch of turmeric. If it stains the stone, move the whole thing onto the path.
  2. Chalk a circle the size of a dinner plate, then six petals round it. Draw the first two and hand her the chalk.
  3. She fills each petal by pinching powder out of the bowl and letting it fall. Show her the pinch once.
  4. Top up any bowl that runs low with flour and another spoonful of turmeric, paprika or cocoa. The yellow goes first, every time.
  5. Kneel her on the old towel for the middle so she can reach in without walking through her own work.
  6. Photograph it from standing height, then pour the pitcher over it together and brush it into the drain. Dry her hands, press one thumb into what is left in the turmeric bowl and stamp page two of the passport. She says the country out loud.

💡Tell her at the start that it gets washed away before dinner. Say it once and she will pour the water herself.

The doorstep patterns end up on a blanket across an elephant's back, and the elephant is a box she can sit in.

One Change, Then Race Again

⏱️ 60 min🧰 7 items

You need: 20 sheets of printer paper, 6 paperclips, 1 saucer for the paperclips, Masking tape for the throwing line and the landing marks, 1 marker, 1 tape measure, or a length of string, The hallway, or the longest room in the house

  1. Tape a line across the floor at one end. Toes stay behind it on every throw, hers and yours.
  2. Fold three planes the same way and number them 1, 2 and 3. Hers from 8:00 can race later.
  3. Throw plane 1. Tape a short strip where the nose lands and write 1 on it. Throw twice more from the same spot and keep the furthest mark.
  4. Change one thing on plane 2 and one thing only: a paperclip pushed onto the nose. Throw, mark, number.
  5. Plane 3 gets a different single change: bend the back edge of both wings down a fingernail's width. Throw, mark, number.
  6. Measure from the line to each tape mark and lay the three planes beside their own marks. She calls the winner.

💡Mark where the nose lands, not where the plane stops. A plane that skids another few feet down the hall has stopped flying, and she will argue it.

The Water Runs Downhill

⏱️ 60 min🧰 8 items

You need: 6–8 large plastic bottles, cut lengthways (adult, done the night before), Parcel or duct tape, 1 pitcher, 1 watering can or bucket, 1 dishpan for the bottom, Corks, bottle caps and 2 foil trays cut into boats, Chalk, Towels

  1. Tape every join with the upper bottle inside the lower one. Water runs out of any join taped the other way round.
  2. Dishpan at the bottom of the run, under the last bottle.
  3. First pitcher of water. Watch the whole length, mark every leak with a finger, then tape those joins again with her.
  4. Chalk the numbers 1 to 5 along the channel, so she has something to shout when a cork sticks at four.

Azlyn builds a carnival one stall at a time this week, and on Thursday the whole garden opens. Friday she prints tickets off a half potato and takes over the back door as the gate. On Saturday she pegs sixteen bunting triangles in a red-red-blue-blue pattern, then chalks that same pattern down the patio and runs it with her feet. Sunday she makes prizes to order from other people's drawings, then throws a sock ball at six tins to win them back. Monday she builds a tea counter out of one box and serves fruit and iced water off a tray, Tuesday she hooks numbered pots out of a bowl of water. Wednesday is two rides built from a pencil, a cardboard circle and a lump of playdough. Sitting under all seven days: what does it take to run something for other people?

No holiday lands in the week. The heat does, and every day is written around it. The outdoor hour goes before eleven, the tea counter stands in shade, and Tuesday's swimming is the coolest hour of the seven. Nothing gets thrown out before Thursday either. The tickets, the bunting, the prize bags, the tins, the counter, the water bowl, the carousel and the standing wheel all come back out on Opening Day, so give them a box by the back door and keep it above Kreston's reach. He is 14 months and walking, and a prize bag at his height lasts about a minute.

🧒 What Azlyn will learn
  • One ticket for one person. She lays the blanks out in pairs, hands one over for each coin, and does the counting before she can count.
  • Two of each along the line. Red red blue blue through the bunting and down twelve chalk squares, called out loud as she jumps and spins along them.
  • Building to somebody else's drawing. She reads an order slip, makes the thing on it, and holds the prize and the drawing up together to find what is missing.
  • A table knife and a loaded tray. She cuts melon with your hand over hers, pours from a pitcher, and carries two drinks out to the tent without setting the tray down.
  • An animal goes where it sleeps. Frog to the pond, mole to the burrow, and a proper argument about the plastic yellow duck sitting next to the plastic yellow digger.
👶 KRESTON THIS WEEK

Kreston is 14 months this week. He scribbles with intent, stacks two blocks and has three to five words you can make out, and he walks straight at whatever is going on. Standing water on Tuesday, the sock ball on Sunday and a pencil axle at his eye height on Wednesday are the three that need him counted in before anybody starts.

The Ticket Press

⏱️ 60 min🧰 7 items

You need: Cereal-box card or thick paper, 6 sheets, torn into ticket blanks, 1 potato, halved, with a shape cut into the flat face last night (a kitchen sponge cut to a shape, or a bottle cap wound with string, does the same job), Washable non-toxic poster paint, 2 colors, 2 saucers, each with a folded washcloth in it as an ink pad, 1 permanent marker, Newspaper for the table, 1 shoebox or ice-cream tub for the finished tickets

  1. Newspaper over the table. A folded washcloth into each saucer, then a spoonful of paint on each so it soaks in like an ink pad.
  2. Show her the press once. Potato flat onto the washcloth, straight down onto a blank, count to three, lift without dragging.
  3. She prints two of every ticket, one color each, laid down in pairs as they come off.
  4. Reload on the washcloth every second print. Prints go pale as the paint runs out, and a pale ticket still counts.
  5. Number them with the marker while they dry, one number per pair. Take her numbers however they arrive, backwards and sideways included.
  6. Stack the dry pairs in the shoebox by the back door. She needs the whole pile at one o'clock.

💡Print more than the family needs. A gate that runs out at ten past one ends the afternoon, and forty tickets is ten minutes of pressing.

Jump Jump Spin Spin

The pattern comes off the string and onto the patio in chalk. She runs it now instead of looking at it.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 3 items

You need: Sidewalk chalk in the same 2 colors, A patio, path or drive, 1 pitcher of water and 1 old brush for washing it off

  1. Chalk twelve squares down the patio, each big enough for both her feet: two red, two blue, two red, two blue, to the end.
  2. Agree the moves before anybody runs. Red is a jump, blue is a spin. Do one of each yourself so she has seen them.
  3. She runs the line calling the move as she lands on it. Slow the first time.
  4. Run it yourself and get it wrong twice on purpose. She stops you mid-square.
  5. Hand her the chalk and let her add four squares on the end in whatever order she likes. Then you run hers and she calls it.
  6. Change one move and run the line again. Blue is a clap now.
  7. Wash it off with the pitcher and the brush when she is finished. On hot chalk it takes a minute.

💡Say the pattern in the rhythm of the moves. Jump jump spin spin gets into her feet in a way red red blue blue never does.

Building the Box Guitar

⏱️ 60 min🧰 7 items

You need: 1 cookie tin, or a sturdy shoebox, 3 rubber bands of different thicknesses, 2 screws or cup hooks (substitute: 2 pencils taped under the bands as bridges), 1 real screwdriver, 1 wooden ruler or paint stirrer, sanded, for the neck, 1 square of sandpaper, 1 roll masking tape

He gets a turn behind the sheet on your hip, with somebody out front telling him what he looks like. At 14 months he will reach for the lamp, so keep it short. At the market give him one thing to carry to the bag.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

She's met shadows three times now, and each time added something.

In February she made animals on a sheet by flashlight and swelled a fox up the fabric by walking him at the flashlight, with the shadow still a thing her hands held. In the summer she found her own on the hot sidewalk and worked out it belonged to her. Today the shadow that changes size is her whole body, and her feet are the control. Concepts arrive in layers at three, months apart, through the same material met in a new arrangement. Nothing was wasted in February and nothing is finished today. She will meet shadows again in December when the sun is low all day.

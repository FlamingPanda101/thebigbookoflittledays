Little Bakery 🥐

February 5 – February 11, 2027 · Days 36–42

Four things make bread, and she counts them on her fingers on Friday morning before anything goes in the bowl: flour, water, salt, yeast. The rest of the week takes them apart one at a time. The yeast gets its own jar and then its own balloon, the dough gets punched flat and stretched until it tears, and the same lump comes back as rolls, a pizza base and a tray of cinnamon buns. She follows one loop all week, from dry powder foaming in warm water to a hollow knock on the bottom of a crust.

No holiday lands in this week, so the shape comes from the theme. Four days run on a hot oven, three of them on real dough. Monday and Wednesday run on playdough, paper and a pretend shop counter, and they sit there to give your kitchen a day off. Thursday takes the whole thing out of the house: she boxes what she baked, walks a round she scouted that morning, and hands it over at four front doors.

🧒 What Azlyn will learn
  • Yeast is alive and it eats. A jar of foam at eight thirty, a balloon standing up by eleven thirty, and an hour of waiting she can see the point of.
  • Hands that knead, roll, cut and press. Ten minutes of heavy work at the table on Friday, a rolling pin on Monday, and floss pulled through a log of dough on Thursday.
  • Her own words hold still on paper. She dictates a recipe, you read it back the same way twice, and by Thursday she picks butter out of the page on her own.
  • Words for what her mouth finds. Soft, chewy, seedy, holey. Five breads on five numbered plates and one word each.
  • Numbers doing a job. Twelve pastries counted onto a tray, a price written on every one, buttons counted into the register and back out at closing.

The Fizzing Jar

You need: 1 clean jam jar · active dry yeast, 1 packet · sugar, 1 tsp · warm water, 100 ml · 1 teaspoon.

  1. Fill the jar a third full, cold water in first and hot run in after, until it feels like bath water on your wrist. Let her test it with one finger.

Four Things Make Bread

⏱️ 60 min🧰 9 items

You need: Bread flour, 500 g, Active dry yeast, 1 packet (7 g), Salt, 1 tsp, Sugar, 1 tsp, Warm water, 300 ml, Vegetable oil, 1 tbsp, 1 large mixing bowl, 1 clean dish towel, The jam jar of woken yeast from 8:00

  1. Count the four things on her fingers before anything goes in the bowl: flour, water, salt, yeast.
  2. Flour into the bowl, salt to one side, and let her punch a well in the middle. The packet of yeast goes in the well, then the oil and the warm water. The jar of foam from 8:00 stands beside the bowl as the proof it is alive, and it goes down the sink at the end.
  3. She mixes with one hand until it comes together as a shaggy lump. It sticks to her. Leave it sticking.
  4. Turn it onto the table and knead: push with the heel of your hand, fold, turn a quarter, push again. Say the four words out loud and hand it over.
  5. Ten minutes of that between you. She will manage two and wander off, so take it on and hand it back when she returns. The lump goes from ragged to smooth.
  6. Oil the bowl, drop the dough in, cover it and stand it somewhere warm. Mark its height on the outside with a strip of tape.

💡Kneading is heavy work and it settles a wound-up morning better than any instruction to calm down. Give her all ten minutes.

The Rolls and the Hollow Knock

The dough has doubled by one o'clock and it holds the dent when she pushes a finger in.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 7 items

You need: The risen dough, 1 baking tray and baking paper, Extra flour for the table, 2 tbsp milk and a pastry brush or a spoon, 1 wire rack or a cold plate, Oven gloves, Butter for the first one, eaten warm

  1. Tip the dough onto a floured table and let her press all the air out of it. It sighs down and she will want to do it twice.
  2. Cut it into eight or ten pieces. She cuts, you even them up afterwards without saying so.
  3. Show her the roll: hand cupped over the piece, palm circling, dough turning underneath. Hers come out as lumps and lumps bake fine.
  4. Space them on the lined tray a hand's width apart, cover, and leave them thirty minutes. Oven on to 425°F.
  5. Brush the tops with milk and bake fifteen to eighteen minutes. She watches through the glass and the door stays shut.
  6. Out onto the rack. Once one has cooled enough to hold, turn it over and knock the bottom with a knuckle. Hollow means baked through, so let her knock every one.
  7. Butter the first and eat it standing up in the kitchen before anyone suggests plates.

💡Knock on a raw one before the tray goes in. She needs the dull thud at one thirty to hear the hollow one an hour later.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Auditory attention is trainable, and it underpins reading.

Azlyn knocking the bottom of a roll and deciding whether it sounds hollow is doing fine listening work: two sounds held side by side, one picked out from the other. That skill sits underneath reading. Before she can match a letter to a sound she has to hear that bat and pat start with different mouths, and children who catch small differences in sound learn to read with less effort later on. Kitchens are good ground for it. The kettle, the fizzing jar, the knock on a crust, the oven timer. Name a sound out loud and she starts collecting them.

The Recipe She Tells You

⏱️ 60 min🧰 8 items

You need: Soft butter, 100 g, Sugar, 100 g, All-purpose flour, 200 g, 1 egg, Her chosen additions: raisins, chocolate chips, cocoa, sprinkles or a mashed banana, 1 bowl and a wooden spoon, 1 baking tray and baking paper, Paper and a pen for her words

  1. Sit down with her and ask how it is made, starting with the first thing into the bowl.
  2. Write every word as it comes. And then you shake it a lot. Two hundred sugars. Do not tidy the order or fix the grammar.
  3. Read it back from the top. She will change things. Cross out and write the new version underneath so both stay.
  4. Now make it off her page. Quantities come from the list above, order comes from her.
  5. Roll the mixture into walnut-sized balls, flatten them onto the lined tray with gaps between, and she adds her extras where her page says.
  6. Bake at 350°F for twelve to fifteen minutes until the edges brown. Read her recipe out loud once more while they bake.

💡Follow her order even where it makes a mess of the batch, and say out loud what happened. A batch that comes out odd beats one you fixed behind her back.

Her Page in the Cookbook

This afternoon her recipe becomes a page in a book with her name on the front.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 7 items

You need: Paper, 5–6 sheets, The written recipe from this morning, Crayons or markers, Tape or glue, 1 cereal box cut flat for the cover, String and a hole punch, or a stapler, A permanent marker

  1. Trim the sheets to a size her hands can turn and lay them in order.
  2. Tape the morning's recipe onto the middle page, butter stain and crossings-out and all.
  3. She draws one ingredient per sheet on the pages before it: butter, sugar, egg, flour, her own addition. Write the word underneath in your own hand, big.
  4. Cut the cereal box flat for the cover and let her color it. Ask her the name of the book and write it across the front word for word.

  7. Stand it on the kitchen shelf with the real cookbooks, at her height.

💡She matches the shape of the word to the picture she made, and by Thursday she will find butter on the page unprompted.

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Dictated stories are the bridge to real literacy.

Azlyn cannot write and she can compose. Those two come apart at three, and taking her recipe down word for word lets the second run ahead of the first. She watches her own speech turn into marks you read back the same way twice, which is the idea underneath all of reading: the marks hold the words. Teachers call it language experience, and children who dictate to an adult arrive at writing with more to say and less fear of a blank page. Keep her wrong grammar in. Her sentence made the trip onto paper whole and comes back the same.

The Dough That Fights Back

⏱️ 60 min🧰 6 items

You need: The overnight dough (500 g bread flour, 7 g yeast, 1 tsp salt, 300 ml water, 1 tbsp oil), Or a 500 g pack of ready-made pizza dough, if last night got away from you, Flour for the table, a good handful, 2 baking trays, 1 tbsp oil for the trays, 1 dish towel

  1. Flour the table well. Cut the dough in half and give her one half.
  2. Show her both flat hands pushing out from the middle, then work yours.
  3. She will tear hers. Pinch it shut, carry on, and say out loud that dough mends and paper does not.
  4. Once it is about tray-sized, lift an edge and let it hang from her hands. It stretches under its own weight and springs back when she lays it down. Let her try yours as well.
  5. Lay both bases on the oiled trays and push them to the corners with her fingertips. Bumpy is right.
  6. Cover them with the dish towel until one o'clock.

💡Flour her hands and keep it off the dough. Flour worked in makes it tough, and hers has four hours of poking ahead of it.

One Pizza Each

Everything comes to the table in small bowls at one o'clock and nobody builds anybody else's.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 7 items

🧠 One thing worth knowing

Cabin fever is a real physiological thing, not a bad attitude.

Six weeks into winter, Azlyn is short of two things her body runs on: daylight and room to move. Morning light sets the clock that governs her sleep and her mood, and a February day indoors gives her a fraction of what an August one hands over. Add a body built to cover a few miles a day and a house that allows eight steps in a line, and the four o'clock meltdown reads as a shortage of both. Twenty minutes outside under flat gray cloud beats an hour under a lamp. Yours counts too, and you run the same shortage she does.

Coloring the Dough

You need: all-purpose flour, 2 cups · salt, 1 cup · warm water, 1 cup · oil, 2 tbsp · food coloring · 1 big bowl.

  1. Flour and salt in the bowl, her hands in to mix them dry. Then oil and warm water, stirred until it leaves the sides.

Shaking Cream into Butter

A jar, a tight lid, and everybody in the house taking a turn.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 7 items

You need: Heavy cream, 300 ml, out of the fridge an hour before, 1 clean jar with a tight-fitting lid, A pinch of salt, 1 sieve and a bowl, 1 butter knife, Bread or crackers, A cold plate for the butter

Where the Colors Run

Water carries paint further than a brush does. Wet the paper first and the colors travel on their own.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 7 items

You need: Thick paper, 6 sheets, The mixed colors from this morning, thinned with water, 1 wide soft brush, 1 clean sponge for wetting the paper, 1 baking tray or the draining board to work in, Paper towels, 1 jam jar of water

  1. Set the tray on the table and lay one sheet inside it. Everything from here runs off the edges.
  2. Thin the morning's colors with water until they drip off the brush in a stream instead of a blob.
  3. Wet the whole sheet with the sponge until it shines, then mop the standing water off with paper towels.
  4. Show her one drop. Loaded brush held above the paper, one touch, and the color opens out across the wet like ink.
  5. Now a second color beside the first, close enough that the two edges meet. Green arrives where blue meets yellow.
  6. Do a dry sheet for comparison. Same paint, hard edges, no travel. Let her say which she prefers.
  7. Leave them flat where they are. Wet paper tears the moment you lift it, so nothing moves until dinner.

💡She will keep going until every sheet is brown, because brown is where these end. Take the brush after five or six drops and call it finished.

Fixing the Lemonade

Three lemons, a pitcher and a bag of sugar. She is in charge of how it tastes and you are the one doing what she says.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 7 items

The String Telephone

Two cups and five meters of string between two rooms. She works out the rule before you get to say it.

⏱️ 90 min🧰 5 items

You need: 2 paper cups or clean yogurt cups, 3–5 meters of string, 1 pencil or skewer to pierce the cups (adult only), 2 paperclips or matchsticks to anchor the knots, Scissors (adult only)

  1. Pierce one small hole in the bottom of each cup yourself, dead center, with her hands well back.
  2. Thread the string in from the outside and knot it round a paperclip inside the cup so it cannot pull back through. She holds the cup while you tie.
  3. Test it across one room, both of you standing, string pulled tight. You talk, she listens, then swap over.
  4. Let the string go slack and try again. She gets nothing, and she will tell you before you ask. Run it round a door frame into the next room and it dies at the corner too. Leave her to find the straight line that brings it back.
  5. Whisper down it. That is the run that convinces her, because she can see your mouth moving and hears nothing without the cup at her ear.
  6. Take it down, wind the string up and put it away before you do anything else.

💡Give her the listening end first. A three-year-old handed the talking end shouts into the cup, and a shout crosses the room through the air anyway.

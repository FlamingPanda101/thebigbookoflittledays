## ⚠️ Quick Safety Reminders

- **Small parts** — dried beans, pasta, lolly sticks, salt dough bricks. A choking risk for *both* children. Count them out and count them back in.

- **Salt dough is not food.** It is dangerous if eaten, especially for Kreston and for any dog in the house.
- **Winter specific** — check for ice before outdoor play, and keep outings short below freezing. Little bodies lose heat fast.

## Week 1: Shape Engineers & Building 🏗️
**📅 January 1 – January 7, 2027**

---

### 🎯 What This Week Is About

Building — towers, bridges, houses, forts. Every day asks the same question in
a different form: **what makes a structure stand up, and what makes it fall
down?** She will build seven things, knock most of them over, and rebuild them
better. That cycle is the week.

It is also the opening week of the year, so Day 1 seals the time capsule you
open on Day 365, starts the growth chart on the wall, and hangs the first of
twelve handprints. Those three threads run to December. Everything else this
week is cardboard.

---

### 🧒 What Azlyn Will Learn

- **Structures need a base.** Wide bottoms hold, narrow ones topple.
- **Shape names in use** — circle, square, triangle — attached to real objects rather than flashcards.
- **Trial and error as a method.** Build, watch it fail, change one thing, build again.
- **Hand strength and control** through stacking, taping and pressing.
- **That she can make a real thing** that stays in the house and gets used.

---

### 👩 What You'll Learn

- Why writing down her exact words matters more than you'd think
- The cardboard box beats the expensive toy, and there's a reason
- Narrating beats quizzing, every time
- Knocking it down *is* the science
- Why kneading dough calms a wound-up kid down
- The pause before you help
- Why small enclosed spaces are so appealing right now

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Sorting the Build Box

**🧰 You need:** the recycling pile · 3 paper bags or empty boxes · masking tape.

1. Tip the pile into the middle of the floor and sit down in it with her.
2. Give her three bags and three words: **boxes**, **tubes**, **cups**. Let her sort. She will argue about the odd ones, which is the interesting part.
3. Ask which shape she thinks will hold the most weight. Do not answer. Tell her you will find out at 9:15 and leave the question hanging.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Tallest Tower That Stands

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The sorted boxes, tubes and cups
- 1 roll masking tape
- 1 measuring tape or a length of string
- 1 permanent marker

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Build a tower as tall as you can with no tape. Let her place every piece. Do not correct the wobbly ones.
2. When it falls, and it will, say *what could we change?* rather than *that was too narrow.* Wait for her answer even when it takes a while.
3. Build again, this time widest boxes at the bottom. Ask her which is stronger and let her push it to find out.
4. Measure the tallest one against her. Mark the height on the wall with a pencil and write **TOWER, JAN 1** beside it.
5. Now hand her the tape and let her build a third one however she likes. Tape changes the whole problem, and she should get to discover that herself.
6. Leave the best tower standing. She will come back to it at 4:15.

> 💡 **Tip:** Knocking it down is not the end of the activity, it is the measurement. A tower that never falls taught her nothing about why the others did.

### 🌳 Get Outside: First Walk of the Year

Bundle up and walk the route you will walk all year. Look at the trees today —
bare, sleeping, all structure and no leaves, which is the same thing she has
been building all morning. Hand her a paper bag and one instruction: find one
thing worth keeping. It goes in the capsule this afternoon, so tell her now.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 6 months and sits with his hands free.
> Give him his own cardboard tube and one paper cup on the floor near the
> building. He will bang, mouth and drop them, which is the same investigation
> Azlyn is running with the tower. On the walk, wear him facing out — he is
> alert enough now to look around properly, and cold air on his cheeks is a
> whole new sensation. Name three things you pass out loud.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Time Capsule

Morning was tall, loud and falling over. This one gets sealed and put away for
a year.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 shoebox with a lid
- Thick card, 2 sheets
- Washable paint and a wide brush
- 1 wet flannel in a shallow bowl
- 1 pencil, 1 permanent marker
- 1 measuring tape
- Parcel tape
- Today's walk treasure

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Paint her palm with the brush rather than dipping her hand. Press flat, count to three, lift straight up. Write **January** and **3** beside it.
2. Do Kreston's on the same sheet while he is calm, flannel already in your hand.
3. Stand them both against the door frame. Mark, date and write the names. Draw round Azlyn's flat hand next to her mark. Do everyone in the house so she can see the row.
4. Ask her four questions and write the answers **word for word**: favourite colour, favourite food, favourite song, what she wants to do this year. Do not tidy her grammar. The wrong words are the whole point.
5. Let her draw the family. Whatever comes out goes in.
6. Load the box: the drawing, her answers, the walk treasure, a photo. Photograph everything first — boxes get lost, phones get backed up.
7. Tape it shut, write **OPEN DECEMBER 31, 2027** across the lid, and put it somewhere she can see and not reach.

> 💡 **Tip:** Let her shake it once it's sealed. It does not open for a year, and telling her that is the whole conversation.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- Handprints only. Skip the box and do the rest tomorrow.
- Trace round her whole body on the back of a flattened box instead.
- Sort the walk treasure by colour, then by size.
- Look through last year's photos and name who is in each one.

### 🌳 Out Again: **Pick one**
- A friend's house, if anyone is up for a visitor on New Year's Day
- The park, emptier today than any other day this year
- The same walk again in fading light — ask what's different, the answer is usually the sound
- Drive somewhere with a view and stay in the warm car

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** He goes on the growth chart too, lying down, with
> his length marked and dated on the same wall as everyone else. After that,
> give him the flattened box to lie on. At six months the crackle and the give
> under his hands hold him longer than any toy in the house, and it puts him at
> floor level where Azlyn is working.

### 🌍 Around the World: Mongolia, the Arctic & the American Southwest 🏠

Not every house is a box. Mongolian families build round felt **gers** they
pack up and move with the seasons. Inuit builders cut snow blocks and lay them
in a spiral to close an **igloo**. In the American Southwest, **adobe** homes
are built from mud brick that keeps the inside cool through the heat.

> **✨ Try it:** Build a round house instead of a square one — a blanket over a
> laundry basket makes a good ger. Ask Azlyn which shape feels stronger.

> *Did you know?* Mongolian gers always face south, toward the sun.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Why writing down her exact words matters more than you'd think.**
> Three-year-olds are in the fastest language stretch of their lives, adding
> something like ten new words a day. Their mistakes are not random, they are
> evidence of rule-building. When Azlyn says *I goed outside*, she has not
> forgotten *went* — she has worked out that adding *-ed* makes things past
> tense and she is applying her rule everywhere. Linguists call it
> overregularization, and it signals sophisticated thinking rather than sloppy
> speech. You do not need to correct it. Say it back correctly in your next
> sentence — *you went outside? what did you see?* — and she absorbs the
> exception on her own timeline.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Keep tape rolls, lolly sticks and walk treasure out of
> Kreston's reach the moment you stop watching him. Build the tower away from
> where he is sitting: a falling cardboard tower is harmless to Azlyn and not
> to a six-month-old who cannot move out of the way. Washable non-toxic paint
> only.

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Choosing the Box

**🧰 You need:** the big box · masking tape · 1 permanent marker.

1. Let her climb in before anything else happens. She will do this anyway.
2. Ask what it should be — house, shop, boat, rocket. Write her answer on the side in marker. The decision is now fixed and it is hers.
3. Give her the tape and let her mark where the door and the windows go. You do the cutting at 9:15. She does the deciding now.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Box House

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 large box, tall enough for her to sit up in
- 1 craft knife or heavy scissors — **adult only**
- 1 roll masking tape
- 1 permanent marker
- Offcut card and small boxes

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Cut the door on three sides so it swings open. Do this yourself with her standing back, and say why she is standing back.
2. Cut two windows where she taped. Follow her marks even when they are in daft places.
3. Hand her the offcuts and the tape and let her build a roof. It will be crooked and it will hold.
4. Give her the marker. Every surface, inside and out, is hers to draw on.
5. Write a house number on the front and let her choose the number.
6. Get in with her. A house nobody sits in is a craft project, not a house.

> 💡 **Tip:** Cut less than you think. You can always cut more, and a box with three walls left standing survives the whole week. A box cut into flaps lasts an afternoon.

### 🌳 Get Outside: The Shape Hunt

Walk the street looking for the shapes she sorted yesterday. Windows are
rectangles, drain covers are circles, road signs are triangles, gates are made
of squares. Ask her to find you three circles before you turn back. When she
finds one you didn't see, say so out loud.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 6 months. Sit him inside the box house
> on a folded blanket while Azlyn draws on the walls around him. The change in
> light and sound inside a box is genuinely interesting to him, and being in the
> middle of her project rather than beside it is worth more than any toy. Keep
> the door flap propped open so you can see him the whole time.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: Furnishing the Box House

The morning built the structure. This afternoon somebody moves in.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The box house
- 6–10 small boxes, cups and tubes
- 1 roll masking tape
- 1 scrap of fabric or an old tea towel
- 1 permanent marker
- Crayons or paint

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Ask what a house needs. Take her list seriously even when it is a bed, a cake and a dog.
2. Make the bed first: a shoebox with the fabric folded into it. Let her decide where it goes.
3. Turn a tall box on its end for a table, cups upside down for stools.
4. Cut a rectangle of card and tape it inside as a picture. She draws whatever hangs in it.
5. Make one thing from her list that you would not have thought of. Follow her design, not a better one.
6. Move a doll or a teddy in. Give it a name and write the name on the door.
7. Turn the big light off and give her the torch. The house changes completely.

> 💡 **Tip:** Resist tidying the furniture into sensible positions. A stool on the roof is a decision, and if you move it she will stop making decisions.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- One box, one blanket over it, done.
- Draw the house you'd like to live in on a flattened box instead.
- Post things through the windows and fish them back out.
- Cut a big cardboard door for the *real* doorway and pretend the house is the room.

### 🌳 Out Again: **Pick one**
- **The museum** — first Saturday of the month, and the year's first
- The park, to run off whatever the box didn't
- Soft play if it's raining sideways
- A café with a window seat and something to look at
- The hardware shop to look at boxes, timber and things that stack

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** Give him one of the small furniture boxes to
> handle before it gets taped down. At 6 months he passes objects hand to hand
> and turns them over to look at the other side, which is new this month and
> worth watching for. Let him keep one box that never becomes furniture.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **The cardboard box beats the expensive toy, and there's a reason.**
> A toy fire engine can be a fire engine. A box can be a fire engine, a house,
> a boat, a bed, and a hiding place, sometimes inside ten minutes. Researchers
> who study play call the difference open-endedness, and children reliably play
> longer and talk more with materials that have not decided what they are. The
> box also fails, which the fire engine never does — it collapses, tears and
> needs mending, and every one of those is a problem she gets to solve. You are
> not being thrifty by handing her the packaging instead of what came in it.
> You are handing her the better object.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** The craft knife is adult-only and goes back out of reach
> between cuts, not on the arm of the sofa. Check inside the box for staples
> before either child gets in. Never leave Kreston in the box house alone, and
> keep the door flap open the whole time he is in it.

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Taping the Tubes

**🧰 You need:** cardboard tubes · masking tape · scissors · a smooth wall or the side of a wardrobe.

1. Cut two or three tubes in half lengthways so they become open channels. You cut, she holds.
2. Tape one channel to the wall at a slope. Roll a dried bean down it and watch where it lands.
3. Ask her where the next one should go so the bean keeps travelling. Tape it exactly where she says, even if it will not work.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Ball Run

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 6–10 cardboard tubes, some cut open
- 1 roll masking tape
- Dried beans, dried pasta, a ping-pong ball if you have one
- 1 shallow box or saucepan for the bottom
- A wall or wardrobe side you don't mind taping

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Put the catching box on the floor at the bottom. Everything is aimed at that.
2. Work upward. She chooses each next position, you tape it. Say what you are doing rather than asking her questions about it: *I'm taping this one steeper, let's see.*
3. Test after every single piece. One bean, every time.
4. When it fails, look at where the bean actually stopped and change that one joint. Change one thing, not three.
5. Once beans run the whole way, try pasta, then the ball. Different weights behave differently and she will notice before you point it out.
6. Leave it taped up. Tomorrow it comes down as part of the demolition.

> 💡 **Tip:** Narrate, don't quiz. *This end is higher than that end* teaches more here than *is this end higher or lower?*, and it keeps her building instead of performing.

### 🌳 Get Outside: Stick Bridges

Find a puddle, a gutter or a gap between two stones and build a bridge across
it with sticks. Test each one with a pebble. Most will fall in. Ask what a
longer stick does, then what two sticks side by side do. Wet gloves are the
cost of this activity and it is worth it.

> 👶 **Infant Integration:** Kreston is 6 months and tracks moving objects
> across his whole field of view now. Sit him facing the ball run and drop a
> bean down it while he watches. Do it four or five times. Watching something
> disappear at the top and reappear at the bottom is early object permanence
> work, and it costs you nothing to point him in the right direction.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Bridge That Holds Teddy

The morning made things travel downward. This afternoon something has to stay
up, which is a harder problem.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 2 dining chairs, set a hand's width apart to start
- Flattened cardboard, several pieces
- Lolly sticks and cardboard tubes
- 1 roll masking tape
- 1 teddy or doll
- A tin of beans as the heavy test weight

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Set the chairs close together. Lay one flat piece of card between them and stand teddy on it. It holds.
2. Move the chairs a hand's width apart and try again. Keep going until it sags.
3. Ask *what could we change?* and wait. She will usually say a bigger piece of card. Try it. It still sags.
4. Show her one trick and then step back: fold the card into a concertina, or roll it into tubes and lay them side by side. Let her work out which way up it goes.
5. Test with teddy. Then the tin of beans. Then both.
6. Push the chairs as far apart as the bridge will survive and mark the floor with tape.
7. Take a photo of the winning bridge with teddy standing on it.

> 💡 **Tip:** When she gets stuck, count to five in your head before you help. Most of the time she starts again on her own inside those five seconds, and the fix she finds is worth more than yours.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- Bridge two sofa cushions with one piece of card and stop there.
- Build a bridge for toy cars instead of teddy.
- Fold paper into concertinas and stand books on them.
- Lay a plank between two low stools outside and walk across it.

### 🌳 Out Again: **Pick one**
- A long walk with the pram, the whole loop, no destination
- Feed the ducks and count the bridges you cross
- The park, for the climbing frame she can look at as a structure now
- A drive to somewhere with a real bridge, and stand under it
- The playground with the big slide, which is a ramp she can ride

> 👶 **Kreston's Afternoon:** Lay him under the finished bridge and let him look
> up through it. At 6 months he is rocking on hands and knees without moving
> forward yet, so put the teddy just past his reach on the far side of the
> bridge. Rocking with a target is how crawling starts.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Narrating beats quizzing, every time.**
> The instinct with a three-year-old is to turn everything into a question —
> *what colour is that? how many? is it big or small?* — because answers feel
> like proof of learning. What they mostly produce is a child performing for
> an adult. Describing what you are doing gives her far more: new vocabulary in
> a real context, sentence structures she has not built yet, and no pressure to
> be right. Children whose parents narrate hear more words and more varied ones.
> Save your questions for the ones you cannot answer yourself. *What could we
> change?* is worth twenty rounds of *what shape is this?*

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Dried beans and pasta are a choking hazard and they will end up
> on the floor. Sweep before Kreston is put down, and keep the catching box off
> the floor when he is loose in the room. Scissors stay with you.

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Cup Pyramids

**🧰 You need:** paper cups, 20 or so · a flat bit of floor.

1. Build a pyramid together: six cups along the bottom, then five, then four.
2. Take one cup out of the bottom row and see what happens. Then put it back and take one from the top instead.
3. Ask which cup is doing the most work. Whatever she says, agree that you will test it properly at 9:15.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Knock-Down Experiment

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- Boxes, cups and tubes from the build box
- 1 roll masking tape
- Paper and a marker for recording
- The ball run from yesterday, still taped to the wall

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Build three towers side by side, all the same height. Tower one has a wide base. Tower two is a straight stack. Tower three is widest at the top on purpose.
2. Give each one a number and write the numbers on paper.
3. Push the floor near tower one with a flat hand. Count how many pushes before it goes. Write the number down. Repeat for two and three.
4. Ask her which fell first and which held longest, and write her explanation word for word next to the numbers.
5. Rebuild the winner and let her push it over herself, as hard as she likes.
6. Now take down the ball run together and add its tubes to the pile. Nothing in this room is permanent and that is the point.

> 💡 **Tip:** Do not save the towers. The falling is the data, and a child who is told to be careful with her tower stops testing it.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Wrecking Ball

The morning knocked things over with hands. This afternoon the force arrives
from somewhere else, which is a different idea entirely.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 clean sock
- Dried beans or rice to fill it
- 1 ball of string, about two metres
- A door frame, a broom handle across two chairs, or a curtain rail bracket
- All the boxes and cups
- Masking tape

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Fill the sock a third full, knot it, and tie the string tight around the knot.
2. Hang the free end from the door frame so the sock swings a hand's width above the floor.
3. Build a tower directly under the swing. Pull the sock back and let go. Do not throw it.
4. Rebuild in the same spot and try releasing from lower down. Ask her which knocked more over.
5. Move the tower further away and let her work out that the swing only reaches so far.
6. Let her build a tower she thinks can survive it. Test it. Let her be right or wrong without commentary.
7. Finish by building one tower together that you both agree is too good to knock down, then knock it down anyway.

> 💡 **Tip:** She will want to swing the sock by hand. Let her, once, then go back to releasing it — the whole idea is that the height it starts from decides what happens, and that only shows up if you let go.

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Knocking it down *is* the science.**
> Watching a three-year-old destroy something she spent forty minutes making
> can feel like the work being wasted. It is the second half of the work. She
> is testing a prediction, and demolition is the only way to get the answer.
> Children learn the physical rules of the world by breaking things and
> watching how they break, which is why the same tower goes up and down eleven
> times. The instinct to say *don't, you worked so hard on that* protects the
> object and interrupts the learning. Take the photo, then let her flatten it.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Tie the string to something that cannot come down on top of
> her — a door frame, not a curtain pole or a shelf. Take the sock down and put
> the string away the moment the activity ends: a loop of hanging string is a
> strangulation risk, and it must not be left up overnight or within reach of
> either child.

### 🌅 Opening Activity: Rolling the Bricks

**🧰 You need:** the salt dough · a rolling pin · a table knife · a floured board.

1. Give her a fist-sized lump and let her squash it for a few minutes before anything useful happens. This is the point of the hour.
2. Roll it out to about a finger's thickness. Let her do the rolling badly.
3. Cut rough rectangles with the table knife. They do not need to match. Aim for thirty and stop when she stops.

### 🎨 The Main Event: Salt Dough Bricks

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The salt dough, 2 cups flour to 1 cup salt to 1 cup water
- 1 rolling pin, 1 table knife
- 1 baking tray and baking paper
- Extra flour for the board
- 1 pencil for scoring

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lay the cut bricks on the lined tray without touching each other.
2. Let her press a mark into each one with her thumb, or scratch a line with the pencil. Every brick becomes hers.
3. Set them in the oven at its lowest setting. They need two to three hours, so they go in now and come out around lunch.
4. While they bake, knead the leftover dough together. Give her a big lump and take one yourself and sit with it. Ten minutes of quiet kneading does more for a wound-up morning than any instruction.
5. Make three or four larger pieces — a doorstep, an arch, a chimney — and add them to a second tray.
6. When they come out, spread them on a cold tray and leave them until 1:00. Warm bricks crumble.

> 💡 **Tip:** They will not all survive. Bake ten more than you need and let the broken ones become rubble, which turns out to be the part she plays with most.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: Building the Brick Wall

The morning made the material. This afternoon she finds out what it is for,
which is the whole lesson of the week in one move.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- The cooled salt dough bricks, thirty or so
- A flat tray or baking sheet as the building site
- 1 small bowl of flour and water paste
- 1 teaspoon for the paste
- A ruler or a straight piece of card
- The teddy from Sunday

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lay a row of bricks in a straight line along the tray. Push them tight together.
2. Build a second row directly on top, matching every joint. Push it and watch the whole wall hinge apart.
3. Rebuild the second row offset, each brick bridging the gap below it. Push again. This one holds, and she will feel the difference in her hand.
4. Keep going up. Use the paste between rows like mortar if the wall gets tall enough to need it.
5. Leave a gap for a door and lay one long brick across the top of it. That is an arch, and it is holding up everything above it.
6. Build a second wall at a right angle to the first so they lean on each other.
7. Stand teddy inside. Photograph the wall next to the tower from Day 1.

> 💡 **Tip:** Step three is the whole activity. Do not explain the offset before she has seen the matched-joint wall fall apart, because the falling is what makes the fix make sense.

### 🎯 Afternoon Alternatives
- Bricks only, no paste, and knock it down when it bores her.

---

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **Why kneading dough calms a wound-up kid down.**
> Pushing, squeezing and pulling something that resists is what occupational
> therapists call heavy work, and it is one of the most reliable regulation
> tools available. Deep pressure through the hands and joints feeds the
> proprioceptive system, which tells the brain where the body is, and a body
> that knows where it is settles. Dough is the cheapest version: it pushes back,
> it never fails, and there is no way to do it wrong. On a day that has gone
> loud, ten minutes of dough will land better than any conversation about
> calming down.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** The oven is on for three hours today — say it out loud when
> she comes near, and keep Kreston out of the kitchen while the trays move.
> Salt dough is dangerous if eaten, and seriously so for a 7-month-old and for
> any dog in the house. Count the bricks in and out and store them high.

1. Tape four sticks into a square. Let her do the taping, badly, with your thumb holding the corners.
2. Tape three sticks into a triangle beside it.
3. Push gently on the top corner of each one. The square folds flat. The triangle does not. Say nothing else about it and leave both on the table until 9:15.

### 🎨 The Main Event: The Triangle Test

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 30–40 lolly sticks
- 1 roll masking tape
- The square and triangle from this morning
- Cardboard tubes, 4 or 5
- A small toy as the test load

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Hand her the folded square and ask her to make it stop folding. Then wait. Count to five in your head before you say anything at all.
2. If she gets stuck, put one loose stick on the table beside it and say nothing more. Most children put it across the diagonal within a minute.
3. Once the square has a diagonal, push it again together. It holds now, and it holds because there are two triangles in it.
4. Build a tall tower out of taped triangles. Build a second out of taped squares. Race them upward.
5. Load both with the toy and push both over. Write down which survived.
6. Go and look at the box house from Saturday and find where a triangle would fix its saggy roof. Add one.

> 💡 **Tip:** This is the day to sit on your hands. Every time you fix a joint for her you take away the exact thing the activity exists to teach.

### 🎨 Second Main Event: The Zip Line

Everything this week has pushed down. A zip line pulls, which is the opposite
force and a genuinely new idea.

**🧰 Complete Materials List:**
- 1 ball of string, 3 metres
- 2 dining chairs, or a door handle and a chair
- 1 paper cup
- 1 cardboard tube cut into a short ring
- Masking tape and scissors
- Small toys, a spoon, a dried bean — cargo of different weights

**📝 Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Tie the string tight between the chair backs, one end higher than the other. She decides which end is higher.
2. Tape the paper cup to the cardboard ring so it hangs below it, then thread the string through the ring before you tie the second end.
3. Load the cup with a small toy at the high end and let go.
4. When it stops halfway, ask what would make it keep going. Raise the high end and try again. Let her be the one who lifts it.
5. Race two different weights down the same line. Ask her to guess first, every time, before she lets go.
6. Slacken the string right off and try again so she sees a sagging line stop everything dead.
7. Send one thing down for each person in the house and let her decide who gets what.

> 💡 **Tip:** Ask her to predict before every single run. Getting it wrong out loud and then watching is worth more than getting it right in her head.

> 🧠 **A Little Parenting Insight**
> **The pause before you help.**
> A three-year-old struggling with a joint that will not hold looks like a
> child who needs you. Most of the time she needs about four more seconds.
> Adults step in fast, usually before frustration has actually arrived, and
> every early rescue teaches the same background lesson: hard things get solved
> by someone else. Count to five before you move. If she is still stuck, give
> the smallest possible nudge — put the loose stick on the table rather than
> taping it on. What you are protecting is not her mood today, it is her
> willingness to stay with a hard thing next year.

> ⚠️ **Safety:** Take the zip line down and put the string away the second you
